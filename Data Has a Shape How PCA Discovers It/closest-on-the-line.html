Data Has a Shape: How PCA Discovers It

Closest on the Line

A point that won't sit on the line. A line that won't bend to meet it. Somewhere between them lives the answer to the first real question PCA has to settle.

Where we left off

Last time, four points sat exactly on the line \(y = 2x\). Each one needed only a single number to describe it. The compression was perfect, and we got it for free because the data lined up nicely.

Here's where the story gets harder.

Four obedient points. One troublemaker.

Same line as Part 1. Four orange points sit on it. The fifth point, in blue, doesn't. Notice it's hovering above the line, not to the side.

The four orange points are exactly where they were. Each one still has a clean position along the line. The blue point at \((1, 3)\) is the problem. The line \(y = 2x\) doesn't pass through it. There's no scalar \(c\) that makes \(c \cdot w\) land on \((1, 3)\), no matter how we stretch or shrink \(w\).

So the trick from Part 1 has stopped working. What now?

The shape of a compromise

Here's the move. We can't put the blue point on the line, but we can pick some position on the line and call it our "best guess" for where the point would go if it had to live on the line. That guess will be wrong by some amount. We write that wrong-ness down explicitly:

\[x = c \cdot w + \text{error}.\]

The first piece, \(c \cdot w\), is something on the line — that's the part we'd actually store. The second piece is the gap between the line and the real point. Add the two back together and you recover \(x\) exactly. We haven't lost anything yet. We've just made the bookkeeping honest.

A point off the line is "something on the line, plus a leftover." The job now is to make the leftover small.

But what does "small" actually mean here? It's not as obvious a question as it sounds.

What does "small" even mean?

stop and ponder

Look at the picture above. If someone asked you to summarise the blue point using a single spot on the line — where would your finger go?

Don't go for the official answer yet. The gut answer. What feels like the right spot to land on?

Take a second with that. Different people pick different spots, and they're all defensible.

Some people drop straight down. They look at the blue point at \((1, 3)\), and pick the spot on the line that has the same \(x\)-coordinate. That'd be \((1, 2)\), since the line says \(y = 2x\). The "error" is then how far above the line you started: one unit, straight down.

Other people slide horizontally. They keep the \(y\)-coordinate fixed at 3, and pick the \(x\) on the line where \(y = 3\). That'd be \((1.5, 3)\). The error is half a unit, sideways.

And some people just go for the closest spot on the line, period. As the crow flies. Not vertical, not horizontal, but the perpendicular drop.

Three different choices. Three different errors. Three different geometries. Look at them next to each other.

Three honest ways to measure "off the line."
option A
Vertical gap
\(|y - 2x|\)
option B
Horizontal gap
\(|x - y/2|\)
option C
Straight-line distance
\(\|x - cw\|\)

Same point, same line, three different yardsticks. Each green segment is what that option would call "the error." None of them is wrong. They just disagree about what we're trying to minimise.

Once we pick a definition of error, that definition decides everything else. Different errors lead to different optimal positions, different formulas, eventually different algorithms. We're at a fork. We have to commit to one.

The choice we're going to make is option C, the perpendicular distance. And we'll square it. So our error is the squared length of the leftover vector \(x - cw\), and what we want small is

\[\|x - c w\|^2.\]

Three things to say about this choice, none of them coincidences.

It doesn't play favourites with directions. The vertical gap and horizontal gap both privilege the coordinate axes — fine in 2D, awkward when you get to fifty dimensions and there's no natural "up." The perpendicular distance treats every direction the same.

It plays nicely with algebra. Squaring kills the absolute value. The squared length \((x_1 - cw_1)^2 + (x_2 - cw_2)^2\) is just a sum of squares, and that's the kind of thing calculus is built for.

And the deeper reason, which won't pay off until later in the series: squaring punishes big errors much more than small ones. A point twice as far away contributes four times as much. This will turn out to align with what we mean by "spread" in a dataset, and it's why the algorithm we eventually build sees structure the way we want it to. Take it on credit for now.

From here on, "error" means \(\|x - cw\|^2\). We're committing.

The actual problem we're solving

Here's where we stand. A point \(x\) is given. A direction \(w\) is fixed. The only knob we get to turn is \(c\), the position along the line. We want to pick \(c\) so the squared error is as small as it can get.

In symbols:

\[\min_{c} \;\|x - c w\|^2.\]

One scalar to optimise, one quantity to minimise. As clean as optimisation problems get. The only reason it's not yet a one-line formula is that we haven't done the work.

And we're going to do that work twice. Once geometrically, by playing with a slider until the answer becomes obvious. Once algebraically, by writing the squared error as a function of \(c\) and finding its minimum the way calculus tells us to. Both paths land on the same place, which is satisfying. Geometric first.

stop and ponder

You probably noticed it already. Slide it back and forth. There's one spot where the picture just... settles. Where the red arrow looks at peace with the line.

What's that spot doing differently from all the others?

Look at the angle readout while you slide. When the error is shortest, the angle is exactly ninety degrees. The red arrow is perpendicular to the orange line.

This isn't a coincidence of this point or this line. Try the snap button from a few different starting positions. Same result every time. The error hits its minimum precisely when the error is at right angles to the line.

Why the perpendicular wins

The slider showed us the pattern. Now let's understand why it has to be true. The argument is short, and it uses Pythagoras in a way that I've always found pretty.

Suppose you've landed exactly at the perpendicular foot — the spot where the error makes a right angle with the line. Someone challenges you: "Bet I can do better. Slide a tiny bit and your error will get shorter."

Are they right? Slide by a small amount \(\delta\) along the line. The new error connects this new green dot back to the blue point. How long is it?

Sliding away from the perpendicular always makes things worse.

The blue point is fixed. The orange line is fixed. The green dot starts at the perpendicular foot — the unique spot where the dotted green error meets the line at a right angle.

Slide the dot. The new error in red is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the original perpendicular and the slide. Pythagoras: the hypotenuse is always longer than either leg.

0.00

Slide left. Slide right. Either way the new red error grows. The perpendicular position is not just a local minimum, it's the only minimum. There is no shorter alternative.

That's the argument. If you're standing at the perpendicular foot, the original error is one leg of a right triangle, the slide is the other leg, and the new error is the hypotenuse. Hypotenuses are always longer than legs. So you can't do better.

And what if you weren't standing at the perpendicular foot to begin with? Then your error has some component pointing along the line. Slide in the right direction by the right amount and that component cancels. The error gets shorter. So any non-perpendicular position can be improved.

Putting both halves together: the perpendicular position is the unique minimum. Anywhere else is improvable. Right at the foot, you're stuck. There's nothing left to gain.

The error is shortest exactly when the error is perpendicular to the line. Always.

Same answer from algebra

The slider gave us the answer. Pythagoras explained why. The calculus gives us a clean formula and, more importantly, generalises to any number of dimensions instead of just two.

Start with what we want to minimise:

\[f(c) = \|x - c w\|^2 = (x - c w)^\top (x - c w).\]

That \((x-cw)^\top(x-cw)\) is just the squared length, written as a dot product with itself. Expand it using the identity

\[(a - b)^\top (a - b) = a^\top a - 2 a^\top b + b^\top b,\]

with \(a = x\) and \(b = c w\):

\[f(c) = x^\top x - 2 c \, (x^\top w) + c^2 \, (w^\top w).\]

Stare at this for a second. As a function of \(c\), it's a parabola. The first term is constant, the second is linear in \(c\), the third is quadratic in \(c\) with positive coefficient \(w^\top w = \|w\|^2\). A parabola opening upwards has exactly one minimum, sitting at the bottom.

Squared error, as a function of c.

Plot \(f(c)\) for our blue point. Horizontal axis is \(c\). Vertical axis is the squared error. The whole thing is a parabola, opening upwards because \(\|w\|^2\) is positive.

Hover over the curve to see the squared error at any \(c\). The minimum sits at the bottom of the bowl. That \(c\) — the one where the bowl is deepest — is exactly the perpendicular foot we found with the slider.

The geometry and the algebra are looking at the same picture from two angles.

Bottom of the bowl = shortest error = perpendicular foot. Three names for the same point.

To find the bottom of the bowl, take the derivative and set it to zero.

step one

Differentiate \(f(c)\) with respect to \(c\). The constant term contributes nothing, the linear term contributes \(-2(x^\top w)\), and the quadratic term contributes \(2c \, (w^\top w)\).

\[f'(c) = -2 (x^\top w) + 2 c \, (w^\top w).\]

step two

Set the derivative to zero. At the bottom of a parabola, the slope is flat.

\[-2 (x^\top w) + 2 c \, (w^\top w) = 0.\]

step three

Solve for \(c\). The factor of two cancels. Move the \(c\) term to the other side and divide.

\[c = \frac{x^\top w}{w^\top w} = \frac{x^\top w}{\|w\|^2}.\]

That's the formula. Given any point \(x\) and any direction \(w\), the position along \(w\) that minimises squared error is \(x^\top w\) divided by \(\|w\|^2\).

One small simplification at no cost. Throughout the rest of this series we'll always work with unit directions, meaning \(\|w\| = 1\). It costs nothing — any direction can be scaled to unit length without changing the line it points along. With \(\|w\|^2 = 1\), the formula collapses to:

\(c = x^\top w\)

The optimal position of \(x\) along the unit direction \(w\) is just their dot product. One inner product. No iteration, no search, no slider.

Two paths, one answer

Notice something. The calculus we just did never said the word "perpendicular." But we already know from the slider and from Pythagoras that the optimum has to be perpendicular. So both paths should be telling the same story. Let's check.

The perpendicular condition says the error is at right angles to \(w\). Two vectors are at right angles when their dot product is zero. The error vector is \(x - cw\), so:

\[(x - c w)^\top w = 0.\]

Distribute: \(x^\top w - c \, (w^\top w) = 0\). Solve for \(c\):

\[c = \frac{x^\top w}{w^\top w}.\]

Same formula. Geometry and algebra agree, exactly. Two routes, identical destination — the kind of small beautiful agreement that makes the math feel like it knows what it's doing.

Orthogonal projection

The operation we just defined has a name. Taking a point \(x\), finding the position along \(w\) that minimises squared error, and using \(c \cdot w\) as the reconstruction, is called the orthogonal projection of \(x\) onto \(w\). The number \(c = x^\top w\) is the scalar projection. The vector \(c \cdot w\) is the vector projection.

"Orthogonal" just means perpendicular, and we earned it twice — once with Pythagoras, once with calculus. "Projection" is the older, more visual word: imagine light shining straight onto the line, casting the point's shadow exactly at \(c \cdot w\).

Three things worth carrying forward.

This is provably the smallest the error can be. We didn't pick a reasonable summary. We picked the best one.

It generalises immediately. Nothing in the derivation cared about being in 2D. Plug in vectors with fifty entries, or fifty thousand, and \(c = x^\top w\) still gives the optimal scalar projection. Perpendicular still means dot product is zero, no matter how many coordinates you have.

This is the seed of everything else. From here on, every time we want to compress a point onto a direction, this is the operation. PCA is, in a real sense, a long story about which directions are worth projecting onto. The projection itself is settled.

What we did, in one breath

We had a point that didn't sit on a line. We needed to summarise it using only the line, which meant accepting some error. After looking at three honest ways to measure that error, we picked perpendicular distance and squared it.

Then we attacked the resulting problem twice. The slider showed us the answer is the perpendicular foot. Pythagoras showed us why. And the calculus, expanding and differentiating, gave us the formula \(c = x^\top w\) for unit \(w\).

The operation has a name — orthogonal projection — and it'll be the building block for everything that follows.

Where this is going

So far, one point at a time. Real datasets have many points, and each one will have its own best \(c\). Next post leaves the single-point world behind and looks at a whole dataset at once. The question shifts from "what's the best position for this point" to "what's the best line to project everyone onto." That's where the algebra starts looking genuinely different.
