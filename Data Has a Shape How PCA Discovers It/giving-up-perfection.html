Seeing in Fewer Dimensions

The Storage Question

How many real numbers do you actually need to represent your data? The answer is almost always less than you think, and that gap is where dimensionality reduction lives.

A question no one asks until they have to

When you first look at a dataset, the question that pops up is "what does it mean?" Then maybe "how do I plot it?" Eventually "what model do I fit?" The question almost nobody asks first is the most practical one you could imagine: how many real numbers do I actually need to store this?

It sounds like a question for someone worrying about their cloud bill. It isn't. Once you take it seriously, it forces the entire machinery of dimensionality reduction into existence. PCA, autoencoders, and every embedding technique you've ever used are, deep down, elaborate answers to this one question.

Let's start small. Suppose I hand you four data points in 2D:

\[ \mathcal{D} = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} -7 \\ -14 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\} \]

How many real numbers do you need to store this? The obvious answer: four points, two coordinates each, so eight numbers. Done. Move on.

Except every single one of those points lies on the line \(y = 2x\).

Notice what that means. If I tell you once that "the direction is \([1, 2]\)", I don't need to tell you both coordinates of each point anymore. I just need to tell you how far along that direction each point sits. Point one: scale the direction by \(-7\). Point two: scale by \(2\). Point three: scale by \(0.5\). Point four: scale by \(0\). That's four scalars, plus two numbers for the direction itself. Six numbers total.

We just shaved 25% off the storage cost, and nothing was lost. The points are exactly where they were. We didn't approximate anything.

Two ways to describe the same data

What just happened is small and, on this toy example, not very impressive. But it reveals a distinction that's going to run through the entire series. There are two fundamentally different ways to write down a dataset.

The naive way is what a spreadsheet does, what a CSV does, what you do when you type numbers into NumPy. Every coordinate of every point sits in memory. If you have \(n\) points in \(d\) dimensions, that's \(n \cdot d\) numbers. No structure assumed, no structure exploited.

The structured way separates two things: what shape the data takes, and where each point sits within that shape. In our example, the shape is "the line through the origin with direction \([1, 2]\)" and the position of each point is a single scalar. That splits the \(nd\) numbers into two much smaller buckets. The shape costs \(d\) numbers (one per dimension of the ambient space). Each point's position costs \(1\) number. Total: \(d + n\).

Naive storage: \(nd\). Structured storage: \(d + n\).

When \(n\) and \(d\) are both small this barely matters. When they're large it stops being a rounding error and starts being the difference between "this fits on my laptop" and "this doesn't fit anywhere at all."

Start with "On the line", where the points sit perfectly on y = 2x and the structured form recovers them exactly. Now drag the jitter slider up, or grab a point and pull it off the line. The geometry of the dataset stops being one-dimensional, and the single-direction trick stops working. By the time you reach a real cloud, you'd need two directions to reconstruct the points exactly, which means the compression has evaporated.

Why the savings stop being optional

On four 2D points, the difference between 8 numbers and 6 numbers is a curiosity. At any realistic scale, the story changes fast. Imagine a dataset of one million face images, each 192 by 168 pixels in greyscale. Every image is a point in a space with \(d = 192 \times 168 = 32{,}256\) dimensions. Store that naively and you have \(nd = 10^6 \times 32{,}256 \approx 3.2 \times 10^{10}\) numbers. Thirty-two billion floats. At four bytes each, that's about 128 gigabytes, which is already uncomfortable.

Now suppose (and this turns out to be roughly true in practice) that faces actually live on a much lower-dimensional surface inside that huge pixel space. Say \(k = 100\) directions are enough to capture the vast majority of the variation. Then the structured cost is \(kd + kn = 100 \times 32{,}256 + 100 \times 10^6 \approx 1.03 \times 10^8\) numbers. Around 400 megabytes. A 300× saving, and it's the difference between a dataset that fits in RAM and one that doesn't.

Crank d up and the naive cost explodes linearly. Crank k down and the structured cost barely moves. That asymmetry is the engine behind every dimensionality reduction method. The whole game is finding a small k that still captures what matters.

The catch, spelled out

Everything in this post has been based on a quiet assumption, and it's time to say it out loud. Our storage trick only works because the data actually lies on a line. The moment a single point drifts off that line, the structured form can no longer reproduce the data exactly. You still get a description, the closest point on the line, say, but it's an approximation. The original is gone.

Real data never lies perfectly on a line. Or a plane. Or any other neat surface you might draw through it. It's always a bit scattered, a bit noisy, a bit messy. So the strict version of our trick, "exact reconstruction with fewer numbers", doesn't survive contact with reality.

The question changes. We can't ask for exact reconstruction anymore. We have to ask: what's the best approximation we can get for our budget?

That reframe is the whole series. Once we accept that representation will be lossy, a dozen sharper questions appear. Which direction to project onto? How to measure "best"? What if one direction isn't enough? What if the underlying structure isn't a line at all? Every post from here on picks up one of those questions and answers it.

Where this is going

We started by noticing that structured data can be stored with far fewer numbers than its raw form suggests, and that the savings grow dramatically with scale. We ended by conceding that real data doesn't sit on perfect lines, so we'll have to settle for approximations. That concession isn't a failure. It's where the interesting mathematics starts.

Next post, we'll take a single point and a single candidate line and ask the most basic version of the approximation question. If this point isn't on that line, what's the closest point on the line to it? The answer is a short piece of calculus, but it sets up everything that follows: projection, error, and the first real derivation of the series.
