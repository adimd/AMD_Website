Biosignal Acquisition for Embedded Engineers · Class 1

A Microvolt of Meaning in a Millivolt of Mess

This is the opening post of a build log: a biosignal amplifier from scratch, aimed at capturing EEG and EMG with an analog front-end and a microcontroller. Before any of that, one number sets the whole agenda, and one sentence about what a voltage even is tells you why the ADC was never the hard part.

The number this whole series is about

Here is a sentence that sounds reasonable and will quietly ruin your first board: "EEG is just a voltage, you have a 24-bit ADC, so digitize it." Every word is true. The conclusion is a trap, and I want to walk into it with you slowly, because the calculation that springs the trap is, in miniature, the whole reason this series exists.

So let us actually look at what arrives at the converter. The thing you want from the scalp is on the order of a single microvolt of structured wiggle. The thing it is riding on, before you do anything clever, includes a steady offset from the electrode itself of around 300 millivolts, which has nothing to do with the brain at all. Line those two up and the unwanted part is bigger than the wanted part by

0.3 V / 0.000001 V = 300,000

Three hundred thousand to one. That number is hard to feel, so let me put it in decibels, where these huge ratios become friendlier. And right here is where I always have to catch myself, because the reflex is to reach for 10 log₁₀ and that would be wrong. The decibel is defined on power, but a voltage is not a power; in a resistor the power goes as the square of the voltage. That square does not vanish, it climbs out of the log as a factor of two, and 10 log₁₀(V²) becomes 20 log₁₀(V). So for any amplitude, volts rather than watts, the magic number is 20, not 10. The nice payoff is that each factor of ten in voltage is now worth exactly 20 dB, so our 300,000, a touch over five tens, sits a touch over 100 dB. Call it 110.

Now the question that actually matters to someone holding a converter: what does 110 dB cost me in bits? Here I find it easiest to ask what a single bit is even worth. Add one bit and you double the number of levels the converter can place, which doubles the span it can cover at the same fineness. Doubling a voltage, by the rule we just found, is 20 log₁₀(2), almost exactly 6 dB. So one bit buys six decibels, and the conversion falls right out: 110 / 6 ≈ 18 bits, just to reach across the gap. And that is before I have asked for a scrap of headroom, any margin against noise, or any room for the signal to move once it is in there. Suddenly a 24-bit converter stops looking like overkill and starts looking like a budget, most of it spent not on seeing the signal but on spanning the distance between the signal and the mess it is buried in.

The central question of this course: how do you pull a microvolt of meaning out of a millivolt (often far more) of mess, so that a faint biological voltage becomes a digital sample you can trust?

And the moment you see the gap that way, the whole job reframes itself. The problem was never really about making the signal bigger; a microvolt amplified a thousand times is still a microvolt of signal sitting on 300 millivolts of garbage amplified a thousand times. The problem is getting rid of the garbage. So almost everything ahead of us, the electrode, the noise budget, the sampling, the front-end specification we land on at the end, is in service of one stubborn little quantity making it across to a number intact. Which means I owe you a much closer look at what that quantity even is, because I have been throwing around two words, "signal" and "voltage", as if they were obvious, and neither one is.

What a signal actually is

Strip the jargon and a signal is a physical quantity that varies, and carries information in its variation. Mathematically it is just a function of time, an amplitude that changes as the clock moves: we write it v(t). For us the amplitude is almost always voltage.

The part worth sitting with is that the meaning lives in the variation, not in any single instant. A flat line at 40 microvolts tells you almost nothing. A line that rises and falls in a particular rhythm tells you the brain is idling with the eyes shut, or that a muscle just fired. This is exactly why, later in the series, we will obsess over which frequencies are present and how fast things change: that is where the information is. Bandwidth and sample rate are not bookkeeping. They are about preserving the variation that is the message.

One more thing to plant a flag on. Out in the body, before any converter touches it, v(t) is continuous in both time and amplitude. It has a value at every instant, and that value can be any real number in range. It does not become a list of discrete numbers until we sample and quantise it. The gap between continuous reality and discrete samples is where a lot of trust is won or lost, and it is a whole unit on its own. For now: the signal starts life analog.

What makes it a biopotential

This is the jump your circuits background will quietly fight. In a wire, current is electrons drifting through metal. In the body there are essentially no free electrons doing that. The body is a warm bag of salt water, and the charge carriers are ions: sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium, dissolved and moving. When tissue does electrical work, it is moving ions across membranes, not pushing electrons down a wire.

A biopotential is the voltage that appears because living cells actively separate ionic charge. One cell is far too small to notice from outside. But when a population of them lines up and acts together, the charge separation sets up an electric field that reaches all the way to the skin, and what you measure at the surface is the faint electrical shadow of all that ionic activity inside. That shadow is your biopotential.

Hold on to the ionic picture rather than the electron picture, because it makes the next unit feel inevitable instead of mysterious: somewhere between the salty tissue and your SPI bus, ionic charge has to become electronic charge. That handoff happens at the electrode, and it is messy enough to deserve its own unit.

The one rule that breaks naive designs: voltage is a difference

This is the most important idea in the post, so let me build it rather than assert it. There is no such thing as "the voltage at a point" in any absolute sense. Potential is defined only as a difference between two locations; it answers "how much work to move charge from here to there," and "here to there" is two places, not one. This is not a measurement inconvenience that better gear would fix. It is the definition.

The consequence is immediate and concrete: you can never measure a biopotential with one electrode. You always need at least two, and what your instrument reports is the difference between them. The person who draws a single wire from the scalp to an ADC input and expects a number has already made a category error. This single fact is the seed for differential amplifiers, reference electrodes, montages, and common-mode rejection, all of which show up later. The diagram below is the whole idea: one electrode gives you a question, two give you an answer.

One wire asks a question. Two wires answer it.

Every channel on the device will use at least two electrode connections, because a difference is the only thing that physically exists to measure. The whole reason serious front-ends are built around differential inputs starts right here.

The axis is logarithmic, spanning a microvolt up to a volt, because nothing else can hold a microvolt and a third of a volt in the same picture. The "bits just to span it" figure is the floor, not the spec: real designs need headroom and noise margin on top, which is the whole reason 24-bit parts exist for signals that only swing a few microvolts.

The whole zoo lives on a log axis

It helps to see all the players at once. The biopotentials we will chase span a huge amplitude range on their own: EEG at the faint extreme, around 1 to 100 microvolts; EMG from muscle, broad and reaching toward a millivolt; ECG from the heart at roughly a millivolt. And sitting on top of all of them are the things we do not want: the electrode's DC offset in the hundreds of millivolts, and mains hum that couples into the leads and can dwarf everything. What this means if you are building the hardware

Three things fall straight out of this post and shape every later decision.

First, every channel uses at least two electrodes, because voltage is a difference and there is no single-ended shortcut hiding anywhere. Second, the front-end's job is rejection, not mainly gain. Its real work is to throw away offsets and interference and carry a tiny structured variation intact to the converter, which is exactly why serious parts are built around differential inputs, high common-mode rejection, and a wide-dynamic-range delta-sigma converter rather than a big amplifier. Third, "it is just a voltage to an ADC" is the mindset to unlearn. The ADC is the easy part. Whether you end up seeing alpha rhythm or just seeing hum is decided almost entirely by everything that surrounds it.

That is why the rest of this unit walks from a single cell's membrane out to the population signal you actually record. Before we can defend a microvolt, we have to know exactly where it comes from and what shape it has. Next post: the cell membrane as a battery, and where that resting voltage actually comes from.

A biopotential is a difference in potential set up by ionic charge, measured as a faint shadow at the skin and buried under offsets and hum thousands of times larger. The job is not to amplify. It is to reject the mess and digitise the meaning.
