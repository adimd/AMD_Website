Biosignal Acquisition for Embedded Engineers · Class 3

The Spike That Builds Itself

A resting cell just holds a steady voltage. Add one kind of channel, the kind that opens when the voltage changes, and the cell can fire a spike: a quick jump that is the same size every time and travels without shrinking. The spike lasts about a millisecond, and that millisecond is why EMG and EEG need different settings on the same recorder.

A stable battery has a problem

Last class we left the membrane sitting at about minus 70 millivolts. It stays there because at rest the only channels much open are potassium channels, so potassium sets the voltage, and it ends up near potassium's own equilibrium of about minus 89 millivolts. That resting state is stable, and that is a problem.

Stable means it corrects itself. Push the voltage up a little and potassium flows out, taking positive charge with it, which pulls the voltage back down. Push it down and the opposite happens. It works like a thermostat: small changes get cancelled. And a thermostat does not suddenly swing on its own.

But cells do fire. A nerve or muscle cell can jump by about 100 millivolts for roughly a millisecond and then drop back. It either happens all the way or not at all, and once it starts it runs down the whole cell without getting smaller. None of that is in the resting picture, so something is missing. The rest of this post is about what.

The missing piece: channels that open with voltage

In Class 1.2 I treated the open channels as fixed doors: at rest, mostly potassium doors are open. That was a simplification, and the part I left out is the whole story here. Some channels are not fixed. Whether they are open depends on the voltage across the membrane. These are the voltage-gated channels, and two of them matter, one for sodium and one for potassium.

Here is why that one change matters so much. A fixed channel just sits there. But a channel that opens in response to the voltage, and that by opening also changes the voltage, is wired into a loop: it reacts to the very thing it affects. Loops like that do one of two things.

If opening the channel pushes the voltage back the way it came, the loop is calming. That is the resting potassium leak from a moment ago: nudge the voltage up, more potassium leaves, the voltage comes back down. Small disturbances die out. Same steadying behavior as a thermostat.

If instead opening the channel pushes the voltage further in the direction it was already heading, the loop runs away. A small push gets amplified into a bigger one, which gets amplified again. You have heard this happen: a microphone held too close to its own speaker. The speaker puts out a faint sound, the mic picks it up, the speaker makes it louder, the mic picks up the louder version, and within a second it is a screech. Nobody added energy on purpose. The loop just fed itself.

Two loops: one settles, one takes off

Same idea, two wirings. On the left the response undoes the push, so a nudge fades and the voltage returns to rest. On the right the response feeds the push, so a nudge grows on its own. The sodium channel is the right-hand kind, which is why a big enough push does not fade but launches.

The voltage-gated sodium channel is wired the second way, and we will see exactly how in the next section. For now the shape of the answer is enough: put a runaway loop next to a stable resting state and you get a trigger. The cell ignores small pushes and stays quiet, until one push is big enough to start the loop feeding itself. After that it fires on its own, all the way, no matter what set it off.

Sodium is under a lot of pressure

Before anything fires, it helps to see how much is built up and waiting. At rest the sodium channels are shut, but there is a lot of sodium outside the cell and very little inside. From last class, sodium's equilibrium voltage is about plus 60 millivolts. So at rest both forces on sodium point inward: the concentration difference pushes it in, and the negative inside pulls the positive sodium in as well.

Two forces pushing sodium in, one shut gate holding it out

Both arrows point the same way, inward, and they are strong because sodium is crowded outside and the inside is negative. The only thing stopping a flood is the shut gate. The whole spike begins the instant that gate opens.

We can measure how hard it is pushing with one number, the driving force. That is the membrane voltage minus the ion's own equilibrium voltage, which is what is left after diffusion and the field are accounted for. An ion's current is its conductance times that leftover:

I_ion = g_ion × (V_m − E_ion)

For sodium at rest:

V_m − E_Na = (−70) − (+60) = −130 mV

That is a big push. Potassium, by comparison, has only (−70) − (−89) = +19 mV. Sodium is pressing on its shut doors almost seven times harder than potassium presses on its open ones. Nothing happens only because the sodium conductance g_Na is almost zero while the doors are closed. Open them, and that push is suddenly free to act.

How hard each ion is pushing at rest

The resting voltage sits close to potassium, so potassium has little push left. Sodium sits far away with a large push that nothing is using yet. The whole spike is what happens when the sodium door opens briefly and that push is released.

The upstroke: a loop that runs away

Now push the membrane a little less negative, from minus 70 up toward minus 55. The sodium channels open with voltage, so that small push opens a few of them. Sodium flows in. Sodium is positive, so it makes the inside more positive, which is a bigger push in the same direction, which opens more sodium channels, which lets in more sodium.

That feedback speeds itself up. The voltage does not drift up, it shoots up, going from rest toward sodium's equilibrium in well under a millisecond. The inside, negative a moment ago, passes zero and goes briefly positive, peaking around plus 30 millivolts. It does not quite reach the full plus 60 of sodium's equilibrium, for reasons in the reset section below, but the jump past zero is real and shows up on every recording.

Why the rise speeds up as it climbs

Each bit of rise opens more sodium channels, which lets in more sodium, which raises the voltage faster. That is why the curve is gentle near threshold and almost vertical higher up. The loop is feeding itself, and the steepening slope is what that looks like.

A spike is not a signal forced through the membrane. It is the membrane tipping over: a stable resting state sitting next to a strong feedback loop, set off once the voltage crosses a threshold.

Threshold, and why the spike is all-or-nothing

The same feedback explains the most important thing about the spike. At any moment two currents are fighting on the membrane. Sodium flowing in pushes the voltage up. The potassium leak pulls it back down. Whether a push turns into a spike depends on which one is winning.

For a small push, potassium wins. Only a few sodium channels opened, not much sodium comes in, and the potassium current pulls the voltage back to rest. Nothing happens. This is a push below threshold. But there is a tipping point, the threshold, usually around minus 55 millivolts. Past it, sodium coming in opens new channels faster than potassium can pull back. Now the loop keeps itself going, and the spike finishes on its own no matter what started it.

So it is basically all-or-nothing. Just past threshold, you get a full spike. Far past threshold, you get the same full spike, the same size. The size tells you nothing about how hard you pushed. Only whether you crossed threshold matters. What carries information is when spikes happen and how often, not how big they are. The simulator below runs the real equations. Set a stimulus and watch the membrane either settle back to rest or make a full spike that looks the same whether you barely crossed threshold or went way past it.

The conductance picture, from Hodgkin and Huxley

We have been saying the sodium conductance and the potassium conductance change during the spike. Now let us pin down what sets them, because that is what the simulator is really computing.

Start with the simplest part. The conductance of a patch of membrane to an ion is set by how many of that ion's channels are open right now. All of them open gives the maximum conductance; all shut gives zero; half open gives half. So if we call the maximum ḡ (g with a bar over it), then at any moment

g = ḡ × (fraction of channels open)

The whole job is now one question: what fraction of the channels is open at this instant? Everything else is working that fraction out.

Why a channel needs more than one gate to be open

A single channel does not just flip fully open or fully shut. Inside it are a few small gates, and the channel only lets ions through when all of its gates happen to be open at the same time. Each gate flutters open and shut on its own, and we track it with a number between 0 and 1: the fraction of the time that gate is open, which you can also read as the chance it is open at any instant. Hodgkin and Huxley named these numbers m, h, and n.

Here is where the exponents come from, and it is not deep. If a channel needs several gates open at once, and each gate is open with some chance, then the chance they are all open together is those chances multiplied. So if a sodium channel has three gates of the same kind, each open with chance m, the chance all three are open is m × m × m = m³. The exponent is just how many gates of that kind have to line up. Nothing more.

The exponents are just gates multiplied together

Ions get through only when every gate is open at once, so you multiply the open-chances. Three sodium gates of type m and one of type h give an open fraction of m³h. Four potassium gates of type n give n⁴. The exponent is a head count of gates, not anything mysterious.

The two kinds of sodium gate, and why that builds a spike

Put the maximum conductance and the open fraction together and you get the two lines Hodgkin and Huxley wrote:

g_Na = ḡ_Na · m³ · h g_K = ḡ_K · n⁴

The sodium channel has two kinds of gate, and they react to voltage in opposite directions and at different speeds. The three m gates are fast, and they open when the voltage rises. The single h gate is slow, and it closes when the voltage rises. Sodium gets through only when all four are open at once.

Now watch what that does over the course of a spike. At rest m is near 0 (the fast gates are shut) and h is near 1 (the slow gate is open and ready). The voltage starts to climb:

  • The fast m gates fly open, so m³ shoots up while h, being slow, is still near 1. So m³h is briefly large, sodium pours in, and that is the upstroke.
  • A short time later the slow h gate finally swings shut. Now m³h collapses and sodium stops, even though the voltage is still high.

That gap in timing, fast gates opening and a slow gate closing a beat later, is the whole reason the sodium current is a brief pulse rather than a permanent flood. The channel shuts itself off. Potassium is simpler: four n gates, all slow, all opening when the voltage rises. Because they are slow, the potassium conductance turns on late, after the peak, and that is what pulls the voltage back down.

Fast m, slow h, slow n, and the spike that results

Top: m snaps up and back, h slides down and slowly recovers, n drifts up late. Bottom: multiply them as m³h and n⁴ and you get a sharp early sodium pulse (which drives the upstroke) followed by a slower potassium hump (which brings the voltage down). These are schematic shapes; the simulator computes the exact ones, and this is the moment to scroll up and switch its conductances on.

So the one thing to carry forward is the timing: m is fast, h and n are slow. The fast gate builds the spike, and the slow gates take it down. The exponents are only a count of how many gates have to agree.

The reset, the phases, and the dip below rest

If the loop only ran forward, the membrane would stay stuck at the peak. Two slow effects bring it back, and both arrive a little late. The first is sodium inactivation, the slow fall of h, which shuts the sodium channels a fraction of a millisecond after they open. The sodium current stops. The second is the delayed potassium conductance, the slow rise of n, which opens extra potassium channels just as the peak arrives. With sodium shutting off and potassium opening, the balance swings hard toward potassium and the voltage falls back down toward potassium's equilibrium.

The potassium channels are slow to close, so they stay open slightly too long, and the voltage dips a little below the resting level before settling. That dip is the afterhyperpolarization, and it is a real part of the waveform, not an error. Here is the full shape with its phases labeled.

The action potential, phase by phase

Resting, then a push to threshold, then the runaway upstroke, the overshoot past zero, the potassium-driven fall, the dip below rest, and recovery. The active part of the event is over in about a millisecond. That millisecond is what sets the bandwidth your hardware needs.

The two-gate channel and the refractory period

The inactivation gate h does more than end one spike. It also sets when the next one is allowed, which gives the membrane a built-in dead time. Think of the sodium channel as a tube with two gates in a row, the fast activation gate and the slow inactivation gate, and both have to be open for sodium to get through. That gives three states.

One channel, two gates, three states

At rest, the fast gate blocks. During the spike both are open and sodium pours through. Then the slow inactivation gate swings in and plugs the channel, and it stays plugged until the voltage has been low again for long enough to reset it. That reset delay is the refractory period.

This gives two kinds of refractoriness. Right after a spike, in the absolute refractory period, so many inactivation gates are still shut that no stimulus, however strong, can fire another spike. A little later, in the relative refractory period, inactivation has partly recovered and the delayed potassium is still up, so a spike is possible but needs a stronger push. You can see the relative period in the simulator: hold the current on and the spikes settle into a steady train at a capped rate, because each spike has to wait for the channels behind it to recover.

The refractory period is not a flaw to design around. It does two useful jobs. It limits how fast the cell can fire, and it keeps the spike moving in one direction, which is the next section.

How the spike travels without shrinking

A spike stuck in one patch of membrane would be no use. Here is how it moves. When a patch fires, the sodium that rushes in does not all stay put; some of that charge spreads sideways inside the cell and depolarizes the next patch. That patch is pushed past its own threshold, fires its own full-size spike, depolarizes the patch after it, and so on down the cell. The spike is not carried from one end to the other. It is made fresh at each step, every patch firing from its own ion gradients.

That is why the spike keeps its height. One that starts at full size arrives at full size a meter away, because every point along the way rebuilds it using local energy. And the patch just behind the moving front is refractory, so it cannot fire again, which means the wave cannot turn around or echo backward. It goes one way only.

This is different from the other way voltage moves through the body, the passive spread we cover in Class 1.5, where a voltage simply leaks outward through the resistance and capacitance of the tissue and gets smaller with distance. So two different things happen in the body at once: the spike is an active wave that rebuilds itself and does not shrink, while plain conduction through tissue is passive and fades. The difference matters, because what reaches a scalp electrode is the faded, passive version of these events, never the spikes themselves.

Active regeneration versus passive decay

Top: the axon carrying a signal at full strength over a long distance. Bottom: what happens to a voltage that is only conducted through tissue. The signal at your electrode comes from the bottom case acting on many summed sources, which is why it is so small.

Two practical points. The body speeds this wave up in two ways: by making the fiber thicker, which lowers its internal resistance so charge spreads further per step, and by wrapping it in myelin insulation broken by gaps, so the spike jumps from gap to gap instead of crawling along continuously. Speeds range from under a meter per second in thin bare fibers to over a hundred meters per second in thick myelinated ones. None of this changes the spike's height, only how fast it arrives.

The charge still adds up

It is worth checking that the spike, big as it looks, moves about the same small amount of charge as the quiet resting potential. The voltage swings by about 100 millivolts, and the membrane capacitance is still about 1 microfarad per square centimeter, so the charge needed to swing it is

Q = C · ΔV = (1 µF/cm²)(0.1 V) = 1 × 10⁻⁷ C/cm²

Divide by Faraday's constant and you get about 10⁻¹² moles of ions per square centimeter per spike, a thin layer next to all the ions in the cell. A cell can fire many times before its gradients drop much, and the pump restores them between spikes. So under the big fast event, only a little charge has moved across a small capacitance. That is why the battery-and-capacitor picture from last class still holds.

What this means at the bench

Three things from this carry straight to the hardware, and the first is the one that drives the most decisions.

The millisecond sets the bandwidth. Something that rises and falls in about a millisecond has frequency content reaching up into the high hundreds of hertz. Any signal made directly of these spikes, EMG from muscle most of all, is therefore wide-band, and needs sample rates in the kilohertz range with an analog bandwidth to match. When we get to sampling and Nyquist in Unit 3, this is the fact behind those chapters: you cannot pick a sample rate until you know whether your signal is fast spikes or slow waves. It is also why one configurable recorder needs an EMG mode and an EEG mode with very different filter and rate settings.

You will never see 100 millivolts. That swing is across the membrane, measured inside the cell. A scalp or skin electrode sees only the faint, distant, summed remainder of huge numbers of these events, weakened by everything between the source and the surface. That is how a 100 millivolt event at the cell becomes a few microvolts at your amplifier. Working out how the signal shrinks from membrane to surface is what Classes 1.4 and 1.5 are for.

What you mostly record is not the spike. Spikes are brief and tend to fire out of step, so when many of them add up across a population their sharp shapes partly cancel and the distant signal is small. Slower events, like the synaptic potentials that move across many neurons at roughly the same time, add up much better and dominate what a surface EEG electrode sees. That is why scalp EEG is mostly slow, synchronized activity rather than individual spikes, and why EEG sits at low frequencies while EMG, which rides the spikes directly, sits at high ones. We build that contrast over the next few classes. Here we just needed the single event that all of it is made from.

The action potential is a one millisecond, same-size-every-time pulse that rebuilds itself as it travels. Its speed sets your bandwidth, its drop from 100 millivolts to a few microvolts sets your sensitivity, and whether your signal is made of these fast spikes or of slower synchronized waves decides most of your hardware settings.
