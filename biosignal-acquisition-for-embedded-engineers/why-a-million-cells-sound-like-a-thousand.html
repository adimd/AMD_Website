Why a Million Cells Sound Like a Thousand

A scalp electrode never hears one cell. It hears a crowd. So the only thing we really need to understand is how a sum of many small pushes grows as you add more of them. We will add them one at a time, watch what each new cell does, and let the pattern hand us the answer.

The whole class, in one question

One cell swings about 100 millivolts across its membrane. By the time anything reaches a scalp electrode it is a few microvolts. Part of that shrinking is distance, which is next class. But part of it is that the electrode is never listening to one cell. It is adding up the contributions of an enormous number of cells at once.

So everything reduces to one question, and the rest of this post is just answering it carefully: when you add up many small pushes, how big does the total get? The lazy answer is that N cells give N times one cell. That is true in one special case and badly wrong in another, and we cannot tell them apart by guessing. So we will not guess. We will add the cells one at a time, watch exactly what each new one does to the total, and let the pattern force the formula on us.

One cell, then two

One cell contributes some small voltage. Call its size a. Nothing to add up yet, so the total is just a. That is the whole of N = 1.

Add a second cell, and right away we hit something we have to settle before we can go any further: each cell's push has a sign. Sometimes it pushes the total up, sometimes down, depending on what the cell is doing at that instant. With two cells there are exactly four equally likely combinations, and it is worth seeing all of them.

Two cells: every possible outcome

Half the time the two cells disagree and the total is zero. Only when they agree do you reach the full 2a. So the second cell does not reliably add its full size; on average a lot of it is cancelled.

That picture already tells us the lazy answer is wrong, and it also tells us we have a measurement problem. If we try to describe the size of the two-cell total by its plain average, we get zero, because the +2a and the −2a cancel. Zero is obviously the wrong description of a thing that is usually not zero. So before we can add a third cell, we have to fix how we measure size.

The one honest way to measure a thing that can be positive or negative

The fix is forced, and it is the move the whole class turns on. To measure the size of something that is randomly positive or negative, you cannot average it directly, because signs cancel. So you square it first, which makes every value positive, then you average the squares, then you take the square root at the end to get back to the original units. Square, average, root. You have met the result on a multimeter: it is the root-mean-square, the RMS.

Let us run it on the two-cell total. Square each of the four outcomes: (+2a)² = 4a², then 0, then 0, then (−2a)² = 4a². Average them: (4a² + 0 + 0 + 4a²)/4 = 2a². So the average of the squared total is 2a². We will track that quantity, the average squared total, all the way up, because it is the one that adds cleanly. For one cell it was a² (the square of ±a is always a²). For two it is 2a².

Stop and notice the jump: it went from a² to 2a². It went up by exactly a², which is one cell's worth. It did not go up to (2a)² = 4a², which is what it would be if the second cell added its full size. The second cell contributed only its own square and none of the cross effect with the first. Hold that, because the next cell is going to do the same thing, and once we see why, we are done.

The third cell tells us the rule

Add a third cell, x₃, to the running total of the first two, which we will call S₂. The new total is S₂ + x₃. Square it, since the squared total is what we track:

(S₂ + x₃)² = S₂² + 2·S₂·x₃ + x₃²

Now average each piece. The average of S₂² we already have: 2a². The average of x₃² is a², as always. The interesting one is the middle, 2·S₂·x₃. The new cell x₃ is independent of the first two and is equally likely to be +a or −a, so whatever S₂ happens to be, multiplying it by x₃ is equally likely to flip it positive or negative. Over many tries that averages to zero. So the cross term vanishes, and

average squared total for 3 cells = 2a² + 0 + a² = 3a²

The third cell added a², exactly like the second. And look at why: the only thing a new cell can add beyond its own square is its cross term with everything already there, and for an independent random push that cross term always averages to zero. There is nothing special about the third cell. Every new out-of-step cell adds exactly its own a² to the average squared total, because its cross term with the existing pile cancels itself out.

That is the rule, and the pattern is now forced. Add the cells yourself below and watch the average squared total climb by exactly one each time.

Out of step, every cell adds exactly 1 to the squared total, so after N cells it is N, and the typical size (its square root) is √N. In step, shown alongside, each new cell also brings a cross term, but now the cross term reinforces instead of cancelling, so the squared total runs up as 1, 4, 9, 16, which is N², and the typical size is N. Same cells. The only difference is what the cross term does.

The formula, and where the second case comes from

For out-of-step cells the squared total is the running sum of a² taken N times, so it is N a², and the typical size is its square root:

typical total, out of step = √(N a²) = a√N

The whole derivation rested on one assumption, and it is worth saying out loud: the cross term of each new cell with the existing pile averaged to zero because the cells were independent, each doing its own thing. That is the out-of-step case. So the natural next question is the only one left: what happens if they are not independent, if they are all doing the same thing at the same instant?

Then the cross term no longer cancels. If every cell is the same +a, then when you add the third cell its cross term with the running total S₂ = +2a is 2·(2a)·a = 4a², a large positive number, not zero. Every cell reinforces every other one. You can watch this in the "in step" column above: the squared total runs up as 1, 4, 9, 16, which is N², so the typical size is

typical total, in step = √(N² a²) = N a

So the two cases are not two separate facts. They are the same calculation with the cross term either cancelling or reinforcing, and which one happens depends only on whether the cells are in step.

Out of step, each cell adds only its own square, so the total grows like the square root of N. In step, the cross terms reinforce, so the total grows like N. Timing is the only difference.
going deeper: the all-at-once version

The one-cell-at-a-time argument is a proof by itself, but here is the same thing done in a single step, which some people find cleaner. Squaring the whole total at once means multiplying the sum by itself, and that pairs every cell with every cell, producing a grid of products x_i · x_j. Toggle the two cases and watch the grid.

diagonal (always +a², N of them)
off-diagonal cross terms
whole grid = (total)²
typical size = √(grid)

The diagonal of the grid is the cells multiplied by themselves: always +a², always there, N of them. That is the part that gave us the running sum of squares. The off-diagonal is the cross terms. Out of step they are equally often plus and minus and cancel, leaving just the diagonal, so the grid totals N a² and the root is a√N. In step they are all +a² and fill the whole grid, so it totals N² a² and the root is Na. Same conclusion as the step-by-step build, seen whole.

Two quick confirmations

We have the result. Before spending it, here are two places you may already have met it, so it feels less like algebra and more like something true about the world.

Coins. Out-of-step cells are exactly coin flips: each a push of +1 or −1 at random. Flip 100 coins and count heads minus tails, and you do not get anywhere near 100; you get a leftover imbalance of about 10. Flip 10,000 and the imbalance is about 100. A hundred times the flips, only ten times the imbalance. That is √N, the same formula we just built, showing up in your hand.

Applause. A full auditorium clapping at random is a steady wash of sound with no punch to it, the small √N total. The instant the room falls into a rhythm and claps together, it becomes a loud thud you feel in your chest, the N total, from the very same hands. The loudness of a crowd was never the number of hands. It was their togetherness.

How big the gap gets

Now spend the result. The two totals are N a in step and a√N out of step, so the ratio between them, the price of being out of step, is

(N a) / (a√N) = √N

For real populations that lever is huge. Take one cell up to a million on the slider and watch the in-step total and the typical out-of-step total separate. The honest random draw, a real sum of that many coin flips, always lands near the √N line and never near the N line.

A million in step is a million. A million out of step is about a thousand.

The random draw jumps a little each redraw, because it is a real random sum, but it stays near √N. That gap, √N, is the difference between a signal you can record and one lost in the background, and nothing physical changed between the two cases except whether the cells acted together.

Nobody is perfectly in step, and that is the interesting part

Real cells are neither perfectly together nor perfectly independent. They are partly correlated, a little shared rhythm mixed with their own activity. So where does the total sit in between, and how quickly does a little togetherness start to count? This is the same calculation once more, with the cross terms allowed to be partly aligned instead of fully cancelling or fully adding. Measure togetherness with one number ρ from 0 (independent) to 1 (perfectly together), and the total is

total = a × √( N (1 + (N − 1)ρ) )

The ends check out: ρ = 0 gives a√N and ρ = 1 gives aN, our two cases. The surprise is in the (N − 1)ρ term. Move the synchrony knob, especially at large N, and watch how little togetherness it takes to leave the random floor behind.

At a million cells, a synchrony of 0.1 percent already lifts the total from a thousand past thirty thousand, because when N is huge the (N−1)ρ term dwarfs the 1 beside it. So for a large population the recording is set by whatever faint shared rhythm runs through it, not by how busy it is. Desynchronizing a region, which is what happens when it gets to work, can therefore make its surface signal smaller. We meet that as the alpha block in Class 1.8.

What you are actually measuring

Now the payoff, and it follows directly from everything above. A surface biopotential is not a measure of how much activity is happening underneath. It is mostly a measure of how synchronized that activity is. A busy patch of cortex whose cells are out of step gives the small √N signal; the same patch loosely locked together gives something hundreds or thousands of times larger, from the same work. The applause again: loudness is togetherness.

That is why scalp EEG looks the way it does. Slow synaptic potentials wash across many neurons at nearly the same time, so they are in step and add like N, and they dominate the recording. The fast spikes from Class 1.3 fire out of step, so they add like √N and nearly vanish beside them. EEG is a low-frequency picture not because the fast activity is missing, but because it cancels itself down to the floor while the slow synchronized activity rides up on the full gain.

Why the slow, together part wins at the scalp

The slow waves line up into a big smooth signal; the spikes land at random times and cancel into a small ragged remainder. A scalp electrode sees mostly the top one, and a single neuron, a population of one, is invisible.

The same square root when you average (a peek at Unit 3)

One more place the same rule lives, because we will lean on it hard later. Record the same event N times and average the repeats. The real signal is identical on every trial, so across trials it is in step and grows like N. The noise is different each time, so across trials it is out of step and grows like √N. The signal outgrows the noise, and the ratio improves by

SNR gain from averaging N trials = N / √N = √N

It is the identical calculation, with repeats in time in place of cells in space. Average 100 trials, clean up the signal by 10. Synchrony across space and repetition across time are the same square root.

What this means at the bench

Recording rests on synchrony. You never amplify one cell; you rely on a population firing together so its signal climbs by the N factor rather than the √N one. When a datasheet says scalp EEG is a few microvolts, that microvolt is already the synchronized sum. The raw activity underneath is far larger but cancels, so you are building for the part that survived, which sets how sensitive and quiet the front-end has to be.

Timing smear is expensive. Because the signal is a synchrony measure, anything that blurs timing across your sources does more than add noise. It can drag a signal from the N regime toward the √N regime and cost a whole factor of √N. That is part of why electrode placement and the choice of reference, later in this unit, matter so much: they decide which sources line up at your two contacts.

Keep the square root in your pocket. It returns throughout the noise budget in Unit 3. Cells, noise sources, or trials, the first question is always the same: in step or independent? In step they add like N; independent, like √N. Almost every signal-against-noise argument left in the course is that one question in a new setting.

A surface biopotential is a synchrony meter. In-step sources add like N, independent ones like the square root of N, and because even a little synchrony tips a large population toward N, the recording is set by the faint shared rhythm in the crowd, not by how busy it is.
