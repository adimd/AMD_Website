A Battery Made of Salt Water

Every living cell holds a steady voltage across a membrane two molecules thick, and it does it while sitting electrically still. This post derives that voltage from one law of statistical physics, then springs a trap: the same equation produces the single largest offset your electrodes will ever fight.

A voltage that just sits there

Last time I said a biopotential exists because cells "actively separate ionic charge," and then I moved on before answering the obvious question: separate it how, and why does that make a voltage that just sits there? Look at what we are actually claiming. A cell at rest, not firing, not signalling, doing nothing you would call dramatic, still holds about 70 millivolts across its membrane, inside negative. It holds it for hours, the way a battery on a shelf holds 1.5 volts: quietly, indefinitely, with nothing obviously happening.

A voltage usually means something is going on, yet here is a thing that is electrically quiet and maintains a potential difference forever. So: what is the battery? What chemistry pins a stable voltage across a sheet of fat, and can we predict its value from first principles instead of memorising "about minus seventy" from a chart? Answer that and we own the source of every signal in this series. There is also a second result waiting at the end that I will get to once we have the equation.

Three ingredients, and the fight between them

The whole result falls out of three facts rubbing against each other.

The first is a concentration gradient. The water inside the cell and the water outside are both salty, but not the same salty. Potassium ions (K+) are packed densely inside and scarce outside, roughly 140 against 5 in the usual units. Sodium (Na+) is the mirror image, plentiful outside and rare within. Somebody built those gradients and somebody pays to keep them, which we will come back to.

The second is selectivity. The membrane is an oily insulator that ions cannot cross unaided, punched through with protein channels that act as fussy doorways. At rest, the doors that happen to be open are mostly potassium doors, so the resting membrane lets K+ through and blocks nearly everything else. That pickiness is the mechanism, not a footnote.

The third a circuits person already knows. A thin insulating sheet with a conductor on each face is a capacitor, and the membrane, with salt water inside and out, is exactly that. It becomes important at the end.

Now watch the three collide. Potassium is crowded inside and empty outside, so diffusion, the blind statistical drift of crowded things toward open space, shoves K+ out through the open potassium doors. But every K+ that leaves carries its positive charge out and abandons a negative charge inside. The interior tips negative. And a negative interior reaches back out and tugs the positive potassium toward home. So one ion feels two influences at once, pointed opposite ways: diffusion pushing it out, the growing electric field pulling it back. The voltage slides until those two exactly cancel, net flow stops, and whatever voltage exists at that instant simply stays. That balance point is the number we are after, and it is called the Nernst potential.

Two forces on one ion, pointed opposite ways

The crowd inside wants to spill out; the negativity it leaves behind wants to pull it back. The resting voltage is precisely the value at which those two exactly cancel for potassium. Everything below is just putting a number on that balance.

The one law we need: population follows energy, exponentially

I am going to borrow exactly one deep fact, and it is the cornerstone of all statistical physics. Put a pile of particles in contact with a warm bath at temperature T, let them settle, and the probability of finding a particle in a state of energy E is proportional to

probability(E) ∝ exp( −E / kT )

with k the Boltzmann constant and kT the thermal energy scale. In plain words: costlier states are less populated, and the population falls off exponentially as the cost climbs, with kT setting how steep the fall is. The intuition, if you want one: to sit in a pricier spot a particle has to have scrounged that much extra energy from random thermal kicks, and the odds of having scrounged a given surplus shrink exponentially with how much you need. A warm bath (large kT) is generous, so particles spill into expensive states easily; a cold bath (small kT) keeps them huddled in the cheap ones. The curve is the same shape that sets our concentrations: the amount of potassium that ends up on each side of the membrane is just how populated that side is, and population is governed by this exponential. That single connection is what turns a chemistry question into an equation we can solve.

From the law to the voltage, in four steps

Now the derivation. Each symbol enters only when the physics forces it.

Step 1: concentration is just population

Concentration is particles per unit volume, so the ratio of potassium inside to potassium outside is exactly the ratio of how populated the two sides are. Boltzmann hands us that ratio directly as a ratio of exponentials:

c_in / c_out = exp( −E_in / kT ) / exp( −E_out / kT ) = exp( −(E_in − E_out) / kT )

That is most of the derivation. All that remains is to name the energy difference, then dig the voltage out of it.

Step 2: the only energy difference is electrical

At equilibrium both sides are the same salt water at the same temperature, so the only thing that makes a potassium ion's energy differ between inside and outside is the electricity. A charge q sitting at electrical potential φ has potential energy E = qφ, the most elementary fact in this whole course. For an ion of charge number z (that is +1 for potassium, +2 for calcium, −1 for chloride) the charge is q = ze with e the elementary charge, so

E_in − E_out = ze(φ_in − φ_out) = ze · V_m

where I define the membrane voltage the way we always will, inside minus outside: V_m = φ_in − φ_out. Notice there was no chemistry to invent. Charge times voltage equals energy did the work.

Step 3: substitute, then take a logarithm

Drop that energy difference into the population ratio from Step 1:

c_in / c_out = exp( −ze V_m / kT )

The voltage is trapped up in the exponent, and the natural way to free it is to take the log of both sides:

ln( c_in / c_out ) = −ze V_m / kT

Step 4: solve for V_m

V_m = −(kT / ze) ln( c_in / c_out ) = (kT / ze) ln( c_out / c_in )

That is the Nernst equation, and now look at where the logarithm came from. It is nothing but the inverse of Boltzmann's exponential. Population depends on energy exponentially, so when we run the relationship backwards to pull the energy (and therefore the voltage) out of a population ratio, the exponential inverts into a log. The log is not a quirk of biology; it is the fingerprint of thermal statistics. And kT rode along for the same reason: temperature set the scale of the exponential, so it sets the scale of the voltage.

One cosmetic step matches the textbooks. We worked per ion, with k and e. Physiologists count per mole, so multiply top and bottom of the prefactor by Avogadro's number: k times Avogadro is the gas constant R, and e times Avogadro is the Faraday constant F. Both scale together, so kT/ze becomes RT/zF unchanged:

V_m = (RT / zF) · ln( c_out / c_in ). The equilibrium voltage is set by temperature and a ratio of concentrations, nothing else. Per ion or per mole is just a choice of counting unit.

Does the formula behave?

Before trusting any equation I poke it at the edges, because a formula that misbehaves at the extremes is lying somewhere in the middle too. Equal concentrations: the ratio is 1, the log is 0, the voltage is 0. Good, no gradient means no battery. Steepen the gradient: the ratio runs further from 1, the log grows, and the voltage grows with it, exactly as a stronger concentration difference should need a stronger field to hold it. Warm it up: RT rises and so does the voltage, which fits, since hotter diffusion needs a bigger field to oppose it. Flip the charge from +1 to −1: the voltage flips sign, as it must, because a negative ion feels the field the opposite way. Four checks, four passes. Now I believe it, and now we can compute.

Put numbers in: build any ion's battery yourself

Start with the prefactor at body temperature, T = 310 kelvin, R = 8.314, F = 96485:

RT / F = (8.314 × 310) / 96485 ≈ 0.0267 V = 26.7 mV

Commit that 26.7 mV to memory; it is roughly the thermal voltage at body temperature and it turns up constantly. For potassium, z is +1, so the prefactor stays 26.7 mV, and with 5 outside over 140 inside:

V_m = 26.7 mV × ln(5 / 140) = 26.7 mV × (−3.33) ≈ −89 mV

A real physiological voltage, pulled from a temperature and a concentration ratio with no fitting. Now do it for every ion at once, and watch where each one's battery wants to sit. Pick an ion, then drag the concentrations or the temperature and see the Nernst potential move.

The same answer from balancing two flows

If the Boltzmann route felt too statistical, here is the same result from watching the two flows cancel directly. Diffusion drives a flux proportional to the concentration gradient (Fick's law). The field drives a drift flux proportional to concentration times the voltage gradient. Set the total flux to zero so they cancel at every point in the membrane, separate variables, and integrate across. The integral of dc/c is a logarithm, and you land on the identical V_m = (RT/zF) ln(c_out/c_in). Both pictures, balance of populations and balance of currents, describe the same equilibrium, so it is no accident they give the same formula. The log shows up either way, once from inverting an exponential and once from integrating dc/c, which are the same fact reached from two directions.

Why minus 70 and not minus 89

I opened quoting minus 70 and then derived minus 89. That gap is the price of the "mostly potassium" simplification. The membrane is not a perfect potassium filter; a trickle of sodium leaks in, and sodium's own Nernst potential is strongly positive because its gradient points the other way. That trickle tugs the resting voltage up from pure potassium's minus 89 toward zero, parking it near minus 70. The full version weights each ion by how permeable the membrane is to it, a permeability-weighted tug of war between the ions' individual Nernst potentials, and that weighted formula is the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation. Potassium wins the rope only because its doors are the ones mostly open, so we sit close to its value but not on it.

The resting potential is a weighted tug of war

Resting potential is not any single ion's number; it is a weighted average that lands wherever the most permeable ion drags it. At rest that is potassium. When we reach the action potential next class, sodium's doors fly open, the weighting flips, and the voltage lunges toward E_Na. That flip is the spike.

Why the battery does not drain itself

This is where the capacitor earns its place, and it settles a worry the derivation may have planted. If potassium has to leave to build the voltage, does the cell slowly empty of potassium? Almost not at all. A membrane holds about 1 microfarad per square centimeter, and the charge needed to reach 90 millivolts is just Q = CV:

Q = (1 µF/cm²)(0.09 V) ≈ 9 × 10⁻⁸ C/cm²

Divide by Faraday's constant to turn coulombs into moles of potassium, and you get under 10⁻¹² moles per square centimeter that actually had to cross, about one ion in a hundred thousand against the 140 millimolar sitting inside. The voltage is genuine, but the charge that makes it is a vanishing skim off the top, so the bulk concentrations barely flinch. That is why our working electrical model of tissue, used all series long, is a battery (the Nernst potential) in parallel with a capacitor (the membrane): not a loose analogy, but a literal accounting of charge.

What the chemistry literally is, as a circuit

This little cell is the brick we will build tissue from. Populations of these, charging and discharging together, are literally what your electrodes pick up. When we model the body electrically later, we are transcribing this picture, not approximating it.

What this means at the bench

Back at the workbench, this class pays off in three concrete ways.

First, the signal source is now concrete. Every EEG rhythm, every EMG burst, every heartbeat traces back to populations of these Nernst batteries charging and discharging their membrane capacitors. When we draw battery-and-capacitor circuits for tissue later, we are not reaching for an analogy; we are copying down the physics the chemistry already wrote.

Second, a warning about current. The whole thing runs on that tiny charge across a tiny capacitance, so there is almost nothing to spare. The source is delicate and effectively high impedance, which means if your measurement draws meaningful current, you load the source and distort the very thing you came to read. That is why "high input impedance" will be non-negotiable when we specify the front-end, and why Unit 2 cares so much about how the electrode's impedance stacks against the amplifier behind it.

Third, the second result I mentioned at the start. The Nernst equation you just derived for a cell membrane is the same equation that governs the junction where your metal electrode meets the gel and skin. That junction is also two phases with different ion concentrations across a selective boundary, so it too sits at a Nernst-style equilibrium voltage, and that voltage is precisely the hundreds-of-millivolts DC offset I called "the mess" last class. The physics that creates your signal also creates the single largest obstacle in front of it, so most of Unit 2 is this same idea applied to metal and skin instead of membrane.

A cell is a battery in parallel with a capacitor. Its voltage is a temperature times a log of a concentration ratio, built from a tiny charge, maintained by a pump. The same equation sets your electrode's offset, which is why understanding the signal and understanding its biggest enemy are one task, not two.
