Everything That Repeats · Post 1

The Circle You Can Hear

Two instruments play the same note at the same volume, and you can tell them apart with your eyes shut. Nobody taught you that. This post follows one question, what your ear is actually noticing, until it forces us somewhere unexpected: away from sound entirely, and onto a spinning wheel. Everything here is playable, so put the volume somewhere sensible before you start.

Press both of these

Below are two instruments playing exactly the same note. Same volume. Press one, then the other.

Two instruments, one note
Both are the same note at the same volume. Press each one and notice that you can tell them apart instantly, without being able to say why.

You knew which was which before the first note finished. So does everyone. And if I ask you to say what the difference is, you will reach for words like warm, or breathy, or thin, all of which are true and none of which are measurements.

That should bother you slightly. The two properties of a sound that anybody can name are how high it is and how loud it is. Both are identical here. And yet these are obviously not the same sound, so there is a third property sitting there that your ear reads effortlessly and your vocabulary cannot touch.

If they match on loudness and on note, what is left to be different?

The only thing left

To answer that we have to be concrete about what a sound physically is, which takes one paragraph.

When the violin plays, the string shoves the air next to it. That air crowds together, springs apart, crowds again, and the disturbance travels outward until it reaches you. What arrives at your eardrum is not music. It is air pressure, going slightly above normal and slightly below, a few hundred times a second. Draw time along the bottom and pressure up the side, with a line where ordinary room pressure sits, and a sound is a wiggle around that line.

A note is a wiggle that repeats. Both of our sounds repeat 440 times every second, and that shared repeat rate is why your ear files them under the same note.

A word about two words. Frequency is the physical thing, the number of repeats per second, which you could read off an oscilloscope. Pitch is what your ear makes of it. Here the two line up neatly, so the distinction does not matter yet. It matters a great deal later, because there is a situation where you can delete the 440 from a sound entirely and listeners still hear an A.

Now press the buttons again, watching the trace this time.

Same repeat rate, different shape

Both traces cross those dotted markers in exactly the same places, because both repeat at exactly the same rate. Everything else about them differs.

So the third property has a name now, even if we cannot yet put a number on it. It is the shape of one repeat. That is the only thing left for your ear to be reading.

Which is progress, and also an awkward spot to be in. Shape is not a number. You cannot say a violin has 4.2 shape. To get anywhere we would need something to compare shapes against, some reference wiggle to measure the others by.

Is there a simplest possible repeating shape, one that could serve as the yardstick for all the rest?

The shape that keeps turning up

Candidates are not hard to find. A zigzag repeats. A square that jumps up, sits, jumps down, sits, repeats. A sawtooth repeats. Every one of those is a real sound and you have heard all of them, mostly out of cheap electronics.

But there is one shape that keeps appearing whether anyone asks for it or not. Here it is, at the same 440, alongside the other two.

The third one is the odd one out

The pure tone sounds like a hearing test, and that is exactly the point. It is characterless. Nothing has been added to it. It is the wiggle with nothing on top.

Now, the reason to care. Strike a tuning fork and its prong traces this curve. Hang a weight on a spring and let it bob, and its height traces this curve. Swing a pendulum gently and it traces this curve. Three completely unrelated bits of physics, no strings and no air between them, all producing the same shape.

That is too much of a coincidence to be a fact about sound. If forks and springs and pendulums all draw this curve, the curve belongs to motion, and sound is merely one place it shows up.

What kind of motion produces this particular curve?

Read the motion off the curve

Rather than guess, let us work backwards from what we have. If something out there traced this curve, then the picture is a record of what that something did, and we can interrogate the record.

So stop seeing the trace as a shape and start seeing it as a report on a moving object. The height of the curve is where the object was. Below, that motion is shown twice: on the right, plotted against time in the usual way, and on the left, stripped down to nothing but the height. The pale marks on the left-hand track are snapshots taken at equal intervals of time.

The motion hiding inside the curve
the pale marks are equally spaced in time, not in space
The marks are equally spaced in time, so where they crowd together the height was changing slowly, and where they spread apart it was changing fast.

One piece of vocabulary before we go on, because a single word is about to cause real trouble if we let it. From here, rate of climb means how fast the height is changing, and nothing else. It is large where the trace is steep and zero where the trace is flat. Keep it separate in your head from how fast anything is actually travelling. Those two are going to come apart later, and that parting is the whole point of this post.

What would a machine have to do to move like that?

Three demands, and the third is the hard one

Three things, and they are worth taking one at a time, because two are easy and the third is where everything turns.

One. It has to stay between two limits

Whatever it is, it never wanders off. It reaches a certain height, comes back, reaches the same depth, comes back again, and never once exceeds either. This rules out very little, since most repeating motions are bounded, but it does tell us the object is trapped rather than free.

Two. Its rate of climb has to peak exactly at the middle

You can read this straight off the trace, because steepness is the rate of climb. The arrows below are drawn with a length equal to the steepness beneath them.

Rate of climb, along one cycle
Longest arrows at the middle crossings, no arrow at all at the peaks. The rate of climb runs from maximum to zero and back, twice per cycle, and it is always in step with position.

So the rate of climb is not constant. It rises and falls, and its rising and falling is locked to where the object is: greatest dead centre, zero at the extremes, every cycle, never drifting out of step.

Three. It has to arrive at each end gently

Now the fussy one, and it needs care, because there is a distinction here that is very easy to blur.

At the very top of the curve the rate of climb is zero. It has to be: the object has finished going up and has not begun coming down, and there is no way to be doing both. So the height does come to a standstill, twice per cycle.

The question is not whether it stops. The question is how it arrives at the stop, and there are only two ways to arrive anywhere.

Two ways to reach a standstill
Both heights stop at the top. Only one of them slows down first.

You can slam into it. Full rate of climb right up to the last moment, then reverse on the spot. If the object did that, the top of the trace would come to a sharp point, because the height would be rising at full tilt one instant and falling at full tilt the next.

Or you can coast into it. Ease off, run out of climb just as you arrive, hang there for an instant, and gather pace the other way.

Look again at the top of our trace. It flattens as it approaches, rounds over, and flattens again as it leaves. No point, no kink. This one coasts.

The rate of climb falls away to exactly zero and grows again the other way, gradually, with no sudden change anywhere.

Three demands are not enough

Before hunting for a machine, there is an uncomfortable thing to notice, and skipping it would make everything that follows feel like a lucky guess.

Our three demands do not pin down a single curve. They rule out a great deal, and plenty of different shapes still satisfy every one of them. Drag the slider: each of these stays between two limits, climbs fastest at the middle, and eases to a stop with no corner. All three demands, met in full, by all of them.

Every one of those is a legitimate way to bob up and down, and every one would be a real sound. Only one is ours, and nothing in the three demands says which.

So the demands were necessary and not sufficient, and the missing ingredient is not a fourth demand. It is a mechanism. We have to stop describing what the motion looks like and find the thing that produces it.

What could bleed away a rate of climb so smoothly, and then hand it back?

The trouble with going straight up and down

Take the picture at face value. The object moves up and down along a line, and nothing else is going on.

Notice what that assumption quietly does. If up and down is all the object ever does, then the rate of climb is the object's speed. They are one number wearing two names, because every scrap of the object's motion is height and there is nowhere else for any of it to be.

And now demand two becomes a demand on the object itself. It has to physically speed up and slow down, twice per cycle, in perfect step with where it is. So something has to be taking its speed away as it climbs. Fine, but you have to say how much, and how much per what, and that is exactly the question the slider was asking. Lose speed evenly with height and you get one of those curves. Lose it slowly at first and sharply near the top and you get another. Lose it evenly with time and you get a third.

Nothing whatsoever about the phrase "it goes up and down" tells you which rule is correct. The gentle turnaround is not a consequence of the motion. It is an extra assumption you would have to bolt on by hand, and once you are choosing by hand you have not explained the curve. You have picked the answer you already wanted.

The one thing you can do without inventing any rule is to refuse to slow down: travel at a steady speed and flip direction the instant you hit the end. That is the honest, assumption-free version of up and down, and we will come back to see what it produces, because it is wrong in a useful way.

In one dimension the rate of climb and the speed are the same quantity, so the speed has to be destroyed on the way up and created again on the way down, and nothing in the picture is doing either.

What if the rate of climb and the speed were not the same quantity at all?

Two speeds, and we have been using one word

Here is the move that the whole of trigonometry rests on. It arrives looking like a contradiction, so let us put the contradiction on the table first and then take it apart.

The claim is going to be that the object travels at a speed which never changes, not even slightly, while its rate of climb keeps changing, peaking at the middle and dropping to nothing at the ends. Read as one sentence about one quantity, that is nonsense. It stops being nonsense the moment those are two different quantities.

And they can be, as soon as we stop insisting the object is stuck on a line. Suppose it moves about in a flat plane, freely, at unchanging speed. And suppose up and down is not what it is doing, but merely what we have decided to look at.

Speed

How much ground the object covers. On our machine this never changes, not by a fraction, ever.

Rate of climb

How much of that ground was upward. This changes constantly, and it depends on one thing only: which way the object is pointing.

You already know this from walking. Set off in some direction and cover one metre. How much higher are you than when you started? If you headed straight up a slope, a metre. If you walked along the contour, not at all, even though you covered the same ground and worked just as hard. Head somewhere in between and you gain something in between. Same distance covered every time. Different amounts of it turning into height.

Drag the heading below and watch exactly that. The arrow is the same length at every setting, because the distance covered never changes. The blue bar is how much of it became height.

It is our curve.

Read that slowly, because it settles everything. If an object covers ground at an unchanging rate and its heading swings steadily round, then its rate of climb follows that plot, and that plot is the shape we have been chasing since the top of the page. The coast into the stop is not arranged by anybody. It is what a steadily turning heading does.

The demands sort themselves out too. Point the object straight up and all of its travel counts as height, so the rate of climb is at its greatest, which is demand two. Point it sideways and none of it counts, so the rate of climb is zero even though the object has not slowed by a fraction, which is demand three. Neither had to be imposed on anything.

So the heading turns steadily. What path makes it do that?

The path picks itself

Three constraints, and none of them arbitrary. Each comes straight from the sound we started with.

The path has to close up on itself. Our sound repeats, forever, identically. An object wandering off along an open path would never come back to do the same thing again.

The speed has to be the same everywhere along it. That was the entire point of the last section. Allow the object to speed up or slow down and we are back to inventing rules, and the explanation collapses.

The heading has to turn at a steady rate. That is what the right-hand panel just demanded. Not merely turn, but turn evenly, because an uneven turn would draw an uneven curve.

Below are three closed paths, each walked at a completely steady speed, each with a dot showing where the object is and a trace showing nothing but its height. Two fail, and they fail in ways you can see.

Three closed paths, walked at the same steady speed
All three cover ground at the same steady rate. What differs is how their heading turns.

The square fails in the most visible way possible. Along an edge the heading does not turn at all, so the rate of climb is frozen and the trace runs dead straight. At a corner the heading jumps through ninety degrees instantly, and the trace inherits the jump. A steadily turning heading is precisely what a square does not have.

The ellipse fails more subtly, and it is the better lesson of the two. It has no corners anywhere, so its heading does turn smoothly. But it is stretched, and stretched means the heading turns unevenly: barely at all along the long flat side, then quickly round the tight ends. So the height lingers near its extremes instead of easing through them, and the peaks come out squashed.

Which is exactly the kind of near miss that makes the point. Smoothness was not enough on its own. The heading had to turn smoothly and at a constant rate, which is a much stronger requirement.

There is one closed path where covering ground at a steady speed turns the heading at a steady rate, and it is the most familiar shape there is.

Why the circle does it, in one picture

Every arrow is the same length, and that is the detail to hold on to. The speed is identical at all four positions. Nothing accelerates the object and nothing brakes it. All that changes is how much of that fixed travel is aimed upward, and going round the circle slides that amount smoothly from all of it, through none of it, to all of it the other way.

One thing is still owed. Would the honest straight-line version really have failed?

Both machines, side by side

Here they are together. On top, the circle, with only the height being watched. Below, the assumption-free straight line we set aside earlier: steady speed, flip at the ends. Both go up and down, both stay between the same limits, both repeat.

Two ways to go up and down
Both dots reach the top at the same moment and the bottom at the same moment. Everything in between is different.

The straight line slams. It arrives at the top going flat out, reverses on the spot, and leaves going flat out the other way, so the rate of climb jumps from full rise to full fall with nothing in between. That is a corner, and there is one at every peak. Press both buttons back to back and the difference is small but unmistakable: the straight line has a thin edge on it that the circle does not.

The circle coasts, exactly as demanded, and gives our curve. Not something in the family of our curve. Our curve.

A wave is what circular motion looks like from the side.

You can check this on a bicycle in about four seconds. Stick bright tape on the rim, spin the wheel, then crouch until your eye is level with the axle so the circle flattens into a line. The tape stops going round and starts bobbing, hanging at the top, hurrying through the middle. Anyone who has watched a train's connecting rod, or the pistons in a cutaway engine, has seen the same thing.

And the loose end from earlier gets tied. A tuning fork, a weight on a spring and a swinging pendulum all draw this curve, which looked like a coincidence when we met it. It is not. All three are systems where the motion eases off gradually rather than slamming, which is what the shadow of a rotation does. Precisely why those three behave that way needs more machinery, and it gets a post of its own later in the series.

Which leaves a plan that sounds ridiculous and is entirely serious. We set out to describe the shape of a sound. That shape is the side view of an object going round a circle. So stop describing the wave. Describe the circle, and the wave comes along for free.

How do you describe where a point on a circle is?

Two numbers, and they already have names

By its coordinates. That is the whole answer, and it is why this works.

Draw a circle at the origin. How big? For now it does not matter, since a bigger circle just gives a taller version of the same wiggle, and that size will turn out to be the loudness later on. So set the radius to 1 and stop thinking about it. That is the unit circle, and every point on it obeys

\[ x^{2} + y^{2} = 1 \]

Start the point at the far right and let it travel anticlockwise through an angle \(\theta\). Every value of \(\theta\) puts it somewhere definite, so both coordinates are fixed by \(\theta\) alone. Those two numbers need names, and they have had names for a long time:

\[ \cos\theta = \text{the across-ness}, \qquad \sin\theta = \text{the height} \]

The point sits at \(P(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)\). That is the definition. Not a ratio, not a triangle. A position.

That is the same sound as the pure tone from earlier, the curve is the same curve, and the only thing generating either is a dot going round a circle. The chase has closed: the yardstick shape we went looking for is the height of a rotating point.

Except something should be nagging at anyone who did trigonometry at school, and it is the right thing to be nagged by.

Sine used to be opposite over hypotenuse. Has something been quietly swapped?

Paying the debt to Class 9

Fair challenge, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a shrug, because if the old definition and the new one disagree anywhere then one of them is wrong.

Take \(\theta\) between 0 and 90 degrees, so the point is up in the top right. Drop a line straight down from it to the horizontal axis, landing at a point call it \(T\). You now have a right-angled triangle: origin, \(T\), and the point.

The old definition, sitting inside the new one

The hypotenuse of that triangle is the radius, which is 1. The side opposite \(\theta\) is the vertical one, whose length is the point's height. The adjacent side is the horizontal one, whose length is the across-ness. So

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}=\frac{\text{height}}{1}, \qquad \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}=\frac{\text{across-ness}}{1} \]

Identical. The old definition divided by a hypotenuse, and we picked a circle where the hypotenuse is 1, so the division does nothing and the ratio just is the coordinate.

So nothing was swapped. But notice which way the generosity runs. The triangle version has a hard ceiling built into it: a right-angled triangle has already spent 90 degrees on its right angle, so its other angles are stuck being acute. Ask it for \(\sin 150^\circ\) and it cannot parse the question, because no such triangle exists. The circle has no ceiling. Send the point wherever you like and its height is still its height.

That is not a technicality. Our wave goes round and round forever, spending most of its life at angles no triangle could hold. The old definition could not have described one full cycle of the sound we started with.

What else does the circle hand us that the triangle could not?

Things you can stop remembering

Here is the quiet payoff of getting a definition right, and it is worth naming as a principle: a good definition converts things you had to memorise into things you can see. Three school facts collapse the moment sine and cosine become coordinates.

Start with the one everybody has chanted. The point never leaves the circle, so its coordinates satisfy the circle's equation. We renamed \(x\) as \(\cos\theta\) and \(y\) as \(\sin\theta\), so substituting is the entire derivation:

\[ \cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1 \]

No proof by cases, no separate argument per quadrant. It is Pythagoras in different clothes, and it cannot fail, because the point was never allowed off the circle in the first place.

Second, the signs. School gives these as a mnemonic, but on the circle the sign of \(\sin\theta\) is simply whether the point is above or below the horizontal axis, and the sign of \(\cos\theta\) is whether it is right or left of the vertical one. In our story that means something physical: \(\sin\theta\) positive is the half of the cycle where the air is being squeezed, negative is the half where it is being stretched. The sign table is not a rule about angles. It is a description of the sound.

Third, run the wheel backwards, to \(-\theta\) instead of \(\theta\). The point lands as a mirror image of itself, reflected in the horizontal axis. Its across-ness is untouched and its height flips, so

\[ \cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta,\qquad \sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta \]

All three checked at once below, at angles well outside the reach of any triangle.

That last flip is worth remembering, because turning a sound upside down is precisely what noise-cancelling headphones do to the world around you. Oddly, an upside down wave played on its own sounds identical to the original. The flip only becomes audible when you add it to something, which is a later post.

So can we finally write down what an instrument is doing?

Half an answer, and two debts

Half. We can write down the pure tone completely. It is a point going round a circle 440 times a second, and the pressure at your eardrum is its height, so at time \(t\)

\[ P(t)=\sin\big(2\pi \times 440 \times t\big) \]

One line, and it is the note the whole orchestra tunes to before anybody plays a bar.

But there is a \(\pi\) in it, and nothing in this post produced a \(\pi\). It showed up in the code too, buried in a DEG2RAD macro that nobody explained. Something about counting rotations is quietly refusing to use degrees. That is the first debt, and it is the whole of post 2.

The second is worse. Every curve here was drawn by a machine. Nothing in this post lets you work out \(\sin 30^\circ\) by hand, and the list of angles anyone can write down exactly turns out to be startlingly short. That is post 3.

Which leaves the violin. We opened with it and have not explained it, so it would be fair to feel short-changed. Here is the reason not to be. Take the pure tone and start adding more pure tones on top, each at a whole-number multiple of the first frequency. Watch the shape, and listen.

The repeat rate has not moved, so it is still an A. But the character has, and by the time a few are stacked, the shape is recognisably the jagged one the violin had at the top of this page.

That is the answer to the opening question, in outline. An instrument's identity is a recipe of pure tones. Every tool needed to justify that sentence, to say why those particular frequencies, to work out what happens when waves are added, to prove that any repeating shape can be built this way, is what the rest of this series is for.

the thread
Same repeat rate and same loudness leaves only the shape of one repeat. Reading the motion off that shape gives three demands, and the third is impossible in a straight line, because there the rate of climb and the speed are the same quantity. Split them, by letting the object move in a plane and watching only its height, and the rate of climb becomes the share of travel that happens to be going upward. Turn the heading steadily and that share traces our curve. The one closed path where a steady speed turns the heading steadily is a circle.

What to carry out of this one

A sound is air pressure rising and falling, and a note is that pattern repeating. Match two sounds for repeat rate and volume and the only thing left to differ is the shape of a single repeat, which is what your ear was reading all along.

The simplest such shape is the one a tuning fork, a spring and a pendulum all produce, and three unrelated systems agreeing is the clue that it belongs to motion rather than to sound. Reading the motion off it gives three demands, and the third, the gentle turnaround, cannot be met in one dimension without inventing a rule by hand.

It can be met for free the moment you stop conflating two different quantities. How much ground the object covers is one thing. How much of that ground was upward is another, and it depends only on the heading. Keep the first constant and turn the second steadily, and the rate of climb traces our curve on its own. The only closed path that does that is a circle.

So put a point on a circle of radius 1 and turn it through \(\theta\). Its across-ness is \(\cos\theta\) and its height is \(\sin\theta\), and that is the definition. The Class 9 version is the same thing with the hypotenuse set to 1, so nothing was discarded, it simply stopped being trapped in the first quadrant. After that, \(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1\) is the circle's own equation, each sign is just which side of an axis the point is on, and reversing the spin leaves the across-ness alone while flipping the height.

A wave is circular motion seen from the side, and the reason is that going round a circle holds the speed fixed while sliding the heading smoothly from straight up to sideways. How much of the travel becomes height therefore rises and falls all by itself. Name the two coordinates of that rotating point and they are \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\), which is why \(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1\) needs no proof and why the triangle definition was only ever the first quadrant.

Next: the \(\pi\) we could not account for. Degrees turn out to be an accident of Babylonian bookkeeping, and there is a way of measuring turn that the circle hands you for nothing, which is where that \(\pi\) has been hiding all along.

Two words to keep apart

Speed is how much ground the object covers. On a circle this never changes.

Rate of climb is how much of that ground was upward. It changes constantly, and only because the heading does.

In one dimension these are the same number, which is exactly why one dimension cannot produce our curve.
