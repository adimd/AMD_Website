Architecting Intelligence: Building LLMs From First Principle

What the Dot Product Really Measures

How center and context words recognize each other in embedding space

Taking Stock Before We Begin

Before we move forward, it is worth pausing to be explicit about where we now stand. Over the previous chapters, we have reshaped the problem of meaning step by step. We began with co-occurrence tables and saw why they were conceptually right but practically limited. We then watched meaning migrate from explicit storage into geometry. Finally, we arrived at a representation in which words live as vectors, and relationships are expressed through their relative positions.

At this point, several things should feel settled. Meaning is not stored in cells. It is not retrieved from a table. It is encoded implicitly in the structure of a space. Words with similar histories end up close to one another. Words with different histories separate. Learning happens gradually, through accumulation, rather than through any single defining example.

We also made an important distinction. Each word carries two representations. A center vector that reflects how the word behaves when it is the focus of attention, and a context vector that reflects how it behaves when it appears in the neighborhood of another word. Together, these vectors allow us to reconstruct the relational structure that a V by V table once tried to store explicitly.

We now understand what these vectors represent. What we have not yet answered is how to find them.

This is the gap that remains. We have a representation, but no procedure. We know that the vectors must end up arranged in a way that reflects language, but we have not yet said how that arrangement comes about. We have talked about forces, pressures, and gradual movement, but we have not yet written down the rules that govern those movements.

The goal of this next stage is therefore precise. We want to determine values for the center vectors and context vectors that best reflect the structure of language as observed through text. Not perfect values. Not final values. But values that make the interactions between vectors align with what we see in real usage.

Our objective is to find good values for the vectors \( V \) and \( C \).

Everything that follows builds toward this objective. We will ask what it means for one set of vectors to be better than another. We will ask how individual word encounters should influence vector positions. We will ask how local interactions accumulate into global structure.

In short, we are moving from representation to learning. The geometry is already in place. The task now is to understand how it is shaped.

What Does It Mean for One Set of Vectors to Be Better Than Another

If our objective is to find good values for the center vectors \( V \) and the context vectors \( C \), then we need a precise way to judge one choice of vectors against another. Rather than reasoning abstractly, we will ground this discussion in a very small and explicit text corpus.

Consider the following four sentences.

I love my dog.
You love my dog.
I love my cat.
You love my cat.

This corpus is tiny, but it already contains structure. The words “dog” and “cat” appear in exactly the same contexts. The words “I” and “you” also behave symmetrically. The word “love” appears repeatedly between a pronoun and the word “my”. Any reasonable choice of vectors should reflect these regularities.

Recall how our representation works. Each word \( w \) is assigned two vectors. A center vector \( \mathbf{v}_w \in \mathbb{R}^d \), and a context vector \( \mathbf{c}_w \in \mathbb{R}^d \). When a word appears as the center and another appears in its context, we compute their compatibility using a dot product.

\( s(w_i, w_j) = \mathbf{v}_{w_i}^\top \mathbf{c}_{w_j} \)

To make this concrete, let us choose a very small dimension, \( d = 2 \), and examine two different candidate choices for the vectors.

A First Candidate Geometry

Suppose we choose the following center vector for the word “love”.

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \)

Now suppose the context vectors for the words “my”, “dog”, and “cat” are chosen as follows.

\( \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_{\text{dog}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_{\text{cat}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \)

We can now compute the dot products that arise from observed word pairs in the corpus.

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 = 3 \)
\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{dog}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 = 2 \)
\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{cat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 = 2 \)

All of these correspond to word pairs that actually occur in the corpus. Their dot products are relatively large and clearly separated from zero.

Now consider an unlikely pairing, such as “love” appearing with “you” as context. Suppose the context vector for “you” is

\( \mathbf{c}_{\text{you}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \)

The resulting dot product is

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{you}} = 2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 0 = -2 \)

In this first geometry, observed pairs produce larger dot products than unobserved ones. The vectors reflect the structure present in the text.

This geometry makes observed word relationships numerically stronger than unlikely ones.

A Second Candidate Geometry

Now consider a different choice of vectors. Suppose we instead choose

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \)

and context vectors

\( \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_{\text{dog}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{c}_{\text{you}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \)

In this case, all dot products become identical.

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} = 1 \)
\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{dog}} = 1 \)
\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{you}} = 1 \)

This geometry makes no distinction between observed and unobserved word pairs. Although the vectors are perfectly valid numerically, they fail to encode the structure of the corpus.

Not every choice of vectors is equally meaningful, even if dot products can be computed.

We can now state precisely what “better” means. A better set of vectors is one whose dot products systematically assign higher values to word pairs supported by the corpus than to those that are not. The vectors themselves are judged by the behavior they induce when they interact.

Once this criterion is clear, the learning problem becomes well defined. We need a way to adjust the vectors so that dot products for observed pairs increase relative to dot products for unlikely ones. How that adjustment happens is the next step in the story.

Why the Dot Product, and Why Center and Context Are Not the Same

Before we go any further, we need to pause and make one design choice fully explicit. So far, we have said that words interact through a dot product between a center vector and a context vector. This choice might look arbitrary at first glance. Why this operation. Why not something else. And why do we need two different representations for the same word at all.

To answer these questions honestly, we need to return to the way language appears in text. Words do not exist in isolation. They appear in sequences, and those sequences impose roles. At any moment, one word is the focus of attention, while others form its surrounding context. These roles are not interchangeable.

Consider again the sentence “I love my dog”. When we focus on the word “love”, it plays a very different role than the word “my”. “Love” is the word being explained by its neighbors. “My” helps explain something else. Even though both are words in the same sentence, their contributions are asymmetric.

A word behaves differently when it is being described than when it is providing description.

This asymmetry is the reason we assign two representations to each word. The center vector \( \mathbf{v}_w \) captures how a word behaves when it is the focal word. The context vector \( \mathbf{c}_w \) captures how the same word behaves when it appears in the neighborhood of another word. These are not redundant views. They encode different kinds of information.

Now that we have two vectors, we need a way to combine them. This combination must satisfy a few basic requirements. It should produce a single number that reflects how compatible a center word is with a context word. It should increase smoothly when the vectors become more aligned. And it should decrease smoothly when they become misaligned.

The dot product does exactly this.

\( \mathbf{v}_{w_i}^\top \mathbf{c}_{w_j} = \|\mathbf{v}_{w_i}\| \|\mathbf{c}_{w_j}\| \cos \theta \)

This expression reveals why the dot product is so useful. It responds to both direction and magnitude. If the center and context vectors point in similar directions, the value is large. If they point in opposite directions, the value is small or negative. If they are orthogonal, the value is near zero. Alignment becomes a measurable signal.

From the perspective of learning, this is exactly what we want. When a word appears frequently in a particular context, repeated updates will gradually align their vectors. The dot product grows naturally as a consequence of shared experience. No explicit rule is needed to force similarity. Geometry does the work.

The dot product turns geometric alignment into a numerical signal of compatibility.

There is another important reason the dot product matters. It is linear. Small changes in either vector produce small changes in the score. This smoothness is essential for learning. It allows us to adjust vectors incrementally, based on individual word encounters, without causing instability.

Most importantly, the dot product preserves the relational idea we started with. Each interaction score is still an estimate of how strongly two words are related in a specific role configuration. The difference is that the relationship is no longer stored explicitly. It is generated dynamically from the vectors themselves.

By choosing separate center and context representations and combining them with a dot product, we arrive at a mechanism that is simple, expressive, and learnable. It respects the asymmetry of language, produces meaningful numerical signals, and allows geometry to emerge from data rather than being imposed by hand.

The dot product is not a trick. It is the simplest operation that makes learning geometry possible.

With this choice justified, we are now ready to make the idea concrete. The next step is to see how these dot products can be encouraged or discouraged based on what we observe in text. That is where learning rules finally enter the picture.

Why Alignment Happens Between Center and Context, Not Between Similar Words

A common misunderstanding about word vectors is the belief that similar words are pulled directly toward each other during learning. This intuition feels natural, but it is not how the mechanism actually works.

Vectors do not align because words are similar. Words become similar because of how their vectors align with contexts.

Alignment does not happen between words. It happens between a center word and its context.

Language appears as sequences. At each position, one word plays the role of the focal word, while surrounding words provide context. These roles are asymmetric, and the learning signal respects that asymmetry.

In the sentence “I love my dog”, when “love” is the center, the words “I”, “my”, and “dog” act as context. The learning signal compares the center representation of “love” with the context representations of its neighbors. It never compares “love” and “dog” as equal objects.

This is why each word has two vectors. The center vector captures how a word behaves when it is being explained. The context vector captures how the same word behaves when it helps explain something else.

When a center word repeatedly appears with certain context words, their corresponding vectors are nudged to align. Words like “dog” and “cat” are never pushed toward each other directly. Instead, they are pushed toward many of the same context vectors, such as “my”, “love”, and “you”. Because the pressures they experience are similar, they end up close in the space.

Words become similar because they align with similar contexts, not because they align with each other.

Clustering, then, is not something we program explicitly. It is a side effect of repeated center–context interactions. Similarity is not imposed during learning. It is what we observe once learning has taken place.

Interpreting the Dot Product as a Score

At this point, the role of the dot product should feel natural. We use it to combine a center vector and a context vector into a single number. What we have not yet done is give this number a clear interpretation.

From here on, we will refer to the dot product between a center vector and a context vector as a score.

\( \text{score}(w_i, w_j) = \mathbf{v}_{w_i}^\top \mathbf{c}_{w_j} \)

This score is our model’s internal assessment of how compatible a center word \( w_i \) is with a context word \( w_j \) in the role they appear. It is not a probability. It is not a count. It is a raw signal that reflects how strongly the current geometry believes these two words belong together in that configuration.

The interpretation is simple. A higher score means the model believes the pairing is plausible and well supported by experience. A lower score means the model believes the pairing is unlikely or weakly supported. A score near zero indicates uncertainty or lack of evidence.

High score means strong compatibility. Low score means weak compatibility.

This interpretation allows us to restate our objective in a more precise way. Choosing the right vectors is not an abstract geometric problem. It is a problem of calibrating scores so that they agree with reality.

When a word pair appears frequently in text, we want its score to be high. When a word pair rarely or never appears, we want its score to be low. If the scores produced by our vectors match this pattern, then the vectors are doing their job.

Seen this way, learning becomes indirect but clear. We are not adjusting vectors for their own sake. We are adjusting vectors so that the scores they produce line up with the structure of language as revealed by data.

Learning vectors is equivalent to tuning scores so that they reflect what is true in text.

This framing is important because it shifts our focus. We do not need to reason about vector coordinates directly. We only need to reason about whether scores are too high, too low, or about right. The vectors will move accordingly.

With this interpretation in place, the next step becomes inevitable. We need a way to decide how wrong a score is, and how strongly that wrongness should influence the vectors. That is where explicit learning objectives finally enter the picture.

When Is a Score Actually Good or Bad

So far, the idea of a score has served us well. We take a center vector and a context vector, compute their dot product, and interpret the result as a measure of compatibility. Higher scores feel better. Lower scores feel worse. This intuition is useful, but it is also incomplete.

At this point, it is worth stopping and asking a simple but uncomfortable question. Suppose we compute a score for a word pair and obtain a value of 2.3. Is that good or bad. Suppose we obtain a score of 0.1. Is that acceptable. Suppose we obtain a negative score. What does that mean, exactly.

The problem is not with the dot product itself. The problem is that, by itself, the score has no natural reference point. A dot product is just a number. Its scale depends on the lengths of the vectors and their orientation. Without additional structure, there is no clear boundary that tells us when a score is sufficiently high or unacceptably low.

A score tells us how strong a relationship is, but not whether that strength is good enough.

This becomes especially clear when we compare scores across different word pairs. A score of 1.5 might be high for one center word and low for another. A score that seems reasonable early in learning might become inadequate later. The number itself does not carry an absolute meaning.

This reveals a deeper limitation. While the dot product gives us a way to rank word pairs, it does not give us a way to judge correctness. It can tell us that one pairing scores higher than another, but it cannot tell us whether either score is aligned with the truth we observe in text.

In other words, we have a signal, but we do not yet have a metric. We can compute scores, but we cannot yet say whether a particular score represents success or failure.

The dot product produces a score. It does not yet tell us how to judge that score.

This is the gap we need to close next. Our objective is no longer just to produce scores. It is to transform those scores into something we can evaluate. We need a way to look at a score and decide whether it agrees with what actually happens in language.

Once we have such a metric, learning becomes possible in a precise sense. We will be able to say how wrong a score is, not just that it exists. And once we can measure wrongness, we can use it to guide how vectors should move.

The next step, then, is clear. We must convert raw scores into a form that allows us to distinguish good from bad. That conversion is what turns scoring into learning.

A necessary clarification before we move on.

It is important to be explicit about one thing at this stage. Even though we have defined scores, interpreted them, and discussed what it would mean for those scores to be good or bad, the vectors we have used so far are still arbitrary. They were chosen only to illustrate ideas. They are not yet grounded in data.

In other words, the geometry we have been reasoning about is still untrained. The center and context vectors that produce these scores are, at this point, effectively random. The fact that we can compute scores does not mean those scores are meaningful.

This is exactly why learning is required. The end goal is not to accept the scores produced by arbitrary vectors, but to adjust the vectors so that the scores they produce align with what we observe in real text. The geometry must be shaped by data, not assumed in advance.

Everything that follows is about closing this gap. We will move from random vectors to structured representations, from uncalibrated scores to scores that reflect ground truth. That transition is the core task of learning.
