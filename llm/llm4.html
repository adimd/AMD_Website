Rewinding the Story: What Co-occurrence Was Really Telling Us

Before we talk about learning, models, or embeddings, it is worth slowing down and rewinding the story with care. Not to repeat what we already know, but to understand it more honestly. In the previous chapter, we deliberately placed ourselves in a world that felt almost unrealistically small. The vocabulary was tiny. The sentences were few. Everything was carefully constrained.

At first glance, this setup looked like a toy problem, something designed only to illustrate an idea. And yet, that very smallness made something visible that usually disappears once scale takes over. When nothing is hidden behind data volume, structure becomes easier to see.

We did not teach the system what any word meant. There were no labels, no definitions, and no categories. There was no model to train, no loss to minimize, and no optimization process running in the background. All we did was count. We counted which words appeared near which other words and recorded those proximities. Nothing more.

And still, structure emerged.

Words like “dog” and “cat” began to resemble each other in behavior. “I” and “you” started acting as if they played similar roles in language. “My” appeared repeatedly next to nouns, while “love” tended to appear between entities and relationships, behaving less like a thing and more like a connector. None of this was programmed explicitly. It emerged because language itself is structured, and repeated use leaves behind patterns.

Language carries structure within itself, and repeated usage makes that structure visible.

Long before machine learning existed, linguists captured this idea in a simple observation. A word is known by the company it keeps. What we saw in the co-occurrence table was that idea made concrete. Meaning did not arrive from external definitions. It emerged from regularities in how words were used.

This perspective changes how we think about meaning. Words do not carry meaning in isolation. They acquire meaning through interaction, through repetition, and through the roles they play relative to other words. If this is true, then capturing relationships between words is not a crude approximation. It is the most direct representation of meaning we can hope for.

A vocabulary by vocabulary table, often written as a V by V matrix, is an explicit attempt to store those relationships. Each row describes how a word relates to every other word in the language. If two rows look similar, the words tend to appear in similar contexts. If they look different, their usage, and therefore their meaning, diverges.

At this stage, nothing feels broken. In fact, the idea feels almost complete.

The co-occurrence matrix is not naive. It is conceptually correct.

The real question this blog exists to answer is not whether the idea of V by V makes sense. The question is whether storing that idea explicitly is the right way to carry it forward.

Co-occurrence reveals relational structure, but it fixes that structure into a static form.

When the V by V Idea Meets the Real World

So far, nothing about a vocabulary by vocabulary table feels unreasonable. If meaning truly lives in relationships between words, then storing all word relationships feels like the most honest thing we could do. In a small and controlled setting, the co-occurrence matrix even feels elegant. It is direct, interpretable, and grounded in real usage.

The difficulty appears only when we allow language to be as large and unruly as it really is. Real languages do not consist of a few dozen words. They contain tens of thousands, sometimes hundreds of thousands, depending on how vocabulary is defined. The moment the vocabulary grows, the table grows with it.

The growth is not linear. It is quadratic.

A vocabulary of size V implies V by V possible word relationships. Even a vocabulary of one hundred thousand words implies ten billion potential entries. Most of these relationships will never matter. Most word pairs will never appear together in any meaningful way. And yet, the table must still account for them simply because they are theoretically possible.

The table grows for every possible relationship, not only the meaningful ones.

We can compress the table. We can store it sparsely. We can distribute it across machines. These techniques help with storage, but they do not change the nature of the object itself. They reduce the pain without addressing the cause.

The deeper problem is rigidity. Once a co-occurrence matrix is built, it is effectively frozen. It reflects the past exactly as it was counted, but it has no natural way to revise itself. When new sentences arrive, the table must be rebuilt or patched. When earlier assumptions turn out to be misleading, the table cannot correct them. It simply accumulates more counts.

To see what this rigidity really means, consider a single word like “apple”. In an older corpus, the word “apple” appears overwhelmingly next to words like “tree”, “fruit”, “orchard”, “pie”, and “harvest”. A co-occurrence table built from that data faithfully records those relationships. Now imagine that new text begins to arrive. People start writing about “Apple” alongside words like “phone”, “software”, “device”, “store”, and “update”. From a human perspective, something important has changed. The dominant sense of the word has shifted.

The co-occurrence matrix, however, has no notion of shift or revision. It does not know that an earlier interpretation has become less representative. It simply adds new counts on top of old ones. The associations with fruit remain, the associations with technology grow, and both are blended together in the same row. The table cannot decide which relationships are now more relevant. It cannot downplay older contexts or emphasize newer ones. It only accumulates.

What this means in practice is that the matrix preserves history, not understanding. It records everything that ever happened, but it cannot adapt its representation to reflect how meaning evolves over time. Once written down, past usage has the same weight as present usage, even when that is no longer appropriate.

This is where the mismatch with learning becomes unavoidable. Human understanding is not a static summary of experience. It evolves continuously as new contexts are encountered and old ones are reinterpreted. A frozen table can record relationships, but it cannot refine them.

The V by V table captures relationships, but it cannot adapt as understanding evolves.

And yet, abandoning the V by V idea would be a mistake. Word relationships are not the wrong abstraction. They are exactly the right abstraction. The problem lies in insisting that all of those relationships be written down explicitly and preserved forever.

The table grows quadratically even though meaningful relationships remain sparse.

Looking Beneath the Matrix: What the V by V Table Is Really Made Of

To move forward, we need to stop treating the co-occurrence table as a finished object and ask a simpler question. Where did it come from. A V by V matrix feels solid once written down, but it is not fundamental. It is a summary.

Language does not arrive as a table. It arrives as sentences. Sentences arrive as sequences of words. As words pass by one another, they form small neighborhoods. Each encounter is minor on its own, but together they accumulate.

Seen this way, the matrix becomes less mysterious. Every cell answers a specific historical question. How many times did these two words appear near each other in the past. The matrix does not reason. It does not infer. It remembers.

The co-occurrence matrix is accumulated experience, not learned understanding.

By building the table, we made an implicit choice. We chose to forget individual encounters and keep only their totals. We collapsed time. We erased order. We replaced experience with a static snapshot. That snapshot is useful, but once the events are gone, the table has nothing left to learn from.

This reframing opens a different possibility. If the table is nothing more than the sum of events, then perhaps the table itself is not what we should be storing. Perhaps learning belongs earlier, at the level of the events themselves.

Instead of asking how many times two words have co-occurred in total, we can ask what should happen each time they do. Learning becomes continuous rather than retrospective.

Keeping the V by V Idea Without Storing the V by V Table

Once we stop thinking of language as a completed table and start thinking of it as a stream of word encounters, a different representation begins to suggest itself. The original goal has not changed. We still want to capture how words relate to other words. What changes is how we choose to store that information.

A V by V co-occurrence table attempts to store a value for every possible pair of words in the vocabulary. For a vocabulary of size V, each word is represented by a row containing V numbers. Conceptually, this row answers a single question. Given this word, how does it relate to every other word that might appear near it.

Instead of storing this row explicitly, we can ask a more structural question. Is it possible to represent each word in a way that allows these relationships to be reconstructed when needed. In other words, can we replace explicit storage with a system that produces the same information through interaction.

The goal is to preserve the relational idea of V by V, while changing the form in which it is stored.

To see how this becomes possible, we need to look more carefully at what actually happens in text. Language arrives one sentence at a time. Each sentence creates a small local structure. Words do not interact with all other words at once. They interact only with the words that appear nearby.

Consider a simple sentence from our toy corpus. “I love my dog”. This sentence produces a sequence of local encounters. The word “love” appears between “I” and “my”. The word “my” appears between “love” and “dog”. The word “I” appears next to only one neighbor, “love”. The word “dog” also appears next to only one neighbor, “my”.

Words participate in different local neighborhoods depending on their position in the sentence.

Already, an important asymmetry appears. The word “love” participates in two relationships within this sentence. It connects to “I” on one side and to “my” on the other. The word “I”, by contrast, participates in only one relationship. Its entire local context is defined by its interaction with “love”.

This difference matters. Even within a single sentence, words do not contribute equally to context. Some words sit at the center of a local structure. Others appear at the boundary. A single row in a V by V table treats all of these roles as if they were the same.

A word does not play the same role when it is surrounded by context as when it provides context to another word.

Once we recognize this asymmetry, a natural mathematical distinction follows. Each word needs two representations. One representation should describe how the word behaves when it is the center of attention. The other should describe how the word behaves when it appears as context for another word.

We make this distinction explicit by assigning two vectors to every word w in the vocabulary.

The first vector is the center representation:

\( \mathbf{v}_w \in \mathbb{R}^d \)

This vector describes how the word behaves when it is the word we are trying to model or predict.

The second vector is the context representation:

\( \mathbf{c}_w \in \mathbb{R}^d \)

This vector describes how the same word behaves when it appears in the neighborhood of another word.

Each word is represented differently depending on whether it is a center or context.

Collecting these vectors across the vocabulary gives us two matrices. One matrix contains all center vectors and has shape V by d.

\( V = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v}_{w_1} \\ \mathbf{v}_{w_2} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{v}_{w_V} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d} \)

The second matrix contains all context vectors and has shape d by V.

\( C = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{c}_{w_1} & \mathbf{c}_{w_2} & \cdots & \mathbf{c}_{w_V} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times V} \)

Instead of storing V by V relationships directly, we store two V by d structures that can generate them.

When a word appears next to another word in text, we no longer look up a stored count. We take the center vector of the first word and the context vector of the second word and let them interact. That interaction produces a number that plays the same conceptual role as an entry in the co-occurrence table.

As sentences accumulate, these vectors are adjusted so that words which consistently appear in similar neighborhoods move closer together in this space. The global relational structure that once lived explicitly in a V by V table now lives implicitly in the geometry of the vectors.

The table has not been discarded. It has been internalized. Word relationships are no longer frozen as counts. They are expressed as interactions between representations that can shift as language evolves.

A Concrete Example: Reconstructing a Co-occurrence Entry Using V and C

Let us now see explicitly how the vector representation recreates the same idea that the co-occurrence matrix was storing as a fixed number. We will follow a single word pair from text to representation, step by step.

Consider again the sentence “I love my dog”. From the perspective of co-occurrence, this sentence provides evidence that the word “love” is related to the word “my”. In a V by V table, this would correspond to increasing the entry associated with the ordered pair (love, my).

In the vector-based representation, we do not consult a table. Instead, we work with two vectors drawn from two different matrices. The word “love” appears as the center of attention, so we use its center vector from the matrix V. The word “my” appears in the local neighborhood, so we use its context vector from the matrix C.

\( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}} \in \mathbb{R}^d \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} \in \mathbb{R}^d \)

To quantify how compatible these two words are in this specific role configuration, we combine their vectors using an inner product. This produces a single scalar value.

\( s(\text{love}, \text{my}) = \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}}^\top \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} \)

This scalar plays the same conceptual role as an entry in the co-occurrence matrix. A larger value indicates that “love” frequently appears with “my” in this directional relationship. A smaller value indicates a weaker association.

Now consider what happens as we process many sentences. Each time the pair (love, my) appears in text, the same two vectors interact again. The learning process makes small adjustments so that the inner product between \( \mathbf{v}_{\text{love}} \) and \( \mathbf{c}_{\text{my}} \) increases when this pairing is common and decreases when it is rare.

Over time, this inner product comes to reflect exactly what the corresponding co-occurrence count was capturing, but in a very different form. The information is no longer stored as a static number in a table. It is encoded in the relative alignment of two vectors.

A co-occurrence value is no longer retrieved from storage. It is generated dynamically by interaction between a center vector and a context vector.

If we extend this idea across the entire vocabulary, every potential entry of the V by V table can be approximated implicitly. Each ordered word pair corresponds to an interaction between a row of V and a row of C.

\( \text{implicit co-occurrence}(w_i, w_j) \approx \mathbf{v}_{w_i}^\top \mathbf{c}_{w_j} \)

Instead of storing V squared numbers explicitly, we store two collections of V vectors. The full table still exists conceptually, but it is never written down. It lives in the geometry of the vector space.

This is the essential shift. Counting records what happened in the past. Vector interaction creates a structure that can adapt as new text arrives, while preserving the relational idea that motivated the co-occurrence matrix in the first place.

Geometry as Memory

At this stage, something fundamental has changed in how meaning is represented. We are no longer storing information in a table. There are no cells to index and no fixed entries to retrieve. And yet, the relational structure that once lived inside the co-occurrence matrix has not disappeared. It has simply taken on a different form.

Meaning is no longer stored as explicit numbers. It is stored as geometry.

Each word now exists as a point in a shared space. Its position is not chosen arbitrarily. It is shaped by every context in which that word has appeared and every role it has played relative to other words. The history of a word is no longer summarized by a row of counts. It is encoded in where that word settles in the space.

Meaning is no longer stored in cells. It is stored in relative positions.

This change also transforms the nature of memory. In a table, memory is literal. A number sits in a location and stays there unless it is explicitly overwritten. In a geometric representation, memory is distributed. It lives in relationships. Moving one vector slightly changes how it relates to many others at the same time.

To understand why this representation works so well, we need to look closely at how vectors are shaped over time. Every time a word appears in a sentence, it experiences a small adjustment. That adjustment is driven by the surrounding words and by the role the word is playing in that moment. No single update is meaningful on its own. What matters is the accumulation.

Why Similar Words End Up Close

Words that appear in similar contexts are exposed to similar patterns of adjustment. When two words tend to occur in the same kinds of neighborhoods, their vectors are nudged in similar directions again and again. Over time, these shared pressures cause their positions in the space to drift closer together.

This is not something we explicitly instruct the system to do. There is no rule that says similar words must be close. Closeness emerges as a consequence of shared experience.

Each update can be thought of as a small push. One sentence produces one push. Another sentence produces another. When the pushes are consistently aligned, the result is motion in a common direction. Geometry begins to reflect the average of many experiences rather than any single one.

Clustering is not programmed. It is an inevitable consequence of shared context.

Words with different histories experience different pressures. Their updates pull them in different directions. Over time, this leads to separation in the space. Distance becomes a record of difference in usage, not a manually imposed distinction.

This is why geometry is such a powerful carrier of meaning. It compresses a vast number of individual events into a stable spatial arrangement. Similar histories produce proximity. Different histories produce separation.

Similar histories lead to similar vectors. Different histories lead to separation in space.

Learning as Accumulation, Not Revelation

Another important consequence of this view is how it reframes learning itself. No single sentence defines what a word means. A word does not acquire meaning the first time it appears. Meaning emerges slowly, through repeated exposure to varied contexts.

Each sentence contributes a small amount of evidence. One usage nudges a vector slightly. Another usage nudges it again. Over time, these nudges accumulate into a stable position. Learning, in this sense, is not about discovering a hidden definition. It is about integrating experience.

This mirrors how humans learn language. A child does not hear the word “dog” once and fully understand it. The word appears in many situations. With different dogs. In different sentences. Across time, a coherent concept forms. Meaning is not revealed all at once. It is built gradually.

The geometric representation reflects this process naturally. It does not require a moment of insight. It does not rely on explicit instruction. It simply accumulates experience and lets structure emerge.

Learning does not happen in a single step. It happens through the slow accumulation of experience.

This is what gives the representation its plausibility. It behaves like a memory system that learns the way humans do. It forgets nothing instantly, but it also does not cling rigidly to the past. New experiences reshape the space gently, preserving stability while allowing adaptation.

Geometry reflects the accumulated average of many small linguistic experiences.

Seen together, these ideas explain why vector representations feel so natural. They do not encode meaning explicitly. They allow meaning to emerge. Geometry becomes memory, and memory becomes structure. What began as a replacement for a table becomes a model of learning itself.

Where We Go Next

At this point, the core idea should feel clear. We have seen why explicit tables struggle to scale and adapt. We have seen how word relationships can be preserved without ever storing a V by V matrix. We have seen how meaning migrates from cells into geometry, and how memory takes the form of relative position rather than fixed entries.

Words are no longer defined by lists of counts. They are defined by where they settle in a space shaped by experience. Similar histories produce proximity. Different histories produce separation. Learning emerges as accumulation, not revelation.

The idea is now in place. What remains is to learn how to find these vectors.

In the chapters that follow, we will begin that journey. We will move from intuition to procedure. We will ask what forces shape these vectors, how interactions turn into learning, and how simple mathematical operations give rise to rich structure.

Nothing new will be assumed. Each step will build directly on what we already understand. The geometry is waiting. The question now is how to arrive there.
