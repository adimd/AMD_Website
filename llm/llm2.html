From Characters to Concepts: Why Text Alone Isn’t Enough

How raw text representation evolved into structured meaning and why that matters for learning systems

What we actually achieved last time

By the end of the last piece, text had finally made it into machines, not in a metaphorical or aspirational sense, but in a very literal one. Characters had stable numerical identities. Those numbers could be written to memory, copied across systems, transmitted over networks, and recovered intact on the other side. Unicode closed a long and fragile chapter that began with telegraphs, ad-hoc encodings, and documents that quietly broke as soon as they moved. For the first time, text could exist inside machines without constantly threatening to fall apart.

This was not a cosmetic improvement. It solved a real and persistent problem. Text stopped breaking. Files survived migration. Languages could coexist inside the same system without colliding into noise. Computing finally had a stable substrate for written language, and it is difficult to overstate how much modern software depends on that quiet success.

But precision matters here, because it is easy to mistake what was achieved for something larger than it actually was. What we built was a way for machines to hold text reliably, not a way for machines to work with it in any meaningful sense. Representation was solved. Engagement was not.

Text became representable. Nothing became meaningful.

What these numbers do and do not contain

The numbers assigned by Unicode are identifiers. They are references into a table that everyone agrees on, and that agreement is the entire point. The number for a character is stable, global, and unambiguous. Wherever it appears, it points to the same symbol. This is exactly why Unicode works so well at what it was designed to do. It removes disagreement. It removes ambiguity at the level of representation.

What these numbers do not contain is just as important. They carry no internal structure. The number for one character has no awareness of the number next to it. A sequence of numbers does not know it forms a word. A word does not know it participates in a sentence. There is no notion of similarity, distance, or relationship embedded in the representation itself. From the machine’s perspective, these numbers are isolated points with no geometry connecting them.

This absence is not an oversight. It is a deliberate design choice. Unicode was never meant to express relationships or meaning. It was meant to prevent disagreement. Once you see that, its limitations stop looking like failures and start looking like boundaries.

Why learning changes the problem

As long as machines were only asked to store, transmit, and display text, these boundaries did not matter. Unicode did its job, and it did it well. But learning systems ask a fundamentally different kind of question. They are not asked to preserve symbols. They are asked to detect patterns. They are asked to notice what repeats, what varies, and what behaves similarly across different contexts.

Learning systems adjust themselves based on regularities in data. They respond to proximity, to repetition, to things that cluster together in behaviour rather than appearance. All of these ideas quietly assume the existence of structure. They assume that the representation already lives in a space where similarity is even possible.

Learning does not operate on labels. It operates on relationships.

Once this is made explicit, the problem becomes difficult to ignore. If all a system ever sees is a flat sequence of unrelated integers, there is no natural place for similarity to come from. There is no notion of “close” or “far,” no gradient of resemblance, no geometry for patterns to inhabit. There is only equality or inequality. Everything else has to be inferred indirectly, through long exposure and statistical coincidence.

The question we can no longer avoid

Turning text into numbers was necessary. It was never sufficient. If a machine is going to learn anything from text, those numbers must live in a space where relationships are even possible. A space where patterns can form naturally, where similarity is something the system can respond to directly instead of something it has to invent from scratch.

Meaning does not arrive as a definition. It arrives as structure.

Before trying to construct such a space for language, it helps to look at a case where this idea is unavoidable rather than abstract. A case where numbers do not merely label things, but already live inside a geometry. A case where similarity is not a metaphor, but a measurable quantity.

Why we start with images

Images give us a starting point that text never had. Not because they are simpler, but because they already live inside a structure that learning systems understand. An image is not a symbol. It is a field of numbers laid out in space. Even before learning begins, geometry is already present.

To make this concrete, let us look at a real dataset that almost everyone in machine learning has encountered at some point: MNIST.

MNIST dataset samples

A classic snapshot: different people writing the same digits, with just enough variation to remind us that “the same class” is never literally the same pixels.

Nothing semantic has happened yet. There is no concept of “three” or “eight” inside the machine. All that exists are pixel intensities. But those intensities already encode structure. Two handwritten threes tend to activate similar regions of the image. A three and an eight do not.

This is the crucial difference from text. The similarity is not something we must invent. It is already there, waiting to be measured.

Images as vectors, not pictures

Each MNIST image is a 28×28 grid of grayscale values. If we simply read those values row by row, we obtain a vector with 784 components. Nothing clever. No learning. Just bookkeeping.

A handwritten digit is just a grid of numbers. Flatten the grid and you get a single vector — a point in a 784-dimensional space.

At this point, something profound but quiet has happened. The image is no longer a picture. It is a location. A point in space. And once things live in space, geometry applies automatically.

Two images that look similar to us tend to land near each other in this space. Images that look different tend to land far apart. No labels are required for this to be true.

What similarity actually means mathematically

Once an image is flattened, it is no longer a “picture” to the machine. It is a vector. For MNIST, that means a point \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{784}\), where each coordinate is a pixel intensity (after whatever normalisation you choose). At that moment, the question “do these two digits look alike?” becomes a purely geometric question: how close are two points in \(\mathbb{R}^{784}\), and how aligned are their directions?

There are two similarity notions that show up everywhere because they capture two different intuitions:

(1) Euclidean distance. Given two image vectors \(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{784}\), their pixel-level discrepancy is

\[ d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \;=\; \lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y} \rVert_2 \;=\; \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{784} (x_i - y_i)^2 }. \]

This is the most literal notion of similarity: if \(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\) is small, then the two images agree on most pixels, most of the time. But it is also fragile. A small shift in stroke position, a thicker pen, or a global brightness change can move many coordinates and inflate the distance, even when a human would still confidently say “same digit”.

(2) Cosine similarity. Cosine similarity focuses not on raw pixel values, but on the direction of the vectors. It is defined by

\[ \mathrm{cosine}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert_2 \,\lVert \mathbf{y} \rVert_2 }. \]

This formula is doing something extremely clean:

  • \(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y} = \sum_i x_i y_i\) measures raw overlap: do “bright” pixels in \(\mathbf{x}\) tend to also be bright in \(\mathbf{y}\)?
  • Dividing by \(\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert_2 \lVert \mathbf{y} \rVert_2\) removes scale: if one image is uniformly darker or thicker, the similarity does not collapse.

Geometrically, cosine similarity is exactly the cosine of the angle \(\theta\) between the vectors:

\[ \mathrm{cosine}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \cos(\theta), \qquad \theta = \arccos\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert_2 \,\lVert \mathbf{y} \rVert_2 }\Big). \]

So “similar” becomes a crisp statement: two images behave similarly if their vectors point in almost the same direction, i.e., \(\theta\) is small, i.e., \(\cos(\theta)\) is close to 1.

Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors: small angle \( \theta \Rightarrow \cos(\theta)\approx 1 \Rightarrow \) high similarity.

Now the key point becomes mathematically obvious. Suppose \(\mathbf{y} = \alpha \mathbf{x}\) for some \(\alpha > 0\). That corresponds to “the same pattern but scaled” (thicker stroke, stronger contrast, different overall brightness). Then

\[ \mathrm{cosine}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}(\alpha\mathbf{x})}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 \,\lVert\alpha\mathbf{x}\rVert_2} = \frac{\alpha \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2^2}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2 \,(\alpha \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert_2)} = 1. \]

So cosine similarity explicitly treats positive rescaling as “no change in identity”, which is exactly what we want for handwritten digits: the geometry respects the shape more than the absolute intensity. This is the moment where meaning starts to look like a property of space. Not a definition. Not a label. A geometry.

Once you put data into a space, “meaning” becomes something you can measure.

Why this matters for language

This is why images are such a powerful place to begin. They show us what learning systems want but text does not naturally provide.

Unicode gave text stable identities. But identities are isolated points. They do not live in space. They have no distance, no angle, no neighbourhood. A learning system has nothing to measure except equality.

Images taught us a lesson the hard way:

Learning requires a space where similarity is measurable.

If language is ever going to be learnable in the same way images are, we must first build such a space for words. That is the problem embeddings are trying to solve.
