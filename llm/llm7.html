Picking Up the Thread

Before we move ahead, it is worth gently retracing the path that brought us here. Not to repeat the mechanics, but to remind ourselves what problem we have actually been circling all along. In the previous blog, we arrived at a probability. We learned how a raw dot product score, once reshaped and normalized, can be interpreted as a belief over possible context words. Given a center word, the model can now say how plausible each context word is, relative to all others.

That achievement is easy to underestimate. It took several careful steps to get there. The score had to be made positive. It had to be made comparable. It had to be forced into a shared space where different possibilities could no longer exist independently. Only then did probability emerge, not as an assumption, but as a consequence of structure.

And yet, there is something quietly unsettling about where we are right now. Every probability we have written down so far has been computed using vectors that were chosen arbitrarily. They were not designed. They were not learned. They were simply initialized and left untouched. The mathematics worked, but it worked on placeholders.

This distinction matters. A probability distribution can be perfectly valid in form and still be completely wrong in substance. It can sum to one. It can express preferences. And yet, those preferences may have nothing to do with language. At this stage, if the model believes something, it believes it by coincidence.

This brings us back to the motivation that has been present from the very beginning, even if it has stayed in the background. The true objective was never to compute scores. It was never even to compute probabilities. Those were necessary constructions, but they were always in service of something deeper.

The real goal is to shape the vectors themselves. To arrange them so that when they interact, the probabilities they produce begin to reflect reality. A center vector should naturally favor the contexts it truly appears with. A context vector should respond strongly to the right centers and weakly to the rest. When this happens consistently, geometry stops being arbitrary. It starts to carry information.

So far, we have built a way to express belief. What remains is to teach the model what to believe.

Now that probability is on the table, a new possibility opens up. Beliefs can be compared. They can be judged against what actually happens in text. When the model assigns high probability to the wrong context, that mismatch can no longer hide. It becomes visible. And anything visible can exert pressure.

This blog is about that pressure. About how disagreement between belief and observation becomes the force that reshapes vectors. About how repeated correction, applied gently but consistently, turns random points in space into representations that encode meaning.

From this point onward, we are no longer just describing a system. We are preparing to let it learn. The machinery is already in place. What remains is to see how it responds when its beliefs are challenged by data.

That is the journey we begin here.

Two Probability Distributions, One Question

At this stage, it is important to pause and be very clear about what we have actually built. The probability distribution we derived so far is real and well-formed, but it is not yet grounded in language. It is the probability mass function induced by a set of randomly chosen vectors. Given a center word represented by a vector \( \mathbf{v} \), the model assigns probabilities to all possible context words. Formally, we can write this as \( P_{\text{model}}(c \mid v) \).

This distribution has all the right mathematical properties. It is positive. It sums to one. It expresses relative preference. But it expresses the preferences of the model, not the preferences of the data. At this point, the model believes something, but it believes it for no good reason.

What we currently have is a valid probability distribution, but it is a distribution of a random geometry.

To see why this matters, it helps to look backward, to the object that motivated this entire journey in the first place: the co-occurrence matrix. Earlier, we treated it as a table of counts. But those counts carry more structure than we initially acknowledged.

Fix a center word \( v \). Look across its row in the co-occurrence table. Each entry tells us how many times a particular context word \( c \) appeared near \( v \) in the corpus. If we sum all those counts and divide each entry by that total, we are no longer counting. We are estimating frequencies.

And frequencies, when normalized, are probabilities.

Written explicitly, the data induces its own conditional distribution:

\[ P_{\text{data}}(c \mid v) = \frac{\text{count}(v, c)} {\sum_{c'} \text{count}(v, c')} \]

This expression answers a very concrete question. Given that the word \( v \) appeared in text, how likely was it to be accompanied by the context word \( c \)? It is empirical. It reflects what actually happened.

At this point, something important becomes visible. We are not dealing with one probability distribution, but with two. They answer the same question. They live over the same vocabulary. But they come from very different sources.

One distribution, \( P_{\text{data}}(c \mid v) \), comes directly from observed language. It is descriptive. It carries no intention beyond recording experience. The other, \( P_{\text{model}}(c \mid v) \), comes from vector geometry. It is indirect. It expresses what the current vectors imply, whether or not those implications make sense.

Learning begins when these two distributions are placed side by side.

Once both distributions are visible, the objective becomes almost unavoidable. For each center word \( v \), we would like the model’s distribution to resemble the data’s distribution. When the data assigns high probability to a context, the model should do the same. When the data assigns low probability, the model should suppress it.

Every difference between \( P_{\text{data}}(c \mid v) \) and \( P_{\text{model}}(c \mid v) \) represents a disagreement. And disagreement is no longer something abstract. It is a quantity that can be measured.

This reframes the entire problem. We are no longer asking how to store co-occurrences. We are no longer even asking how to compute scores. We are asking how to reshape vectors so that one conditional probability distribution moves closer to another.

From here onward, learning means aligning \( P_{\text{model}} \) with \( P_{\text{data}} \).

The remainder of this blog, and the ones that follow, will be devoted to understanding how to measure this mismatch, how to turn it into pressure, and how repeated pressure slowly transforms random vectors into representations that reflect language itself.

So Where Does \( P_{\text{data}} \) Come From?

At this point, it is very reasonable for a reader to feel uneasy. We keep referring to something called \( P_{\text{data}} \), as if it were a well-defined probability distribution. And yet, we have repeatedly said that we do not want to count, we do not want to build a co-occurrence matrix, and we do not want to explicitly compute probabilities.

So the confusion is natural. If we never constructed \( P_{\text{data}} \), where is it coming from? What does it even mean to say that it exists?

This confusion is not a problem. It is the key insight waiting to happen.

The resolution lies in recognizing a quiet shift in perspective. \( P_{\text{data}} \) is not something we build first and then use for training. It is something that is already encoded in the way the training data appears.

To see this clearly, we need to return to the text itself and look at how experience unfolds. Consider again our tiny corpus: “I love my dog.” “You love my dog.” “I love my cat.” “You love my cat.”

Now imagine reading these sentences one after another. We are not analyzing them. We are not summarizing them. We are simply encountering them.

Focus on the word “my.” Each time it appears, it appears with context. But that context is not random. It has a structure that only becomes visible through repetition.

Across the four sentences, the word “my” appears four times. And across those four appearances, the word “love” appears next to it every single time. That is four experiences of the pair (“my”, “love”).

The words “dog” and “cat” also appear next to “my,” but only twice each. Those experiences are real, but they are less frequent.

At this moment, we can say something very directly. Nothing has been counted. Nothing has been normalized. And yet, the data has already expressed a preference.

Experiencing (“my”, “love”) four times and (“my”, “dog”) twice is already a statement of relative likelihood.

This is where \( P_{\text{data}} \) lives. Not in a table. Not in a formula. But in the unequal frequency with which different relationships are encountered during training.

If we were to stop the process, collect all these experiences, and normalize them, we would recover an explicit probability distribution. But that step is optional. The learning signal does not require it.

Instead of constructing \( P_{\text{data}} \), we sample from it. Every center–context pair emitted by the corpus is one draw from the data distribution. Pairs that occur often are sampled often. Pairs that occur rarely are sampled rarely. Pairs that never occur are never sampled.

Seen this way, the confusion dissolves. \( P_{\text{data}} \) does not need to be computed in advance because it is already present in the sequence of training events. The corpus does not describe the distribution. It instantiates it.

Learning then becomes a process of exposure. The model is shown what the data prefers by how often it encounters certain relationships. Four encounters apply twice as much pressure as two encounters. That difference accumulates naturally over time.

\( P_{\text{data}} \) is not constructed. It is revealed through repeated experience.

Once this is clear, we are finally ready to move forward. Now that we understand where \( P_{\text{data}} \) comes from, we can ask a sharper question. What happens when the model’s own probability distribution fails to respect these repeated experiences?

That mismatch is no longer mysterious. It is measurable. And it is exactly what drives learning.

What Training Actually Does, One Experience at a Time

It helps to pause here and imagine what training really looks like. Not as equations on a page, but as a process unfolding over time. The model does not sit in front of the entire corpus. It does not see distributions. It does not know what is frequent or rare.

Instead, it sees one small experience at a time. A single center word. A single context word. A score is computed from the current vectors. That score is turned into a probability. And then, almost imperceptibly, the vectors are nudged.

On its own, this looks meaningless. If the model sees the pair (“my”, “love”) once, what could it possibly learn from that? The adjustment is tiny. So tiny that it barely registers.

Nothing important is learned from a single example.

The key is that training does not stop at one example. The same kinds of experiences keep returning. The same center words appear again. The same context words appear again. And when they do, the same kind of correction is applied again.

This is where cumulative experience quietly takes over. When (“my”, “love”) appears repeatedly in the corpus, the model does not remember the number four. It does something more physical. It receives the same directional push four times.

Each push is small. But they all point in the same direction. They reinforce one another. Over time, they leave a visible mark on the geometry.

Now contrast this with (“my”, “dog”) or (“my”, “cat”). These pairs also appear. They also apply pressure. But they do so less often. The pushes are fewer. The cumulative effect is weaker.

This difference in repetition is already enough. Without ever counting. Without ever normalizing. The model is being exposed to the same imbalance that exists in the data itself.

Seen this way, training is not a sequence of reactions. It is an accumulation of pressure. Rare events leave faint traces. Frequent events carve deep grooves.

This is why it is misleading to think of learning as responding correctly to individual examples. No single update defines meaning. Meaning emerges from consistency. From seeing the same relationship again and again and adjusting in the same direction each time.

Training works because repetition carries structure.

Over a long enough run, the cumulative effect of these tiny updates reproduces what we would have called \( P_{\text{data}} \), even though it was never written down. The model experiences the data distribution not as a table, but as repeated evidence.

What matters is not what happens at one step, but what keeps happening. And this is the crux of learning: persistent disagreement produces persistent pressure, and persistent pressure reshapes the vectors.

A Tiny Numerical Example: How Repetition Becomes Stronger Pressure

Let us make this intuition painfully concrete with numbers. Not because we need the numbers to train, but because the numbers help us see what training is secretly doing. We want to watch the cumulative effect, not the effect of a single step.

We will stay with the same tiny corpus: “I love my dog.” “You love my dog.” “I love my cat.” “You love my cat.”

Focus on the center word “my.” From this corpus, we keep encountering the pair (“my”, “love”) four times, while (“my”, “dog”) appears two times and (“my”, “cat”) appears two times. That is the only fact we need. Everything else follows from it.

Now imagine the model at the very beginning of training. Its vectors are random, so its beliefs are random too. Suppose that, for the center word “my,” the model currently assigns the following probabilities to three possible contexts:

\[ P_{\text{model}}(\text{love}\mid \text{my}) = 0.34,\quad P_{\text{model}}(\text{dog}\mid \text{my}) = 0.33,\quad P_{\text{model}}(\text{cat}\mid \text{my}) = 0.33 \]

These numbers are not “wrong” yet in any dramatic sense. They are just bland. They do not reflect the asymmetry that the corpus keeps showing. In the data stream, “love” is encountered more often next to “my.” In the model’s belief, everything is nearly equal.

Now we describe training in the most stripped-down way possible. Each time the corpus shows a real pair, the model makes a small adjustment that increases the probability of the observed context and decreases the rest slightly, because probability mass must still sum to one.

We will not specify the exact update rule yet. We do not need to. To illustrate the cumulative idea, we only need to assume something simple: each time the pair (“my”, “love”) appears, the model increases \(P_{\text{model}}(\text{love}\mid \text{my})\) by a small amount, say \(+0.02\), and then renormalizes so the three probabilities still sum to one.

This does not represent the full learning algorithm. It is just a numerical story that makes the intuition visible.

A single update is tiny. The point is what happens when the same update repeats.

Let us run the corpus as a stream of experiences. The pair (“my”, “love”) appears four times in total. So this small \(+0.02\) push happens four times.

Starting from \(0.34\), after four repeated “love” encounters, the probability of “love” rises roughly by \(4 \times 0.02 = 0.08\), ending near:

\[ P_{\text{model}}(\text{love}\mid \text{my}) \approx 0.42 \]

Because probability mass is conserved, that extra \(0.08\) must come from somewhere. It is pulled away from the alternatives “dog” and “cat.” If the reduction is roughly shared, each loses about \(0.04\), leaving something like:

\[ P_{\text{model}}(\text{dog}\mid \text{my}) \approx 0.29,\quad P_{\text{model}}(\text{cat}\mid \text{my}) \approx 0.29 \]

Now notice what just happened. Nothing in this story depended on one magical step. No single update suddenly made the model correct. The only reason “love” rose above “dog” and “cat” is that the pair (“my”, “love”) returned more often. The corpus applied pressure repeatedly in the same direction.

Now do the same thought experiment for (“my”, “dog”). That pair appears only twice. So the “dog” push happens only twice. If each dog encounter also contributes a \(+0.02\) nudge, then “dog” rises only by about \(0.04\), not \(0.08\).

This difference is the entire point. The model does not need an explicit table of \( P_{\text{data}} \). It experiences \( P_{\text{data}} \) as uneven repetition. More repetitions create stronger cumulative drift. Fewer repetitions create weaker drift.

Training is not about reacting to one event. Training is about letting frequency accumulate into geometry.

If we continued training for longer, running through the corpus again and again, these small differences would keep stacking. “Love” would continue to dominate as a context for “my,” because the data keeps presenting it as dominant. Eventually, the model’s belief would begin to resemble the empirical tendencies in the corpus.

That is the picture to keep in mind moving forward. We are not trying to “learn a table.” We are letting repeated experiences apply repeated pressure, until the model’s probabilities begin to mirror what the data keeps showing.

This brings us to a natural pause. Up to this point, we have not introduced any formal notion of error, loss, or objective. We have only described a process. Random vectors produce scores. Scores become probabilities. Probabilities are nudged, again and again, by repeated encounters in the data stream. Over time, those nudges accumulate into structure.

What matters is not any single update, but the direction of the drift. Contexts that appear more often exert more pressure. Contexts that appear less often fade into the background. Without ever constructing a co-occurrence table, the model is quietly exposed to the same statistical regularities that such a table would have recorded.

At this stage, the model has beliefs. What it does not yet have is a way to judge them.

We now have two distributions in play. One is implicit in the data, revealed through repeated experience. The other lives inside the model, expressed as probabilities derived from its vectors. Training, in its most honest form, is about bringing these two into alignment.

In the next blog, we will make this comparison explicit. We will ask a precise question: how different is \( P_{\text{model}} \) from \( P_{\text{data}} \)? Once that question is well-defined, the remaining pieces of learning fall into place naturally.
