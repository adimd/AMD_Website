Architecting Intelligence: Building LLMs From First Principle

From Words to Spaces: How Language Becomes Geometry

The mathematical foundations that make embeddings, similarity, and attention possible

Why meaning is really about behaviour

If you ask a philosopher where the meaning of a word lives, they might say something like: the meaning of dog lives inside the word dog itself. It’s a beautiful thought, and it sounds almost comforting, because it suggests meaning is self-contained—sealed neatly inside language like a jewel inside a box. But the moment you try to build a machine that does anything with language, that beauty becomes a dead end. A computer does not have the kind of life that makes the word “dog” feel like anything. It has no childhood memory of being chased. No sense of fur. No smell. No involuntary “aww cute” reaction. It has no world to attach the word to. All it sees is the surface: a sequence of symbols, "d", "o", "g", and a set of numeric identifiers sitting behind them.

So if meaning is not already sitting inside the word like a secret essence, where does it come from? The answer is less mystical and far more practical, and once you see it, you start noticing it everywhere. We understand words because of the situations they appear in and the neighbours they keep. You do not learn what “dog” means by staring at the letters. You learn it because “dog” tends to show up near things that slowly build a picture in your head: “pet”, “walk”, “bark”, “leash”. In the same way, “cat” gathers its own little orbit: “purr”, “climb”, “couch-ownership” (which, honestly, is the most honest description of cats). And some phrases—like “quantum field theory”—develop a very strong habit of never appearing anywhere near “cute Instagram story”.

We understand words because of the situations they appear in and the neighbours they keep.

Every time you read or hear a sentence, your brain quietly updates an internal model, even if you are not consciously trying to learn anything. It is tracking patterns: what tends to appear together, what rarely appears together, what seems to be interchangeable, what seems to be incompatible. Linguists noticed this long before modern machine learning existed. Zellig Harris hinted at it. J. R. Firth gave it its most famous phrasing: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” And once you recognise this idea, you realise that a huge chunk of modern NLP is basically an elaborate, industrial-scale attempt to operationalise that one sentence.

Distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

A tiny universe of six words

Instead of staying philosophical, let’s do something deliberately small and concrete. Let’s build a tiny language. Not English. Not Kannada. Just a miniature universe where only a handful of sentences exist, so we can see the idea of “meaning as behaviour” form in slow motion. Imagine our entire corpus is just these four lines:

  • I love my dog
  • I love my cat
  • You love my dog
  • You love my cat

Now, to do anything mathematical, we need a fixed vocabulary and a fixed ordering—something a machine can index into without ambiguity. So we declare our six-word universe as follows: 0 → I, 1 → You, 2 → love, 3 → my, 4 → dog, 5 → cat. At this point, something interesting happens: even before we touch a single equation, your brain already starts seeing structure. “Dog” and “cat” feel similar. “I” and “You” feel like they play the same role at the start. “love my” feels almost welded together, like a little phrase that refuses to separate.

The challenge is to make a machine notice the same thing without giving it any external knowledge. No dictionary. No labels. No “dog and cat are pets”. We will be stubborn about this. The only thing we allow the machine to observe is neighbour behaviour—who tends to sit next to whom.

The only thing we’ll let the machine look at is neighbours. Nothing else.

Defining neighbours properly (window size = 1)

Of course, “context” can mean many things. You could define it as “appearing in the same sentence”, or “appearing within five words”, or “appearing somewhere in the same paragraph”, depending on what you want your model to pick up. But for now, we deliberately choose a definition that is strict and clean, because we want to see the mechanism without extra complications: two words are neighbours if they are adjacent in a sentence. That’s it. A context window of size 1.

Rule: two words are neighbours if they are adjacent in a sentence. This is a context window of size 1.

So in the sentence I love my dog, the adjacent pairs are (I, love), (love, my), and (my, dog). Now we make one more design choice that keeps the mathematics pleasant: for every adjacent pair (a, b), we increment both directions, (a, b) and (b, a). That gives us a symmetric co-occurrence matrix. And since we’re not interested in order yet—who comes first, who comes second—this symmetry is exactly what we want. We just care that these words bump into each other as neighbours.

For every adjacent pair \((a, b)\), we increment both directions: \((a, b)\) and \((b, a)\). That way, our co-occurrence matrix is symmetric.

If we do this for all four sentences and then simply add up the counts, something pleasantly simple falls out. I – love appears as neighbours 2 times. You – love appears 2 times. love – my appears 4 times. my – dog appears 2 times. my – cat appears 2 times. Every other pair never touches, so its count is 0. In this tiny universe, the entire behavioural story of these words is captured by those few neighbour relationships.

In this tiny universe, “dog” and “cat” share the exact same neighbour (“my”) with the same strength — so they behave identically, even though we never told the machine what they mean.

Building the 6×6 co-occurrence matrix

Now we convert neighbour behaviour into something the machine can hold as structure: a matrix. We fix the vocabulary order as \[ V = [\text{I},\ \text{You},\ \text{love},\ \text{my},\ \text{dog},\ \text{cat}] \] and we build a matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}\) where \(M[i,j]\) is the number of times word \(i\) appears next to word \(j\) with window size 1, after making it symmetric by counting both directions.

And now comes the satisfying part: the matrix reveals, without any dictionary or semantic labels, the behavioural symmetry we already felt intuitively. The rows for dog and cat are identical: [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0]. The rows for I and You are also identical: [0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0]. Meanwhile, love and my sit in the middle as connectors, acting like the bridges that let subjects reach pets. In other words, the machine has not been told that “dog” and “cat” are similar. It has been shown only neighbour behaviour, and the similarity appears as a structural fact.

Without any dictionary, annotations, or labels, the matrix has quietly learned that “dog” and “cat” behave the same way in this little world.

And notice: we got this just from one-step neighbours. No big window. No sentence-level context. Just adjacency.

Seeing meaning as geometry

At this point, something subtle but powerful has happened. Each row of the matrix is a vector. That means each word now has an “address” in a 6-dimensional space, not because we designed a geometry in advance, but because behaviour naturally became geometry once we represented it as counts. dog is the vector [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0]. cat is the same vector. love becomes [2, 2, 0, 4, 0, 0], and my becomes [0, 0, 4, 0, 2, 2]. If you could flatten this space into a good 2D picture, “dog” and “cat” would sit on top of each other. “I” and “You” would sit together somewhere else. “love” and “my” would appear like busy intersections because they touch many neighbours.

Similar words have similar vectors. Similar vectors live near each other in geometry.

This is the first glimpse of what we will eventually call an embedding space. Our space here is tiny and clumsy—six dimensions is not much, and the corpus is toy-sized—but the conceptual shift is already visible. Meaning is starting to look less like a definition and more like a location: where a word lives, relative to other words, based on the patterns of its usage.

Of course, the moment you get excited about this, reality immediately shows up with a problem. Real language is not a six-word universe.

Why co-occurrence matrices don’t scale

In real NLP, your vocabulary is not 6. It might be 50,000 for a modest model, or 200,000 if you are generous with rare words. And the co-occurrence matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times V}\) scales brutally with that vocabulary size. A 50,000-word vocabulary implies \[ 50{,}000 \times 50{,}000 = 2.5 \text{ billion cells}. \] This is not a cute table anymore. This is the Excel sheet that makes your laptop cry.

And the pain is not just size. The matrix is mostly empty, because most word pairs never sit next to each other, so most entries are zero. The matrix is noisy, because rare words show up in rare contexts, and tiny counts produce fragile statistics. And even when you do manage to store the thing, each row is a 50,000-dimensional vector, which makes similarity computations expensive and awkward. So yes: co-occurrence captures something real about meaning. But it captures it in a form that is information-rich and computationally inconvenient, like keeping your entire life’s knowledge as raw CCTV footage. Everything you care about is in there somewhere. It’s just not the format you want to work with.

From sparse counts to dense embeddings

Once you accept that the co-occurrence idea is correct but unwieldy, the natural next question appears almost automatically:

Do we really need all 50,000 numbers in each word’s vector?

Intuitively, no. We do not need the full high-dimensional fingerprint to preserve the behavioural story. What we need is a compressed representation that still keeps the crucial relationships: which kinds of contexts this word tends to live in, and how that compares to other words. So instead of a giant sparse vector like \[ \text{dog}_{\text{sparse}} = [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, \dots, 0] \in \mathbb{R}^{V}, \] we would like a much smaller dense vector, something like \[ \text{dog}_{\text{embed}} = [0.72,\; -0.11,\; 0.83,\; 0.21,\; \dots] \in \mathbb{R}^{d}, \] where \(d\) might be 50, 100, or 300—dramatically smaller than \(V\), but still expressive enough to preserve similarity.

These smaller, dense vectors are what we call word embeddings.

They are still trying to encode “the company a word keeps”, but in a form that is computationally tractable and learnable. From a matrix perspective, you can imagine approximating the huge co-occurrence matrix \(M\) using a low-rank factorisation: \[ M \approx W C^{\top}, \] where \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) holds one \(d\)-dimensional vector per word as a “center” representation, and \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) holds one \(d\)-dimensional vector per word as a “context” representation, with \(d \ll V\).

But in practice, we don’t actually build \(M\) and then factor it, because that would defeat the whole point. Instead, methods like Word2Vec learn \(W\) and \(C\) directly by turning neighbour structure into a prediction game: given a word, predict its neighbours; or given neighbours, predict the missing word. The gradients do the compression for us, and the geometry emerges as a side effect of learning to predict context.

Co-occurrence gave us the idea of “meaning as a vector”. Embeddings make that idea computationally tractable.

Where this is going next

So here’s the arc so far. In the early parts of this series, we watched text become numbers in the first place—ASCII, Unicode, UTF-8—where the triumph was representability, not meaning. In this part, we made a different move: we treated meaning as behaviour, and we let behaviour solidify into structure by counting neighbours. In a tiny universe, that was enough to make “dog” and “cat” land in the same place without anyone telling the machine what a pet is. And then we hit the real-world constraint: the naïve structure works conceptually, but it collapses under scale.

The big conceptual shift: Meaning = patterns of usage, compressed into vectors.

What we have not done yet is show how those dense vectors are actually learned from real text—how “neighbourhood” becomes a prediction task, how a simple model turns repeated exposure into geometry, and how gradients slowly sculpt an embedding space where similarity becomes measurable. That is where we go next: we’ll turn “who are my neighbours?” into a game the machine can play, and we’ll watch the space of meaning emerge not as a definition we hand to the model, but as structure it earns through prediction.

Once embeddings feel natural, tokens and attention stop feeling like magic tricks. They start feeling like the next logical steps in the same story.
