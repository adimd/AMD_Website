Architecting Intelligence: Building LLMs From First Principle

Why Raw Dot Products Aren’t Enough

How scores become probabilities that let contexts compete

Picking Up the Thread

At the end of the previous blog, we arrived at a quiet but important conclusion. The massive V by V co-occurrence table had disappeared, yet nothing essential had been lost. Instead of storing relationships explicitly, we allowed them to live as geometry. Each word existed as a vector, and the old table survived only implicitly, expressed through interactions between center vectors and context vectors.

From that point on, the core operation became deceptively simple. Choose a center word, choose a context word, and compute a single number. That number came from a dot product. Larger values suggested stronger compatibility. Smaller values suggested weaker association. As sentences accumulated, these tiny numerical signals nudged vectors around, and meaning began to appear as structure. Words with similar usage histories drifted into similar regions of space. Clusters emerged without ever being explicitly declared.

Seen this way, it is tempting to believe that the dot product score is the destination. After all, it seems to respond correctly to data. When words co-occur, the score tends to increase. When they do not, the score tends to decrease. It feels like learning. It feels like progress. But this is precisely where the cracks begin to show, because a raw score is a surprisingly awkward object to build learning around.

The first issue is that the score is unbounded. There is no natural ceiling and no natural floor. If vector magnitudes grow, the score grows automatically. Nothing prevents the model from improving its numbers simply by stretching the geometry, even if the underlying relationships remain poorly structured. A larger score does not necessarily mean a sharper distinction. It may only mean that the vectors have become longer.

The second issue is that the score freely crosses zero. Positive, negative, large, small. Geometrically, this is perfectly reasonable. Semantically, it is ambiguous. What does a negative score actually represent. Is it the absence of a relationship, or an active opposition. How much worse is −3 compared to −1. The number itself does not answer these questions. It is not a probability. It is not a confidence. It is simply a signed measurement with no built-in interpretation.

The deepest problem, however, is more subtle. A dot product score is produced in isolation. When we compute the score between a center word and a single context word, that calculation has no awareness of any other possible contexts. Nothing forces different context words to compete with one another. Nothing enforces the idea that increasing the plausibility of one context should decrease the plausibility of another.

In other words, the score answers the wrong question. It tells us whether two vectors align, but it does not tell us which context should be preferred among all possibilities. There is no notion of a distribution. No conservation of plausibility. No global comparison. Each score floats on its own, disconnected from the rest.

What we actually want is something stronger. Given a center word, we want the model to express a preference over the entire vocabulary. We want it to behave as if it is making a choice. Among all possible context words, which ones make sense here, and which ones should be unlikely. The moment we ask for this, the problem changes shape.

Now contexts must compete. If one context becomes more plausible, others must become less so. Plausibility must be shared, not created out of thin air. This single requirement introduces structure that the raw dot product alone cannot provide.

The dot product does not disappear at this point. It simply changes role. It stops being the final answer and becomes a raw ingredient, a compatibility signal that must be reshaped into something that supports comparison, normalization, and learning.

In the rest of this blog, we will build that transformation carefully. We will see how raw scores are turned into probabilities, why this step is conceptually necessary rather than cosmetic, and how it quietly prepares the ground for loss functions and the practical mechanisms that make large-scale learning possible.

Why the raw score is not enough.

A dot product score is unbounded, so it can grow larger without becoming more meaningful. It carries sign without interpretation, making negative and positive values hard to read. And most critically, each score exists in isolation, with no notion of preference or competition among possible contexts.

Until these issues are resolved, the score can react to data, but it cannot support judgment or learning.

Fixing the Sign Problem: Why Scores Must Become Positive

Let us begin with the most immediate problem of raw dot product scores. They freely cross zero. Positive values, negative values, large magnitudes, small magnitudes. From a geometric point of view, this is perfectly acceptable. From a learning point of view, it is deeply inconvenient.

A negative score does not clearly tell us what went wrong. Does it mean the context is actively incompatible. Does it mean the relationship is simply weak. Is −4 twice as bad as −2. Nothing in the number itself answers these questions. The sign carries direction, but not meaning.

If we want scores to behave like plausibility signals, the first requirement is simple. They must live on a single, interpretable scale. In particular, they must be positive. Not because negativity is mathematically invalid, but because learning needs a notion of strength, not opposition.

Before scores can compete, they must first speak the same language.

This leads us to a very natural transformation. We take the raw dot product score and pass it through the exponential function. Nothing mysterious happens here. We are not adding information. We are reshaping the signal so that it becomes usable.

The exponential has two properties that make it almost unavoidable. First, it maps every real number to a positive value. No matter how negative the score is, the output is still greater than zero. The sign ambiguity disappears. Every score now represents a non-negative degree of plausibility.

Second, and more importantly, the exponential preserves relative differences. This is the subtle but crucial point. The exponential does not care about absolute values. It cares about gaps.

If one score is larger than another by a fixed amount, the exponential guarantees a fixed multiplicative ratio between their transformed values. A difference of one unit always corresponds to the same relative advantage, regardless of where it occurs on the number line.

For example, suppose two context words produce scores of 2 and 1. After exponentiation, their values become e² and e¹. The first is e times larger than the second. Now suppose the scores were −1 and −2. After exponentiation, we again get e⁻¹ and e⁻². Once more, the ratio is exactly e.

Exponentials convert additive differences into multiplicative preference.

This is precisely what we want. Learning should respond to relative evidence, not absolute scale. If one context fits better than another by a certain margin, that margin should matter in the same way everywhere. Exponentiation enforces this consistency automatically.

After this transformation, the score has changed character. It is no longer a signed geometric measurement. It has become a positive signal that expresses relative strength. Negative scores are not banned. They are simply translated into small plausibility. Positive scores become large plausibility.

This fixes our first problem cleanly. The ambiguity of sign is gone. Every score now lives on a shared, positive scale. But notice what has not yet happened. These values still do not compete. Each exponentiated score still exists on its own. Nothing yet forces them to relate to one another.

That remaining issue is deeper. And fixing it will require us to look at all contexts together, not one at a time. That is the next step.

What exponentiation fixes and what it does not.

The exponential removes sign ambiguity and ensures that all scores are positive and comparable. It also preserves relative differences, so stronger evidence grows faster than weaker evidence.

But exponentiation alone does not create preference. Each score still exists independently. Nothing yet forces different contexts to compete.

When Contexts Begin to Compete

At this point, something important is still missing. Even after exponentiation, even after we have ensured that every score is positive and that relative differences matter, the model remains strangely comfortable. Nothing forces it to feel pressure. Nothing forces it to choose.

To see why this matters, it helps to return to actual language. Consider the sentence:

I love my dog

When the word love appears, many context words are theoretically possible. “My” is plausible. “So” is plausible. “The” is plausible. But in the real sentence, only one of these words actually occurs. The presence of my is not just information. It is exclusion. Once my appears, countless alternatives did not.

This is what tension means in language. Every observed word implicitly says: this happened, and everything else did not.

Language creates meaning by choosing one alternative and rejecting many others.

As long as our scores are independent, this tension cannot exist. The model is free to assign high plausibility to my, the, a, and your simultaneously. Nothing forces it to prefer one over the others. But this is not how text behaves.

So instead of asking whether a single context word fits a center word, we must ask a stronger question. Given a fixed center word, how should plausibility be distributed across all possible context words?

This is where the idea of normalization enters, not as a mathematical trick, but as a structural necessity.

Let the center word be represented by a vector \( \mathbf{v} \), and let each possible context word be represented by vectors \( \mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2, \dots, \mathbf{c}_V \). After exponentiation, we obtain a collection of positive values:

\[ \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_1), \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_2), \dots, \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_V) \]

Each of these numbers reflects how compatible the center word is with a particular context word. But taken together, they still do not express preference. They simply coexist.

To introduce tension, these values must share a constraint. They must be tied together. We do this by summing over all possible contexts:

\[ Z(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{j=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_j) \]

This quantity does not encode meaning by itself. Its role is more subtle. It forces the model to look at the entire vocabulary at once. Every possible context word is now present in the calculation, even the ones that fit poorly.

Once this total exists, the interpretation of each score changes. No context word can be judged in isolation. Each one must now be read as a fraction of the whole:

\[ P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_i)}{Z(\mathbf{v})} \]

This single division introduces dependency. If the compatibility between love and my increases, the numerator grows. But as it grows, the denominator grows as well. And as the denominator grows, the relative share available to the, a, and your shrinks.

This is the mathematical expression of linguistic tension. To favor one context, the model must implicitly disfavor others. Plausibility is no longer free. It is conserved.

Preference emerges only when plausibility is shared.

As this competition intensifies, another behavior becomes visible. The context word with the largest dot product naturally claims the largest share of the total. Smaller dot products receive smaller shares.

In the extreme case, if one dot product \( \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_k \) is much larger than all others, then:

\[ P(k \mid \mathbf{v}) \rightarrow 1, \quad P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) \rightarrow 0 \text{ for } i \neq k \]

The operation behaves like a maximum. But it never collapses abruptly. Near alternatives retain nonzero probability. Uncertainty is preserved.

This is why the transformation is called a soft maximum. It prefers without committing absolutely. It chooses while still remembering the alternatives.

The max has been softened into belief.

At this point, the numbers acquire a new identity. Each \( P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) \) is non-negative, and when we sum over all possible contexts, we obtain:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{V} P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) = 1 \]

Without explicitly setting out to do so, we have crossed into probability. For a fixed center word, these values form a probability mass function over the vocabulary.

Probability mass is conserved. It cannot be created or destroyed. It can only move from one context word to another as the geometry of the vectors changes.

Meaning now lives not in individual scores, but in how belief is distributed across alternatives. Only now does it make sense to ask whether the model placed its belief where real language places it.

That question is where learning truly begins.

Slowing Down and Making Sense of the Math

Before moving ahead, this is a good moment to slow the pace on purpose. Up to now, ideas have been flowing naturally from one to the next. Here, the goal is different. This section exists to gently unpack what the mathematics is actually doing, without assuming comfort or fluency. Nothing new is being introduced. We are only making explicit what is already implicit.

This is a pause for understanding, not a jump forward.

We begin with the idea of normalization. Despite how technical the word sounds, the idea behind it is very simple. Normalization is about taking several quantities and forcing them to live together as parts of a whole. Once normalized, no value is allowed to exist on its own. Every value must be understood relative to the others.

At this point in the story, we already have a collection of numbers. For a fixed center word represented by a vector \( \mathbf{v} \), we compute dot products with every context vector \( \mathbf{c}_1, \mathbf{c}_2, \dots, \mathbf{c}_V \). After passing these through the exponential function, we obtain a set of positive values:

\[ \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_1), \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_2), \dots, \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_V) \]

Each of these numbers expresses how compatible one context word is with the chosen center word. However, at this stage, they still do not speak to one another. Each value answers its own private question. None of them yet knows how strong it is compared to the rest.

To change this, we gather all of these values together and add them up:

\[ Z(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{j=1}^{V} \exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_j) \]

This quantity is simply the total amount of plausibility available across the entire vocabulary for this single center word. Nothing mysterious is happening here. We are only measuring how much compatibility exists in total.

Once this total exists, we can finally ask a more meaningful question. Instead of asking whether a context fits, we can ask how much it fits relative to all other possibilities. This is done by dividing:

\[ P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_i)}{Z(\mathbf{v})} \]

The symbol \( P \) stands for probability. The vertical bar should be read as “given.” So this expression means: the probability of observing context word \( i \), given the center word represented by \( \mathbf{v} \).

What makes this expression special is not its name, but its behavior. Every value is positive. And when we add up the probabilities for all possible contexts, the total is exactly one:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{V} P(i \mid \mathbf{v}) = 1 \]

This single property changes everything. It means plausibility is no longer created freely. If one context becomes more likely, others must become less so. A quiet tension is introduced. The contexts are now competing for the same finite mass.

Probability appears because plausibility is forced to be shared.

To see this in concrete terms, consider a very small example. Imagine only three possible context words. Suppose their dot products with the center vector are:

\[ \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_1 = 2, \quad \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_2 = 1, \quad \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{c}_3 = 0 \]

After exponentiation, these values become:

\[ \exp(2) \approx 7.39, \quad \exp(1) \approx 2.72, \quad \exp(0) = 1 \]

Their total is:

\[ Z = 7.39 + 2.72 + 1 = 11.11 \]

Dividing each value by this total gives:

\[ P(1 \mid \mathbf{v}) \approx 0.66, \quad P(2 \mid \mathbf{v}) \approx 0.24, \quad P(3 \mid \mathbf{v}) \approx 0.09 \]

Nothing about the ordering has changed. The strongest match remains strongest. What has changed is interpretation. These numbers now express belief. They describe how plausibility is distributed, not just how large a score happens to be.

This transformation is called the softmax because it behaves like a softened version of a maximum. Large values still dominate, but smaller values are not erased. Every option remains visible, even if its share becomes small.

At this point, the model is no longer producing raw scores. It is expressing a conditional probability distribution. And distributions can be compared to reality.

That comparison is where learning finally becomes possible.

This is a good place to stop and take a breath. We started with a simple score, a dot product between a center vector and a context vector. Step by step, we asked what that number could and could not tell us. By addressing its limitations, we arrived at something more structured. The score did not disappear. It was reshaped into a probability.

Nothing here was added arbitrarily. Each transformation was motivated by a problem we encountered along the way. The need to remove ambiguity, the need to compare alternatives, and finally the need to express belief in a shared, constrained form. Seen together, these steps explain how we moved from raw compatibility scores to a conditional probability distribution over context words.

If this reasoning feels natural, then the foundation is in place.

In the next blog, we will move forward with this probability view firmly in hand. We will ask how these probabilities should behave when they meet real data, and how disagreement between belief and observation creates the pressure that drives learning.
