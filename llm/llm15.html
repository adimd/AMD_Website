What a computer originally was

When we first learn about word embeddings, they feel almost like a breakthrough moment in understanding language computationally. For the first time, words are no longer treated as rigid symbols or dictionary entries. Instead, they are mapped into a continuous numerical space, where meaning appears to emerge naturally from data. Similar words cluster together, analogies begin to work, and language starts to look geometric rather than symbolic. At this stage, it is very tempting to believe that we have finally found a faithful numerical representation of meaning.

Words are no longer symbols — they become points in a space where geometry begins to resemble meaning.

At its core, a word embedding is a remarkably simple idea. Every word in the vocabulary is assigned a vector of fixed length, and once this mapping is learned, the vector becomes the word’s identity inside the model. No matter where the word appears — at the beginning of a sentence, in the middle of a paragraph, or across entirely different documents — the same vector is reused. The embedding does not change based on position, surrounding words, or intent. It is static, stable, and global.

This design choice is not accidental. Word embeddings are learned by observing how words co-occur across large corpora. Over time, the model discovers statistical regularities: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar vectors. As a result, embeddings capture what we might call the average meaning of a word — the dominant semantic signal that emerges across all its usages.

Embeddings do not capture what a word means in a sentence — they capture what it tends to mean across all sentences.

This is precisely why embeddings are so effective at representing general semantic relationships. They are excellent at answering questions like which words are related, which concepts tend to appear together, or which topics share a common semantic neighborhood. In many tasks, this alone already gives a surprisingly strong foundation.

Suggested visual: A simple 2D embedding space sketch with clusters labeled “finance”, “food”, “technology”. Place words as dots. Show that geometry reflects similarity, not usage.

However, this strength quietly hides an important limitation. Consider the word apple. In one sentence, apple refers to a fruit. In another, it refers to a company. Yet under a standard embedding model, apple has one single vector. That vector is neither purely “fruit” nor purely “brand”. It becomes a blend — a statistical average of all the contexts in which the word appears.

Suggested visual: Two short sentences side by side: “An apple a day keeps the doctor away” “I bought an Apple watch” Both arrows pointing to the same single embedding vector labeled “apple”.

The same issue becomes even more evident with words like bank. A bank can be a financial institution or the edge of a river. Humans rarely get confused because we rely heavily on surrounding words to resolve meaning. But a static embedding assigns bank exactly one vector regardless of whether it appears next to money or river. The embedding itself does not “switch meanings”. It remains unchanged.

Suggested visual: Two sentences: “money bank grows” “river bank erodes” Highlight the word “bank” in both and show both mapping to the same vector. Add a small note saying “context ignored”.
Static embeddings encode similarity, not context.

This reveals an important truth about word embeddings. They tell us what a word usually means, not what it means right now. Once trained, an embedding is frozen. It does not adapt to the sentence it appears in, nor does it respond to neighboring words. The word arrives into the sentence already carrying its meaning, rather than discovering its meaning through interaction.

Meaning is fixed before the sentence is even read.

At this point, we have something powerful, but incomplete. Word embeddings give us a rich, continuous representation of language, but they treat words as isolated entities. Meaning is stored inside individual vectors, not shaped dynamically by surrounding words. This gap — between average meaning and contextual meaning — is where the story of attention truly begins.
