Hexagon, Packet by Packet: A VLIW Course for Embedded ML

Why AI Runs on a Different Chip

Trying to understand the chip in my phone that does most of the AI, and finding out it was designed two decades ago for something else entirely.

I've been writing this down because I'm tired of half-understanding things. Every time someone says "your phone runs AI on the NPU", I nod along and the sentence keeps moving, but if you stopped me there I couldn't tell you why an NPU exists, or why the CPU isn't enough, or why the chip running AI in your phone today was actually built for something else first. So I decided to figure it out properly. This is the first post in a course I'm building for myself in public, called Hexagon, Packet by Packet. The plan is to take Qualcomm's Hexagon DSP apart, one concept at a time, and end with something I built that didn't exist before. This post is session one. It's the foundation. Before I write a single line of code for this chip, I need to know why it's shaped the way it is.

Four parts, in the same order I worked through them. Why specialised chips exist at all. What ML actually is, mathematically, when you stop talking about it as magic. What architectural choices fall out of those two facts. And where Hexagon sits among the other chips trying to do the same job.

Part 1

Why specialised chips exist

I'll start with a question that feels too basic, because it actually isn't. Why does your phone have more than one processor?

The lazy answer is "to do more things at once". One chip handles graphics, another handles the camera, another runs apps. That's true at the surface but it isn't the reason. The reason is much deeper, and it's about energy. Once you see the energy argument properly, everything else in the course follows from it like a chain of dominoes.

Here's the question I had to actually answer. You have a fixed transistor budget. Every transistor on your chip costs some power, even when it's idle, just by existing on the silicon. You can't have unlimited compute. So where do you spend the transistors?

A general-purpose CPU spends them on flexibility. The CPU has to be ready for anything. It runs your browser, then a Python script, then a video decoder, then it switches to playing music. It doesn't know in advance what's coming. To handle that uncertainty gracefully, modern CPUs contain elaborate machinery whose only job is to guess. Branch predictors that try to figure out which way an if statement will go before you've actually evaluated the condition. Out-of-order engines that reshuffle instructions on the fly to keep the execution units busy. Speculation that runs ahead before knowing if the work will be needed and throws it away if the guess was wrong. Multi-level caches that constantly evict and refetch data based on what they think you'll touch next.

None of this is doing arithmetic. None of it is the actual work. It's all the cost of being ready for anything. When your CPU computes 2 + 2, only a small fraction of its silicon is doing the addition. The rest is the apparatus of generality, sitting there because the next instruction might need it. That's not a complaint about CPU design. For unpredictable workloads it's the right call. But it's expensive. You're paying for an enormous amount of guessing infrastructure on every cycle.

A general-purpose CPU spends a lot of its energy budget just deciding what to do next.

So what happens if your workload is predictable? What if you knew, in advance, exactly what was coming? You'd throw the guessing infrastructure away. There's nothing to predict if you already know. There's nothing to speculate about if the path is fixed. There's nothing to reorder if the dependencies are visible to a compiler. And the silicon you saved by not building the guessing apparatus, you can spend on more arithmetic units instead. Same chip area, much more compute.

This is the deal specialised chips make. They give up generality. They can't run anything. But for the specific workload they were designed for, they get an order of magnitude more throughput per watt than a general-purpose chip could ever offer.

So the energy argument is also a transistor argument. Every choice you make about what apparatus to put on the chip is a choice about what apparatus you're not putting on the chip. CPUs spent the budget on flexibility. DSPs and NPUs spent it on raw arithmetic. The reason your phone has both is that some of your workload is unpredictable (your browser, your apps) and some is highly predictable (signal processing, ML inference). Each gets the chip that suits it best.

One thing that surprised me when I started thinking about this properly. My background is microcontrollers, and on a microcontroller you don't have any of this guessing apparatus either, but for a different reason. Microcontrollers don't have branch predictors or out-of-order execution because they need predictable timing, not because their workload is predictable. If you're controlling a motor or generating PWM, you can't have a branch predictor occasionally being wrong and stalling for twenty cycles. You need every iteration of your loop to take exactly the same number of cycles, or the timing breaks.

So microcontrollers and DSPs end up at similar architectural conclusions through different doors. Microcontrollers are simple because they need determinism. DSPs are simple because their workload doesn't need the complexity. Either way, the result is "no guessing infrastructure, more direct execution". Once I noticed that, the rest of Hexagon stopped feeling exotic. It's a microcontroller philosophy applied to wide vector workloads. Same determinism, different scale.

DSPs and microcontrollers reach the same place from different directions: no apparatus of guessing, just direct execution.

Why does this matter for phones specifically? Because phones can't afford the CPU answer. Your laptop is plugged in most of the time and can run a 30-watt processor without anyone caring. Your phone has a battery the size of a chocolate bar and is expected to last all day. If you ran every ML feature on the CPU at 2W instead of on an NPU at 0.2W, the difference over a 12-hour day is something like 22 watt-hours, which is roughly 30% of a phone battery. Multiply by the dozens of small ML features running on a modern phone and the maths gets worse. Specialisation isn't an optimisation here. It's the reason these features can exist at all.

Part 2

What ML actually is, mathematically

Once I accepted that ML needs its own chip, the next question was what kind of chip. To answer that properly I had to be honest about what neural network inference actually is, computationally. Not the marketing version about intelligence and learning. The version a hardware designer cares about.

Before I get to that, one thing that tripped me up early and is worth saying explicitly. There are two completely different problems people mean when they say "AI on a chip", and conflating them is the most common mistake.

Training is the process of creating a neural network. You feed it millions of examples, compare its guesses to the right answers, compute gradients, and slowly nudge the weights in the right direction over millions of iterations. Training happens on giant GPUs in data centres, drawing thousands of watts each, in 32-bit floating point, on hundreds of gigabytes of memory. The big language models you've heard of were trained on hardware your phone has nothing in common with.

Inference is the process of using an already-trained network. The weights are fixed. You feed in an input and get an output. No learning, no gradients. Just a forward pass. Inference can be done on much smaller hardware, in much lower precision, on a much tighter power budget. Your phone trains nothing. Your phone runs forward passes of models that were trained somewhere else, on much bigger hardware, and shipped to you in compressed form.

I keep emphasising this because the whole field of edge ML is built on this asymmetry. Train once, expensively, in the cloud. Run a billion times, cheaply, on the device. Hexagon was built for the cheaper half of that deal. It cannot train models. It doesn't have the memory, the precision, or the architectural flexibility for training. What it can do, extraordinarily well, is run quantised inference of an already-trained model, fast, on small power. Every architectural choice we'll examine in later sessions makes Hexagon better at inference and worse at training. It's a specialist, not a generalist.

Phones run inference. Training happens elsewhere. The chip in your pocket was built for the cheaper half of the deal.

Right, so what is inference? Honestly, it's embarrassingly simple. A neural network inference is a sequence of large matrix multiplications, with simple element-wise functions in between. That's it. That's the whole game.

Matrix multiplication is one of those operations that sounds boring on paper and turns out to be the foundation of everything modern AI does. You take two grids of numbers, line them up, and for each cell of the output you compute a sum of products from one row and one column. There are no branches inside it. There are no conditional paths. It's multiply, add, multiply, add, repeated until the output grid is full. A single layer of a transformer is a matrix multiplication. A single layer of a vision model is a matrix multiplication, possibly disguised as a convolution. The forward pass of a model is, structurally, thousands of these stacked on top of each other.

Now here's the part that took me a while to internalise. Matrix multiplication has a property that makes it almost perfectly suited to specialised hardware: data reuse. When you load a weight from memory, you don't use it once. You use it many times, in many different output cells. Same with the inputs. Every byte you load from memory contributes to dozens or hundreds of arithmetic operations. That's the leverage. That's why the chip can stay busy.

This sounds abstract but it's the single most important property of the workload, and once you see it visually it never goes away. Let me draw it.

Every weight you load contributes to many output cells, not just one. This is data reuse. It's the entire reason matrix multiplication can be made fast. Drag the size slider up: the bigger the matrices, the more reuse you get per byte loaded.

So matmul has high arithmetic intensity. That's the fancy term for "many operations per byte loaded from memory". It's the key concept I want you to leave this post with. Memory access is much, much slower than computation. A multiply takes one cycle. A trip to main memory takes a few hundred. So if your code does one operation per memory load, the processor is computing for one cycle and waiting for hundreds. The expensive arithmetic units sit idle. We call this being memory-bound, and it's the worst case.

If your code does many operations per memory load, the chip can stay busy. We call this being compute-bound, and it's the best case. The whole job of the chip designer is to make sure the chip can run compute-bound on the workloads it cares about, and the whole job of the kernel author is to write code that actually achieves it in practice.

Vector add, just adding two arrays element-wise, has terrible arithmetic intensity. You load 8 bytes (two numbers), do one addition, store 4 bytes. You'll never make this fast no matter how many ALUs you put on the chip, because the chip is starving for data.

Matmul is the opposite. Each weight gets reused. Each input gets multiplied by many weights. The bigger the matrices, the more reuse, the more compute-bound the operation becomes, the more the specialised hardware can flex.

Beyond matmul, the rest of inference is small. Element-wise functions like ReLU and softmax are applied to every element of the activations between matmul layers. These are individually simple, usually one operation per element, but they have to be fast too because they happen on every element. Reductions like sums and maxes appear inside softmax and attention; they're a bit trickier to vectorise but well-understood. So the workload of inference is roughly: most of the time, matmul. Some of the time, element-wise ops. A small slice, reductions. If you build a chip that's spectacular at the first, good at the second, and acceptable at the third, you have an ML inference chip. That's roughly what Hexagon is.

Part 3

The architecture falls out of the workload

This is the part I find genuinely beautiful, and I want to slow down for it. If you take the workload properties from Part 2 seriously and design backwards from them, you don't really have a choice about what the chip looks like. Each property forces an architectural decision, and the decisions are surprisingly orthogonal. They don't conflict with each other. They all push in the same direction.

Let me walk through the four big decisions one at a time, because each one is going to be a topic in its own session later.

Decision 1: Wide SIMD with narrow integer multipliers

The workload is massively parallel. Every output cell of a matmul can be computed independently. So you want many arithmetic units running in parallel. The question is what kind. The workload tolerates low precision (INT8 is fine for inference), and integer multipliers take roughly a tenth the silicon of equivalent floating-point multipliers. So you build many small integer multipliers instead of fewer big floating-point ones. Same transistor budget, way more arithmetic per cycle.

This is SIMD, Single Instruction Multiple Data. One instruction operates on a wide vector register full of small numbers. Hexagon's HVX unit can do 128 INT8 multiplies in one instruction, all in parallel. That's not 128 sequential multiplies, it's 128 simultaneous ones, in one cycle.

Decision 2: VLIW. Let the compiler do the smart bit.

You now have hundreds of arithmetic units. How do you decide what each one does each cycle? A general-purpose CPU answers this with the runtime guessing apparatus we already discussed: out-of-order execution, branch prediction, speculation. The CPU figures out at runtime which instructions can execute in parallel and dispatches them. For a CPU running unpredictable code, that's the right call.

But your workload isn't unpredictable. You know the matrix dimensions, the operations, the data flow. Why pay the silicon cost of figuring out at runtime what the compiler could figure out once, ahead of time, for free?

This is VLIW, Very Long Instruction Word. The compiler emits instructions in groups, called packets, where each packet is "do these N things in this cycle". The hardware doesn't have to decide anything. It just executes the schedule the compiler already produced. The instructions arrive at the chip already grouped. The chip is dumb in the best possible way: it trusts the compiler completely.

What does this trust buy? Roughly the 20-30% of die area that an out-of-order CPU spends on the orchestration engine. On a VLIW chip, that silicon becomes more arithmetic units. VLIW is not a strange choice. It's a deal: give up runtime flexibility, gain compute per watt. The deal pays off whenever the workload is predictable enough for the compiler to schedule well.

This is the central concept of the entire course. Every time we look at a piece of Hexagon assembly later, we'll see packets that look like { instruction; instruction; instruction; instruction }, and what we're seeing is the compiler's schedule. The packet is the unit of computation. Reading the packet is reading the chip's plan.

Decision 3: Software-managed memory, not a cache

Your arithmetic units want data continuously. Without enough data flowing in, those expensive multipliers sit idle and the whole design collapses. So how do you keep them fed?

A general-purpose CPU uses caches: hardware that automatically keeps recently-used data nearby, with elaborate replacement policies that try to predict what you'll need next. This works reasonably for general code. But it has costs. Caches are silicon-expensive. Their behaviour is unpredictable, because a cache miss causes a variable-length stall that can wreck timing. They only help when access patterns happen to match what the cache is good at.

For ML inference, you can do better. You know your access patterns in advance. So instead of a cache that guesses what to keep close, you build a scratchpad: a chunk of fast memory whose contents are managed explicitly by software. The kernel author tells the chip: "DMA this tile of weights into the scratchpad, run the kernel against it, then DMA in the next tile." No guessing, no replacement policies, no surprises.

On Hexagon this is called VTCM, short for Vector Tightly-Coupled Memory. It's 8 megabytes on the v75 architecture. Single-cycle access. You manage it yourself. The trade is the same as everywhere else in the chip: you give up automatic management and gain predictable, faster execution. You also give up a lot of transistors that would have been spent on tag arrays and coherence logic, and those transistors go to more arithmetic units.

This is going to be a big chunk of work later in the course. Real ML kernels on Hexagon are mostly about choreographing data movement (DMA in, compute, DMA out, often double-buffered so the next tile is being fetched while the current one is being computed on). The compute itself, once the data is in VTCM, is straightforward. The hard part is the choreography. We'll spend an entire session on it.

Decision 4: Hardware loops and predication, not branches

Most of the code you write for this chip is tight loops. Very tight. A matmul kernel might have an inner loop of four or five instructions executed thousands of times. The branch overhead at the end of each iteration (decrement counter, compare, jump back) would be annoying on any chip. On a chip whose whole point is dense compute, it's worse than annoying. It's wasted slot time in every packet.

So Hexagon has hardware loops. A special instruction tells the chip "run these N iterations" and the chip handles the counting and branching itself. There's a marker at the end of the loop body, written :endloop0, that means "if iterations remain, jump back to the start at zero cost". The branch is genuinely free. It happens inside the same cycle as the last instruction of the loop, using slot resources that would otherwise be idle.

Similarly, instead of branching for conditionals, Hexagon has predicate registers (P0, P1, P2, P3). An instruction can be tagged with a predicate, meaning "execute me, but only commit my result if this predicate is true". No branch, no misprediction, just a compact in-line conditional. Every if in your inner loop becomes predication. Every while becomes a hardware loop. The branch prediction machinery a CPU spends energy on simply isn't needed here.

If you tally up these four decisions:

  • Wide SIMD with narrow integer multipliers
  • Compiler-scheduled VLIW execution
  • Software-managed scratchpad memory
  • Hardware loops and predication instead of branches

...you've described Hexagon. And every choice came from a property of the workload. None of them is arbitrary. Once I saw this clearly, the chip stopped feeling like a list of weird Qualcomm decisions and started feeling like a consequence of the maths. That's the moment I want every reader to have. Part 4

Where Hexagon sits among its peers

Hexagon isn't alone. The edge ML chip space is actually crowded, and it's worth knowing the neighbourhood. Partly because the skill of "writing efficient kernels for specialised SIMD architectures" transfers across all of them, and partly because gaps in the field often live at the boundaries between what one chip is good at and what another is good at.

On Apple devices, the equivalent is the Apple Neural Engine, ANE. Architecturally it's the same philosophy (wide SIMD, INT8-first, software-managed memory) but mostly closed to outside developers. You can't really write hand-tuned ANE kernels from outside Apple. So if you want to learn this kind of programming, Apple isn't the entry point even though the install base is huge.

On Pixel phones, Google ships its own Tensor SoC with an Edge TPU variant. Same philosophy again, also fairly closed. MediaTek (the second-biggest Android SoC vendor) has an APU that's, again, in the same family. ARM licenses NPU IP called Ethos that other vendors integrate into their own chips, often for smaller IoT devices. NVIDIA's Jetson line is technically edge ML but it's really small GPUs, not NPUs, and you'd write CUDA for them rather than HVX.

The point isn't to memorise this list. The point is: Hexagon is one of several chips making roughly the same architectural choices, with different specifics. The mental model transfers. If you understand Hexagon deeply, you can pick up MediaTek APU programming or ARM Ethos programming much faster than someone starting cold. The differences are in the specific instructions and tooling, not in the philosophy.

One reason Hexagon is the right teaching vehicle, beyond its market presence, is that it's relatively open. Qualcomm publishes the SDK. The intrinsics are documented. The simulator is downloadable. You can actually write code and run it without a device. None of that is true for Apple Neural Engine. So even if you eventually want to work on Apple silicon (which you can't directly, since it's closed), starting on Hexagon is the right first step because it lets you actually do the work.

One last piece worth knowing. Most discussions of "AI hardware" you see in the news are about server-side training: NVIDIA H100s, Google TPU v5, AMD MI300. These chips are different beasts entirely. Floating-point heavy, hundreds of gigabytes of memory, hundreds of watts each. They have almost nothing in common with Hexagon. Hexagon is in the bottom-right corner of the AI hardware world: edge inference, quantised, low-power, real-time. Different market, different design, different skill set. They're cousins, not duplicates.

Although interestingly, Qualcomm announced server-side AI accelerators in 2025 (the AI200 and AI250) that are scaled-up Hexagon designs. So the boundary is starting to blur on the inference side, even if training stays in GPU territory. Worth knowing for career planning: the skill we're building isn't permanently confined to mobile.

Where this is going

So that's session one. We figured out why specialised chips exist (the energy and transistor budget argument). We figured out what ML actually is, mathematically (mostly matmul, with reuse, in low precision, on data we can predict). We watched the architecture fall out of those facts (wide SIMD, VLIW, software-managed memory, hardware loops). And we placed Hexagon among its peers (one of several edge inference NPUs, the most accessible one for learning).

This is the conceptual scaffolding. From here, every later session has somewhere to land. When session two introduces a VLIW packet, you'll already know why the packet exists. When session ten introduces VTCM, you'll already know why the chip uses a scratchpad instead of a cache. That's the value of going slow at the start. The investment compounds.

Next session, we open up the simulator, write our first piece of Hexagon C code, and look at our first VLIW packet on screen. The packet is the unit of computation on this chip. Once you can read one, you can start to read the chip itself, and the rest of the course is essentially "learn to read assembly fluently, then learn to write kernels that the assembly looks good for". That's the arc.

The end of the course isn't a kernel. It's a project that didn't exist before, in a corner of the field that needed it.
