The Mathematics ML Runs On · Class 7

The Sum, the Product, and the Number That Vanishes

Two symbols, sigma and pi, do nothing more than fold a list of numbers into one, by adding or by multiplying. The adding runs almost the whole field. The multiplying hides a flaw that destroys training runs after a few dozen steps, and the way out is the single most important piece of arithmetic in machine learning, and the reason logarithms exist at all.

From one output to one number

A function commits to one output for each input. So why is that not the end of the story?

Because we almost never ask a function for a single output. We sweep it across a whole collection of inputs and are left holding a list. A loss function returns an error for every example in the dataset: a thousand examples, a thousand errors. A language model returns a probability for every word it predicts: a ten-word sentence, ten probabilities. A vector is itself a list, one number per dimension. And a list is not an answer. What we want is one number drawn out of it: the total error, the score of the sentence, the length of the vector.

So the operation sitting underneath almost everything is this: take a list of numbers and collapse it into one. What are the ways to do that?

There are two basic ones, and you have used both since long before you could name them. You can add the list, or you can multiply it. Add five prices and you have a bill. Multiply the chances of three coin flips and you have the chance they all land heads, 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125. These two ways of folding a list are so common that each earned its own symbol: a capital sigma, Σ, for "add this list," and a capital pi, Π, for "multiply this list." That is the whole connection to Class 6: functions produce lists of values, and sigma and pi are how a list becomes a single number again.

A function gives a list; sigma folds it into one number

Class 6 built the left half, a function turning inputs into outputs. This class is the right half, folding that list of outputs back into one number, by adding (sigma) or multiplying (pi).

Section 1 · Sigma: add up a list

To add a list of numbers in code, you write a loop with a running total:

add up the list a = [2, 5, 3, 7, 1]total = 0 for i in range(len(a)): total = total + a[i] # total is now 18

The sigma is that loop, written in one stroke:

read aloud: "the sum, for i from 1 to n, of a-sub-i"total = Σ a i (i from 1 to n)

Every part of the symbol matches a part of the loop, and nothing else is hidden in it.

Reading the sigma, part by part

It reads: "let i count from 1 up to n, and add a-sub-i each time." The loop above, in one symbol.

Expanding it just means writing the terms out: Σ ai is a1 + a2 + a3 + a4 + a5, which for our list is 2 + 5 + 3 + 7 + 1 = 18. Build any sum or product below and the running value updates term by term, exactly as the loop would.

Flip to Π and the same list is multiplied instead. For 0.5, 0.5, 0.5 under multiplication the running value shrinks, which is the seed of Section 3.

why this matters for ML

Adding up a list is, quietly, most of what a model computes. It shows up in three places that hold up nearly everything later.

An average

Your model has just made a thousand predictions, and you measured how far off each one was. How wrong is the model? You cannot answer from a thousand separate numbers; you need a single one. So you add the errors and divide by how many there are. The dividing makes it an average; the adding is a sum.

Folding many errors into one number

Written out it is (1/n) Σ errori: the Σ adds the errors, errori is the error on the i-th example, and the (1/n) turns the total into a typical value. That number is what everyone calls the loss, and the entire activity of training is moving it downward.

A dot product

You have a list of inputs, and beside it a list of weights the model has learned, one per input, each saying how much that input should count toward the answer. How do you fold the two lists into a single score? You pair them up by position, multiply each pair, and add the products.

A dot product: pair up, multiply, then add

In symbols this is Σ wi xi. Now look closely at when the score comes out large. It is large precisely when big inputs line up with big weights, when the two lists point the same way; it is small when they pull in opposite directions. A dot product is a measure of how much two lists agree. That single idea is why it is everywhere: a neuron is one dot product, scoring an input against the weights it has learned to look for, a layer is many neurons side by side, and a deep network is layers of them, so "agreement, summed" is the innermost act of the entire machine, run billions of times. The same operation later measures how similar two vectors are, which is how models compare meanings.

An expected value

Sometimes an outcome is uncertain and you want to know what to expect on average. Say a scratchcard pays nothing 70% of the time, pays $5 20% of the time, and pays $50 the other 10%. What is one card worth?

A first guess might be to average the three prizes, (0 + 5 + 50) / 3 ≈ $18. But that is too high, because $0 happens far more often than $50, and a plain average treats them as equally common. The honest way is to imagine playing many times. Over 100 cards you would expect about 70 of them to pay nothing, 20 to pay $5, and 10 to pay $50.

What one scratchcard is worth

So a card is worth $6 on average, and the way we found it was to weight each prize by how often it happens and add: 0.7×0 + 0.2×5 + 0.1×50 = 6. Each outcome multiplied by its probability, all summed. That is the expected value, written Σ pi xi, and it is just the dot product of the outcomes with their probabilities. Weighting values by how likely they are, instead of treating them as equally common, is how you take an average when some things happen more than others, and it runs all through probability and learning.

Average, dot product, expected value: each is a list folded by adding. Read a Σ as a for-loop that adds, and most of the symbols in a paper turn back into code you already understand.

Section 2 · Pi: multiply a list

Pi is the same machine as sigma with one part swapped. The running value starts at 1 instead of 0, and each step multiplies instead of adds:

multiply the list a = [2, 3, 4]running = 1 for i in range(len(a)): running = running * a[i] # running is now 24

You read Π ai exactly like the sigma, with one word changed: the big pi means "multiply everything to my right." The mechanics are that simple. The real question is why we would multiply a list rather than add it.

In Section 1 we combined a list by adding. So why not always add? Probabilities are the clearest case where adding is simply the wrong tool. Take a fair coin and ask for the chance of heads three times in a row. Each flip is a 0.5 chance. Add them and you get 1.5, which is impossible: nothing has a 150% chance. Adding has broken. The correct way to combine the chances of a sequence is to multiply them.

For probabilities, adding breaks and multiplying is right

So pi is the tool for combining probabilities, the way sigma was the tool for combining sizes. But that just moves the question: why does multiplying give the chance of a sequence? It is worth seeing exactly why, because the reason is the whole reason pi matters.

why this matters for ML

Why "all of them happen" is a multiplication

If two uncertain things might each happen, what is the chance they both do? Take a concrete pair. It rains on 30% of days. And on rainy days, half the time you forget your umbrella. Out of 100 days, how many are both rainy and umbrella-less?

Walk it in two steps. First, 30 of the 100 days are rainy. Then, of those 30 rainy days, half, so 15, are the ones you also forgot the umbrella. So it happens on 15 days in 100, a chance of 0.15. Notice exactly how we got 15: we took 30% of the days, and then 50% of those. A fraction of a fraction. And taking a fraction of a fraction is multiplying: 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15.

A fraction of a fraction is a multiplication

That is the rule. When events are independent, meaning one happening does not change the odds of another, the chance that they all happen is the product of their separate chances, one factor for each "and also." So for a list of probabilities, the product Π pi finally has a clear meaning: it is the chance that all of them happen at once. And since every factor is below 1, the product shrinks with each new one, which becomes the seed of Section 3. First, though, this single meaning grows into the most important number in training.

Step 1: a model assigns a probability to what happens

Before we can judge a model, we have to be clear what a model even is here. A model is a guess about how the world behaves, and the simplest one imaginable is a single number: the chance of rain. Suppose a model claims rain has a 30% chance. That one number is a complete model, because it tells you the probability of either thing that can happen on a day: rain with probability 0.3, dry with probability 0.7.

With a model in hand, you can ask how probable any particular stretch of weather is. Take three days that went rain, then dry, then rain. The model gave rain a 0.3 chance, dry a 0.7 chance, and rain again 0.3, and by the multiplying rule the chance of that exact three-day run is their product:

One model, one stretch of weather

So a model, plus a stretch of days, gives back one number: how probable the model thought that exact weather was. That is the model running in its natural direction, from a known model to the chance of an outcome. Hold onto that, because the whole idea of learning comes from turning it around.

Step 2: in real life we have the days, not the model

That example quietly cheated: it started by handing you the model, the 30% rain chance. Real life never does. Nobody tells you a new city's true rain chance. What you actually have is the opposite: the days already happened, you watched them, and the model is the unknown you are trying to find. The question you really face is the reverse of Step 1:

Given the days we actually saw, which model should we believe?

This reversal is the heart of learning from data, so it is worth saying plainly. In Step 1 the model was known and the outcome was the question. Now the outcome is known, it is fixed, it happened, and the model is the question.

Step 3: score a candidate by how probable it makes what you saw

We cannot look up the true model. But we can do something almost as good: take any candidate model we like and ask it the Step 1 question about the data we already have. If this candidate were the truth, how probable was the week we actually saw? A candidate under which the observed week comes out very probable is a believable explanation of it. A candidate under which the week was nearly impossible is not.

Make it concrete. Say the week you watched had two rainy days and three dry ones. Try a candidate that believes rain is rare, a 10% chance. Ask it how probable that week was, the Step 1 way, multiplying the chance it gave to each day:

candidate: rain chance 10%, scoring the observed week (2 rainy, 3 dry)0.1 × 0.1 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.0073

Almost nothing. This candidate is shocked that it rained twice, because it believed rain almost never comes. Under it, the week we saw looks like a fluke, so it is a poor explanation of the data.

Step 4: compare two candidates

One score on its own means little; the score earns its keep when you compare. So try a second candidate that believes rain is much more common, a 40% chance, and score the same week the same way. Here are both, side by side on the identical five days:

The same week, scored by two candidate models

The 40% candidate gives the week 0.0346, nearly five times the 10% candidate's 0.0073. It is barely surprised by what happened, so it explains the week far better. That score, the probability a candidate model assigns to the data you actually saw, has a name: the likelihood. The rule for using it is the one we just watched: the higher a model's likelihood, the better it fits the data.

Step 5: test them all, and keep the best

Two candidates were enough to see the idea. To actually learn the rain chance, do not stop at two, let the candidate take every value between 0 and 1, and score each one by the likelihood, the probability it gives the week you saw. A few of them:

  • p = 0.1: 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.0073. Too low, shocked by the two rainy days.
  • p = 0.4: 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.6 × 0.6 × 0.6 = 0.0346. Comfortable with both the rain and the dry.
  • p = 0.8: 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.0051. Too high, shocked by the three dry days.

Plot the likelihood for every value of p and the scores rise to a single clear peak:

Scoring every candidate by the probability it gives the observed week

The peak sits exactly at p = 0.4, which is just two rainy days out of five, the answer you would have guessed without any of this. That agreement is the reassurance that the method is sane. The real prize is the recipe it followed, choose the model that makes the observed data most probable, because that recipe does not care how complicated the model is. It works the same when the model is a single rain chance and when it is the billions of numbers inside a neural network. Choosing a model this way is called maximum likelihood, and most of training is a version of it.

One feeling to carry out of all five steps: the likelihood measures how unsurprised a model is by reality. A model that fits the world expects what happens and gives it a high probability; a poor one is caught off guard and gives it a low one. Training is the search for the model that finds the world least surprising.

A language model is this exact idea with words in place of weather. Its model is not one rain chance but billions of weights, and at each position it gives a probability to the word that actually came next. The likelihood of a whole sentence is the product of those per-word probabilities, Π pi, and training adjusts the billions of weights to make the real text as probable as it can, the product as large as it can. A three-word sentence the model rated 0.2, then 0.1, then 0.5 has likelihood 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.01. So this product is the exact quantity training works to push upward, which is what turns the next section into a genuine problem.

Section 3 · The product that vanishes

Section 2 left us with the likelihood, a product of probabilities that training works to make as large as it can. A real dataset pours hundreds or thousands of probabilities into that product. Before any maximizing can happen, a computer has to actually compute it, and that is where a quiet disaster is waiting.

A product of hundreds of probabilities, each a number between 0 and 1. What does a real computer actually hold at the end?

The true value shrinks fast, but never to zero

Start with the friendliest product there is, coin flips. The chance of heads n times in a row is 0.5 multiplied by itself n times, and every extra flip halves the result. Ten flips in a row is about one in a thousand; twenty is about one in a million. The number drops with astonishing speed. But notice the one thing that stays true the whole way down: it is never zero. Halving a positive number leaves a positive number, always. On paper the product keeps shrinking forever and never actually reaches zero.

Halving over and over: tiny, but never zero

On a real chip, it hits exactly zero

Now stop doing it on paper and do it on the chip from Unit 1, which stores numbers as a float. Keep halving and count the flips:

on the bench: probability of n heads in a row, in floatfloat p = 1.0f; for (int n = 1; n <= 400; n++) p *= 0.5f; // halve it, again and again // p becomes EXACTLY 0.0 after only 150 flips, // even though the true value 0.5^150 is about 7e-46: tiny, but NOT zero

Not a small number. A flat, exact 0. And not after thousands of steps, after about 150. A real likelihood is worse still, because word and data probabilities sit well below one-half and there are hundreds of them. With each probability around 0.1, the product underflows to zero after only 46 of them. The paper math says positive forever; the chip says zero within a paragraph.

Why exactly zero, and not just very small?

The reason is the single most useful idea in this section, and it is the hinge that the fix in Section 4 turns on. Recall from Unit 1 how a float actually stores a number. It does not keep a long string of digits. It keeps a few significant digits and, separately, an exponent that says where the decimal point sits, a value times a power of two. Those are two different budgets, one for the digits and one for the exponent.

A float is digits times a power, and only the exponent runs out

In our shrinking product, the digits were never in trouble. The exponent was. A float's exponent reaches down only to about 10-38, and a little further, to roughly 10-45, through some special small values. But our product needed an exponent near 10-46, then 10-150, then beyond. It did not run out of digits; it ran out of exponent. Once a step lands past the smallest exponent the float can write, there is no number left for it to be, so it snaps to 0. This is the bottom cliff from Class 4, underflow, and a product of small numbers runs off it within a few dozen steps.

The product walks off the bottom of what a float can hold

Why a zero here is a catastrophe, not a rounding slip

A tiny number rounded to zero sounds harmless. It is not, and the reason reaches back to what learning actually does. From Section 2, training compares candidate models by their likelihoods and climbs toward the ones that score higher. The differences between those scores are the entire signal it follows. Underflow erases that signal. A decent model might give the data a likelihood near 10-50 and a slightly better one near 10-48, but both fall past the floor and are stored as the same flat 0. A genuinely good model and a hopeless one, 10-50 against 10-300, also both read as 0. The numbers that told these models apart are gone, and once every likelihood is pinned at zero there is no "higher" left to climb toward. Training goes blind: no direction looks any better than another, so the model cannot tell how to improve, and so it does not.

There is an obvious thing to try, and it is worth following because it leads exactly where the next section goes. The number is too small to store, so do not store the number, store its logarithm instead. The log of a tiny positive value is a manageable negative one, with no underflow anywhere in sight. That instinct is the right one. But done naively it arrives one step too late. If you let the probabilities multiply all the way down to a stored 0 and only then take the log, you are computing log(0), which is -∞, the infinity from Class 4. That is worse than the zero it replaced: it pours into the loss and turns the gradients to NaN, killing the run outright rather than merely starving it of signal.

So the lesson is sharp, and it sets the whole task of Section 4. Taking the log is the answer, but only if you take it before the product is ever formed, so the doomed multiplication never happens in the first place. The trick will be to turn the product into something the log can ride through safely from the start.

why this matters for ML

This is not a rare corner you can dodge with care. A likelihood multiplies one probability for every example in the data, and any real dataset has far more than the few dozen factors it takes to underflow, so the naive product is all but guaranteed to collapse to zero. The single most important quantity in training cannot be computed the way its definition reads. That is a real wall, and the rest of the class is about getting over it. Section 4 · The fix: turn the product into a sum

Section 3 ended with a sharp demand: take the log before the product is ever formed, so the doomed multiplication never happens. At first that sounds impossible. The likelihood is a product, and the log seems to come afterward, once the product already exists.

How can you take the log of a product without first building the product?

The entire fix rests on one property of logarithms. Once it is in view, the impossible-sounding demand turns routine.

The one property of logarithms

A logarithm answers a plain question: what power is this number? Working in powers of ten, log 1000 = 3, because 1000 = 103. The log of a number is its exponent, loosely the count of its zeros.

Now watch what multiplication does to exponents. 102 × 103 = 105, and the exponent 5 is simply 2 + 3: multiplying the two numbers added their exponents. And since a log just is the exponent, that is the same as saying the log of a product equals the sum of the logs.

A log is an exponent, and multiplying adds exponents

So log(a × b) = log a + log b. It is not a rule to memorize; it is only "exponents add when you multiply," stated for any two numbers instead of round powers of ten. And there is something almost uncanny about it. We were hunting for a function that turns multiplication into addition, and the logarithm is essentially the only continuous function that does. That is exactly why logs were invented four centuries ago, to turn the punishing multiplications of astronomy and navigation into additions a person could carry out by hand. Our underflow is the same old problem in modern dress, and it has the same answer.

The product becomes a sum

Apply that property across the whole likelihood, every probability at once, and the product collapses into a sum:

The log turns the product into a sum

A product of n probabilities, a pi, becomes a sum of n logs, a sigma. This is the moment Sections 1 and 2 were quietly building toward: the fix literally turns the one symbol into the other. And here is how it meets Section 3's demand exactly. You do not multiply the probabilities and then take a log. You take the log of each probability first, getting a modest number from each, and add those up. The product is never formed, so it can never underflow. The log is applied before the multiplication that would have sunk it, which was the whole requirement.

Why the sum cannot underflow

Look at the size of the numbers you now carry. log(0.1) is about -2.3; log(0.5) about -0.69. Add a few hundred of them and you land somewhere around -200 to -700, a number a float holds without a flicker. There is no tiny value anywhere to lose, because we only ever add ordinary ones. Here is the same three-probability likelihood from Section 2, done both ways:

And -4.61 is exactly log(0.01): the sum of logs is the log of the product, carried in a form the machine can hold.

There is a deeper way to see why this buys so much room, straight from Section 3. A float spends only about eight of its bits on the exponent, which is why its reach stops near 10-38 and a product runs out of exponent so fast. Taking the log moves that exponent out of the cramped exponent field and into the float's full value, the whole significant-digits budget, a vastly larger space. We trade a little precision we do not need for an enormous range we very much do. The log-likelihood is the product's exponent, finally given enough room to breathe.

Does it crown a different winner?

It is worth pausing on what we have quietly done, because it should make you uneasy. We set out to make the likelihood as large as possible. Now we are making the sum of its logs as large as possible instead, and those are not the same number. A decent model might give the data a likelihood of 0.01, but its log-likelihood is log(0.01) = -4.6, a negative number. We have swapped the target we chase. So the fair question is whether the swap is harmless: when we climb toward the largest sum of logs, are we still climbing toward the best model, or could it quietly hand the prize to a worse one?

Test it on two models before trusting it on a billion. Model A gives the observed data a likelihood of 0.01; model B gives it 0.0001. A is clearly the better fit, a hundred times more likely. Now compare them the new way, by their logs: log(0.01) = -4.6 and log(0.0001) = -9.2. Both are negative, and the better model's log, -4.6, is the larger of the two because it sits closer to zero. A is still ahead. The swap did not reorder them.

One case is not a proof, but the reason it worked is general. The logarithm has a single feature that settles every case at once: it only ever climbs.

The log only ever climbs, so it never changes the winner

Feed the log a bigger input and you always get a bigger output; its graph rises from left to right and never once turns back down. So whenever one likelihood is larger than another, its log is larger too, with no exceptions. Taking logs stretches and shifts the numbers, but it never swaps their order. The model sitting at the top of the likelihood ranking is still at the top of the log-likelihood ranking. Maximizing the sum of logs therefore lands on the exact same model as maximizing the likelihood would have, except by a route that never goes near the underflow cliff. We get the right winner and a number the machine can actually hold.

why this matters for ML

The quantity Σ log(pi) is the log-likelihood, and training maximizes it. Flip its sign and average it and you have the cross-entropy, also called the negative log-likelihood loss, -(1/n) Σ log(pi), the loss behind essentially every classifier and language model. That formula is not arbitrary. It is a sum of logs precisely so the product it stands for cannot underflow to zero and take the run with it. The two quiet symbols from the opening, sigma and pi, joined by a logarithm, are the difference between a model that learns and one that dies on its first batch.

What to carry out of Class 7

Functions hand us lists of values, and we constantly need to fold a list into one number. Sigma does it by adding, pi by multiplying, and each symbol only records where to start, where to stop, and the term to combine, the loops you already write. Adding runs the field: a loss is an average, a neuron is a dot product measuring how much an input agrees with its weights, an expectation is a weighted sum. Multiplying gives the likelihood, the probability a model assigns to the data actually seen, a measure of how unsurprised it is by reality, which training pushes upward. But a product of many probabilities underflows to a hard zero on real hardware, because a float runs out of room in its exponent, the bottom cliff from Class 4, and that zero poisons training through log(0) = -∞. The escape is to carry the exponent rather than the value, which is what a logarithm is, turning log(a × b) = log a + log b into log(Π pi) = Σ log pi: the dangerous product becomes a safe sum of modest numbers that preserves every comparison. That sum is the log-likelihood every loss function is quietly built from.

A product of probabilities is the right idea and the wrong computation. A float runs out of exponent and the product collapses to zero. Carry its exponent instead, which is exactly what taking a logarithm does, and the pi becomes a sigma, the tiny number becomes a sum of ordinary ones, and the likelihood at the heart of machine learning becomes something a finite machine can hold.
