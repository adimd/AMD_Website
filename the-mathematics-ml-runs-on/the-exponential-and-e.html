The Mathematics ML Runs On · Class 10

The Number Growth Can’t Beat

Push growth as hard as you possibly can and it stops at 2.718. Not because something gets in the way, but because that is as far as compounding goes. The number is called \(e\), a 32-bit chip cannot count up to it, and it is the shape of every probability your network computes.

Imagine a colony of cells that grows by 100% in an hour. If all that growth showed up in one lump at the end, the colony would double. One becomes two, and you are done.

Growth does not wait for the end of the hour, though. A cell that buds off a daughter at the half-hour mark spends the back half of the hour as two cells, both growing. What you earned early starts earning on its own. The colony finishes above double.

So how much above? This is where it gets strange. If growing sooner helps, then growing constantly should help more, and there is no obvious reason for that to ever stop paying off. You would expect the colony to run away to something enormous. It does not. Continuous 100% growth over an hour multiplies by 2.718, and slicing the growth finer buys you nothing past that. There is a wall, and the number painted on it is one of the two or three most important constants in all of mathematics.

Faster and faster compounding should pay off forever. Instead it hits a ceiling, and that ceiling is \(e\).

the thread
One move runs this entire class: multiply, then multiply again. That is all any of it is. Run that move finer and finer and it settles on \(e\). Run it for any stretch of time you like and it becomes \(e^{x}\). Run it too far in either direction and a 32-bit float gives out. Same move every time. Watch what changes around it.

Where the wall comes from

Here is that idea with nothing in it but arithmetic. Start with a quantity of 1, and grow it by 100% over one period.

One lump at the end multiplies you by 2. Now split the period in half and pay out the first 50% at the midpoint, early enough that it has time to grow too. At the halfway mark you are holding 1.5. That 1.5 then grows by its own 50%, and you land on \(1.5 \times 1.5 = 2.25\). Two pieces already beat one lump, because the first piece got a head start.

Keep splitting. Four pieces of 25% reach 2.44, twelve pieces reach 2.61, and a piece a day for a year reaches 2.71. Every finer split does a shade better than the last, for the same reason: each new piece joins the compounding sooner. Split 100% into \(n\) pieces and you multiply by \(\left(1 + \tfrac1n\right)\) once per piece, \(n\) times over, which is \(\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^{n}\).

more pieces, but the total flattens against \(e\)

Now take the dial yourself. Push \(n\) up and watch the total climb.

split 100% into more pieces and watch it press against the ceiling
pieces n = 1
One lump of 100% growth just doubles you. Start splitting it into pieces.

Two things happen here. The first is what you were promised: the value rises as you split finer. The second is the surprise. It rises more and more slowly, then flattens against a ceiling it never crosses. Crank \(n\) into the thousands and the extra pieces buy you almost nothing. You are closing in on one specific number.

\( e \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{n}\right)^{n} \;=\; 2.718281828\ldots \)

Why a ceiling instead of a runaway? Because each finer split multiplies by a thinner sliver above 1, so each piece adds less than the piece before it. The first split added 0.25. The next added 0.12, then 0.07, and shrinking gains like that sum to something finite. That finite total is \(e\), about 36% more than plain doubling, and no amount of pushing gets you a fraction past it.

So \(e\) is not a constant anybody chose. It is the answer to a plain question about how far growth can compound on itself, and everything else in this class falls out of that answer.

The chip that can’t count to e

That formula is also a recipe, and a recipe is something you can hand to a computer. Pick a big \(n\), compute \(\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^{n}\), watch it converge. So point the ESP32-C3 at it and turn \(n\) up. In 64-bit double precision the answer marches toward 2.71828 exactly as it should. In the 32-bit float the chip would rather be using, something else happens.

compute_e_float32.c// (1 + 1/n)^n in double vs single precision, as n climbs long ns[] = {1000, 1000000, 10000000, 16777216, 100000000}; printf("n double float\n"); for (int i = 0; i < 5; i++) { long n = ns[i]; double e64 = pow(1.0 + 1.0/n, n); float step = (float)(1.0 + 1.0/n); // 1 + 1/n rounded to 32-bit float double e32 = pow(step, n); printf("%-13ld%.4f %.4f\n", n, e64, e32); }

The float column never settles. It slips down to 2.59, overshoots to 3.29, never homes in on anything, and then at one exact value of \(n\) it drops to 1 and stays there. The definition of \(e\), run in single precision, hands you back 1.

The culprit is the mantissa from Class 2. A 32-bit float carries 24 bits of precision, so the smallest thing it can add to 1 is \(2^{-24}\). The instant your step \(\tfrac1n\) shrinks below that, \(1 + \tfrac1n\) rounds straight back to exactly 1, and 1 raised to any power at all is 1. That happens the moment \(n\) passes \(2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216\), a threshold you can read directly off the width of the float.

The harder you push for accuracy, the worse the answer gets. That is a strange sentence to have to write, and it is why no serious library computes \(e\) from its own definition. Which leaves you with a question: if grinding the limit does not work, how does anything ever get \(e\) at all? Nobody treats \(e\) as a limit. They treat it as a function and evaluate the function.

From a number to a dial

So far \(e\) is one number, what a single full unit of continuous growth does to you. But growth does not always arrive in one full unit. Sometimes you grow for half a period, sometimes for three. Make the amount of growth a dial, call it \(x\), and what you get is \(e^{x}\): grow continuously for \(x\) worth, and that is your multiplier. Slide it and read it off.

the dial: \(e^{x}\) for any amount of growth \(x\)
x = 1.0

Nothing on the dial, \(e^{0} = 1\), and you end where you began. One unit gets you 2.718, the wall from the opening. Two units gets you 7.39, the growth now compounding on the growth. Turn the dial the other way and growth becomes decay: \(e^{-1} = 0.37\), then \(e^{-2} = 0.14\), sliding toward zero.

Toward it, never onto it. \(e^{x}\) is positive for every input there is. You can decay your way as close to zero as you like and never arrive, and you certainly cannot cross. Keep that in your pocket, because two sections from now it is the only reason probabilities work at all.

There is one more property, and it is the reason \(e^{x}\) and not some other curve runs the rest of this course. Grow for a stretch \(a\), then grow for a further stretch \(b\). You have to land where you would have landed by growing for \(a+b\) in one go. The first route is \(e^{a}\) times \(e^{b}\). The second is \(e^{a+b}\). So they are the same thing:

\( e^{a+b} \;=\; e^{a} \times e^{b} \)

Read that twice, because the next class turns on it. Addition on the dial becomes multiplication in the value, which is to say \(e^{x}\) converts sums into products. Run it backwards and it converts products into sums, and that is precisely how you rescue a long chain of probabilities before it collapses. \(e^{x}\) is a door. The logarithm is the same door, opened the other way.

The shape of a probability

Here is the payoff for spending a whole class on one constant. A network spends its life producing raw scores, any numbers at all, positive or negative, and it has to keep turning those scores into probabilities. A probability demands two things and will not negotiate on either: stay positive, and change smoothly when the score changes. You have already built the one familiar function that does both.

Feed a single score into the sigmoid, \(1/(1+e^{-x})\), and slide it around. That always-positive \(e^{-x}\) is doing all the work.

the sigmoid folds any score into a probability
score x = 0.0

A big positive score drives \(e^{-x}\) toward zero, so the output climbs toward 1. A big negative score makes \(e^{-x}\) explode, so the output sinks toward 0. A score of zero lands exactly on one half. The entire infinite number line gets folded, smoothly, into the strip between 0 and 1 where a probability is allowed to live, and \(e^{x}\) is what does the folding.

The same function handles a whole list of scores at once. The softmax raises \(e\) to each score and divides by the total, \(p_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}\). Every \(e^{z}\) is positive, so every output is positive. You divided by the sum, so they add to 1. A row of arbitrary numbers walks out as a clean distribution.

And the bell curve, the thing that describes very nearly every measurement anyone has ever taken, is \(e^{-x^{2}/2}\). Same exponential, bent around a centre.

the same \(e^{x}\), hiding in three different jobs
the same number, three coats
Three jobs, one function, and not by coincidence. Each of them needs a quantity that stays positive everywhere and bends smoothly, and continuous growth is the only natural source of both at once. So the sigmoid, the softmax, and the bell curve were never design decisions anyone argued over. They are the same \(e^{x}\) in three coats.

Anywhere a model touches a probability, \(e\) got there first. Which would make a tidy ending, if \(e\) came free on a real chip. It does not.

The two cliffs

\(e^{x}\) climbs the way the colony climbed, only harder, and a 32-bit float runs out of room a little past \(3.4 \times 10^{38}\). \(e^{88}\) fits. \(e^{89}\) does not, and the chip hands you infinity. Scores that big are unremarkable inside a real model, so a softmax written the obvious way breaks the first time it meets one: the large \(e^{z}\) terms all become infinity, and infinity over infinity is nothing at all.

The fix is one line, and it leans entirely on that identity from two sections back. Before you exponentiate, subtract the largest score from every score. By the identity, that multiplies the top and the bottom of the softmax by the same factor, so every probability comes out exactly as it was. But the largest exponent is now \(e^{0} = 1\), and nothing has room to overflow. Every softmax in every serious library on earth does this.

softmax_overflow.c// e^x overflows a 32-bit float just past x = 88 int xs[] = {88, 89, 90}; for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) printf("e^%d = %g\n", xs[i], expf(xs[i])); // expf: exp in 32-bit float float z[] = {90, 89, 88}; printf("naive: %g, %g, %g\n", expf(z[0]), expf(z[1]), expf(z[2])); // two overflow to inf float m = z[0]; // the largest score for (int i = 1; i < 3; i++) if (z[i] > m) m = z[i]; float tot = 0, p[3]; for (int i = 0; i < 3; i++) { p[i] = expf(z[i] - m); tot += p[i]; } printf("safe : %.3f, %.3f, %.3f\n", p[0]/tot, p[1]/tot, p[2]/tot);

There is a second cliff at the other end, and it is the colony running backwards. To score a whole sentence, a model multiplies one small probability per word, hundreds of them in a row, and \(e^{x}\) dives downward as hard as it climbs upward. Multiply enough numbers below 1 and the product slips under the smallest value a float can hold and lands on a flat zero. Both failures are the same move pushed too far: double 1 sixty-three times and you erupt past \(9.2 \times 10^{18}\), multiply 0.1 by itself forty-six times and in a float you have nothing left.

two_directions.c// the same growth, both ways double up = 1; for (int i = 0; i < 63; i++) up *= 2; // double 63 times: it erupts printf("up (x2, 63 steps): %.2e\n", up); float down = 1; for (int i = 0; i < 46; i++) down *= 0.1f; // multiply by 0.1, 46 times: it vanishes printf("down (x0.1, 46 steps): %g\n", down);

Overflow at the top, underflow at the bottom, and one cure for both. The logarithm pulls giant numbers and tiny ones alike back into a range the chip can hold, and it can do that because of the identity you just used: it turns the products into sums. That is the whole of the next class.

Even nowhere near a cliff, though, \(e^{x}\) is expensive. A processor has an instruction for adding and an instruction for multiplying, and none at all for \(e^{x}\), so it builds every single value out of a long polynomial. On the FPU-less ESP32-C3, every one of those multiplies is faked in software on top of that. The most common function in machine learning is one of the priciest things the little chip ever does, and Class 13 adds up that bill in cycles.

So: multiply, then multiply again. Run that move as finely as it can be run and it does not run away, it settles, on \(e\), 2.718 and not a hair past it. Run it for any stretch you like and it becomes \(e^{x}\), positive everywhere and able to turn sums into products, which happens to be the exact job description of a probability, which is why it sits underneath every sigmoid and softmax and bell curve a network draws. Run it too far and the machine gives out, in three different ways: too fine to feel in single precision, too steep to raise to a real score, too smooth to have earned its own instruction. One move. The whole machine leaning on it, and straining.

Next class takes that door the other way. The logarithm undoes the exponential, turns its runaway products back into sums and its overflows back into ordinary numbers, and is quietly the single most useful line of arithmetic in the whole field.
