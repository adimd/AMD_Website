Can a Switch Do Mathematics?

We can represent any number in binary. We can convert in both directions. We know why binary won. But a representation that just sits there is useless. Something has to manipulate it. This class is about how that happens, and it begins with a question that sounds absurd until you see the answer.

Class 2 left us with a clear picture of binary representation. Every number has a binary equivalent. We can convert in both directions. We know why binary won, why two voltage states separated by a wide gap are almost immune to noise. The representation problem is solved.

But storage is not computation. A filing cabinet full of records does not think. Numbers sitting in memory as voltage levels just sit there. Something has to manipulate them. The machine needs to add two numbers, compare them, shift their bits, decide which way to branch. The question that has been sitting quietly since Class 1 can no longer be ignored.

What physical mechanism takes bits in and produces a result out?

The answer begins not with electronics but with a mid-nineteenth century Irish mathematician who was trying to do something that had nothing to do with computers at all.

George Boole and the algebra of thought

George Boole
1815 – 1864  ·  Cork, Ireland
Self-taught mathematician. Son of a cobbler. Never attended university as a student but became a professor of mathematics at Queen's College Cork. His 1854 book An Investigation of the Laws of Thought proposed that logical reasoning could be reduced to a system of algebra. He was trying to mechanise logic, not build computers. He died of pneumonia at 49, never knowing what his algebra would one day become.

In 1854, Boole published a remarkable claim. All logical reasoning could be captured using just three operations on values that are either true or false. He called them AND, OR, and NOT. These three operations follow consistent mathematical laws, like addition and multiplication, but radically simpler. No fractions, no infinities. Just true and false. One and zero.

Mathematicians found it elegant. Most of the world found it a curiosity. Logic was logic. Mathematics was mathematics. Boole had built a bridge between them, but the bridge appeared to lead nowhere practical.

It sat there for 83 years.

Claude Shannon's impossible connection

Claude Shannon
1916 – 2001  ·  Michigan, USA
Electrical engineer and mathematician at MIT and Bell Labs. His 1937 master's thesis is widely called the most important master's thesis of the 20th century, and it was only the beginning. What he did after it, and the strange way he worked, are covered just below.

In 1937 a 21-year-old MIT graduate student handed in a thesis titled A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits. The title is unexciting. What it said was not.

Shannon was studying telephone relay circuits, the electromechanical switches used to route phone calls. A relay is a two-state device. Open or closed. Shannon noticed that Boole's algebra of true and false had exactly the same structure as the algebra of open and closed switches.

Boole's AND: result is true only when both inputs are true. A relay circuit with two switches in series: current flows only when both switches are closed. Same rule. Boole's OR: result is true when at least one input is true. Two switches in parallel: current flows when at least one is closed. Again identical.

Boole was a logician working on the philosophy of reasoning. Telephone engineers were routing calls. Neither field had reason to read the other. Shannon happened to know both. He saw what everyone else had missed. And he wrote it down.

Shannon did not invent the transistor or the computer. He did something more fundamental: he proved that a light switch is all you need to do mathematics.

The implication changes everything. If Boolean algebra and switching circuits are the same thing, then any logical statement you can express in Boole's algebra you can build as a physical circuit. And any computation can be expressed in Boolean algebra. Which means any computation can be built from switches. In 1937 those switches were relays. Within a decade, vacuum tubes. Within two decades, transistors etched into silicon. The medium changed. The mathematics did not.

What happened to Shannon after that thesis is worth knowing, because the same mind that spotted the switch hiding inside a syllogism went on to invent the thing that makes this entire course possible.

The man who measured information

In 1948 Shannon published a paper called A Mathematical Theory of Communication. Before that paper, nobody could say what information actually was. You could feel that a long telegram carried more than a short one, that a surprising message told you more than a predictable one, but there was no way to put a number on any of it. Shannon gave it a number. He defined the fundamental unit of information and named it the bit. One bit is the amount of information in a single yes or no answer when both answers are equally likely. Anything, a sentence, a photograph, a song, a strand of DNA, can be measured as some number of bits.

This is the same bit from Class 2. The thing that is either 0 or 1, high or low, on or off. Shannon did not merely name it. He proved deep things about it. He showed that any communication channel, a copper wire, a radio link, a fibre running under the ocean, has a hard maximum capacity measured in bits per second, and that you can push information right up to that limit with almost no errors even when the channel is noisy, as long as you encode the message cleverly. That result, the noisy channel coding theorem, is the reason your phone call stays clear, your download arrives uncorrupted, and a scratched disc still plays. Every error-correcting code, every compression format, every modem that has ever existed descends from that one paper.

He was 32. At 21 he had shown that logic is electricity. Now he had shown that information is a physical, measurable quantity with limits as firm as the speed of light. The bit stopped being a convenience of engineers. It became the atom of the digital world.

Why the eccentricity was the point

Shannon was, by every account, strange in the most productive way a person can be. He rode a unicycle through the corridors of Bell Labs, and because that was not quite interesting enough, he taught himself to juggle while riding it. He built a machine whose only function was to switch itself off: you flip the switch, a small mechanical hand emerges from the lid, flips it back, and withdraws. He built a flame-throwing trumpet, a rocket-powered frisbee, and, with the mathematician Ed Thorp, a wearable computer to beat the roulette wheel in Las Vegas. His house was full of half-finished gadgets, and he treated tinkering and theorem-proving as the same activity.

It is easy to file all this under harmless quirk, the great man who happened to juggle. I think that gets it exactly backwards. The unicycle and the theorems came from the same place. Shannon's central move, seeing that a telephone relay and a logical argument are the same object, is the kind of thing you can only notice if your mind refuses to respect the fences between subjects. A person who takes juggling as seriously as algebra is a person who has already worked out that balance, timing, and pattern are one problem wearing different clothes. The play was not a break from the work. It was the work, running in another medium.

Theseus, the mouse that learned

In 1950 Shannon built a mechanical mouse called Theseus and set it loose in a maze. The maze sat on a board with walls you could rearrange by hand. Underneath, a grid of relays, the same switching relays from his thesis, drove a magnet that tugged the mouse from square to square. On its first run the mouse bumped into walls, backed up, tried other directions, and slowly felt its way to the goal. That much is not surprising. This part is. On the second run it went straight to the goal without a single wrong turn. It had remembered the route.

And if you reached in and moved the walls, the mouse would drive confidently into the changed section, discover that its memory no longer matched the world, explore the new layout, and learn the new path. A machine, built from relays, that improved its own behaviour by storing what it had experienced and reusing it. That is not a metaphor for learning. It is learning, in its plainest mechanical form. Theseus is one of the earliest working demonstrations of a machine that learns from trial and error, and the idea beating at its centre, hold some memory of the world, update it from experience, do better next time, is the same idea sitting underneath modern machine learning.

Shannon and Theseus. The mouse explores a maze, remembers the route, and drives straight to the goal on the next run. Relays doing what we would now call machine learning, in 1950. Notice that the memory is built from exactly the switching elements this class is about.

Hold the whole arc in your head for a moment. One person, using one building block, the switch. First he showed that switches can do logic. Then he showed that information itself can be counted in bits. Then he built switches that learn. Boolean logic, information theory, and the first flicker of machine learning, all standing on a two-state device. Everything this course builds sits on that same foundation.

The three operations

Before touching a circuit, spend time with the mathematics. Boole's three operations are AND, OR, and NOT. They take inputs that are either 0 or 1 and produce a 0 or 1 output. That is the entire domain. Two possible values in, two possible values out. A fourth operation, XOR, will join them at the end of this section, and it earns its place because it turns out to be half of addition.

NOT — the simplest possible operation

NOT takes one input and inverts it. If the input is 0, the output is 1. If the input is 1, the output is 0. No choices, no conditions. Just inversion, every time. There is literally no simpler non-trivial thing you can do to a single bit.

Now here is the connection Shannon made. These are not just abstract operations on true and false. They describe exactly what switches do. A switch that is closed passes current. A switch that is open does not. Closed is 1. Open is 0. And you can wire switches together in ways that implement AND, OR, and NOT physically.

From algebra to wire

A transistor is the physical switch underneath all of this. It has three terminals: a gate, a source, and a drain. The gate is the control. When the voltage at the gate is high, the transistor conducts, connecting the source to the drain so current can pass. When the gate voltage is low, it stops conducting and that connection is broken. That is the entire mechanism. One voltage, at the gate, decides whether one electrical path is open or closed. High voltage means a closed switch. Low voltage means an open switch.

From this single component every gate follows. What changes between gates is only how you arrange the transistors, and where you connect the supply voltage, called Vcc, and ground. A resistor holds the output at one rail by default. The transistors decide whether to override it. That interplay, resistor versus transistor, is what produces a 1 or a 0 at the output.

Start with NOT, the simplest case. It is a single transistor sitting between the output and ground, with a pull-up resistor tying the output to Vcc. When the input is high the transistor conducts and drags the output down to ground, so the output is low. When the input is low the transistor is off, and the pull-up quietly holds the output high. Input high gives output low. Input low gives output high. That is inversion, built from one switch. AND is two transistors in series, so both must conduct before current reaches the output. OR is two transistors in parallel, where either one conducting is enough. NAND and NOR are the inverting versions of those, where the transistors pull the output down toward ground and a pull-up holds it high the rest of the time. Select a gate below and toggle the inputs. Watch which transistors conduct, where current flows, and what the output does.

This is the moment the abstraction collapses into physics. Logic gates are not metaphors. They are transistors, wired in specific configurations, etched into silicon. But every time you use a gate after this, you draw a symbol instead of these transistors. The symbol hides the pull-up resistor, the supply rail, the ground, the channel that opens and closes. That hiding is deliberate. Nobody designing a processor reasons about individual electrons in a channel. They reason about AND and OR, and the symbol stands in for the whole arrangement you just watched. The abstraction is what makes the complexity survivable.

And the complexity is real. A modern processor holds around twenty billion transistors, each one a switch exactly like the ones in this diagram, each one opening and closing billions of times every second. The gate symbol is how twenty billion switches become something a human mind can actually design with.

De Morgan and what it means

The two identities that the whole idea of a universal gate rests on came from a man who wrote them down before Boole's algebra even existed to prove them in. Augustus De Morgan and George Boole were friends and lifelong correspondents. De Morgan was the more established figure, the first professor of mathematics at University College London, and he defended and encouraged Boole's work when almost nobody else took it seriously. In 1847 De Morgan published Formal Logic, in the very same year Boole published his first short book on the algebra of logic. The laws that carry De Morgan's name belong to that period, the 1840s, ahead of the fuller system Boole laid out in 1854.

AdM
Augustus De Morgan
1806 – 1871  ·  London, England
First professor of mathematics at University College London and one of the founders of modern symbolic logic. A playful, contrarian mind. He liked to point out that he had been x years old in the year x squared, being 43 in 1849. For years he traded letters with Boole and pushed the work that became Boolean algebra. The two laws that bear his name are the hinge that turns AND into OR and back again.

The laws are, in spirit, older than either man. Medieval logicians had noticed the pattern centuries earlier. What De Morgan did was state it cleanly in symbols, at the exact moment symbolic logic was being invented, so that when Boole's algebra arrived a few years later the laws slotted straight in as provable theorems rather than loose philosophical remarks. In modern notation they look like this:

De Morgan's laws — two identities that reshape circuits

Why does this matter? Because of what it says about a single gate, NAND. A NAND gate is AND followed by NOT. It outputs 0 only when both inputs are 1, and 1 in every other case. De Morgan's first law says NAND is the same thing as OR with both inputs inverted, and that one hint is enough to unlock everything.

Watch what you can build from NAND alone. Tie both inputs of a NAND together and feed in a single signal. NAND(A, A) is 1 when A is 0 and 0 when A is 1. That is exactly NOT, so NAND hands you an inverter for nothing. Take an ordinary NAND of two inputs and pass its output through one of those inverters and you get AND, because you have simply undone the inversion that made it a NAND. And by De Morgan, OR is a NAND fed with two already-inverted inputs, which works out to three NANDs in total. NOT, AND, OR, all three, out of one repeated part.

Everything from one gate — NOT, AND and OR built from NAND alone
NOT A → NANDinputs tied → NOT A
AND A B → NAND → NANDas NOT → A AND B
OR A→NAND (tied) B→NAND (tied) → NAND → A OR B

One part, repeated and rewired, produces every basic operation.

A set of operations that can build every other operation is called functionally complete. The trio AND, OR, NOT is functionally complete, which is what the sum of products construction in the next section quietly relies on. The genuinely surprising part is that you do not need all three. NAND on its own is functionally complete, and so is NOR, by the mirror image of the same De Morgan argument. That is precisely what the word universal means here. From one gate, any circuit at all.

The consequence inside a real factory is enormous. A chip maker does not have to perfect five or six different components and keep them all in tolerance. Perfect one, the NAND cell, stamp out billions of identical copies, and wire them into whatever arrangement each corner of the processor needs. That uniformity is a large part of why chips can be built at the scale and precision they are. The same logic Boole worked out as pure philosophy in the 1850s is the reason a fab can print billions of identical switches and get back a working computer.

What any function looks like

Here is the deeper implication of Boolean algebra, and it deserves to be built slowly, because it is the claim the entire rest of this course stands on.

Any function on bits, no matter what it does, can be built as a circuit of AND, OR, and NOT. Not most functions. All of them. And the proof is not clever. It is a mechanical recipe with three moves. Find the rows of the truth table where the output is 1. Build one AND term for each of those rows. OR the terms together. Done.

To see the recipe work, we need a function to run it on. Take the majority function of three inputs. It is a voting machine: three voters, A, B, and C, each voting 0 or 1, and the output is whatever the majority voted. Two or three 1s in, and the output is 1. Otherwise 0. This is not a toy chosen for convenience. Majority of three is exactly the carry logic you will need in Class 4, so building it now is building ahead.

Move one — find the 1-rows

Three inputs means eight possible combinations, so the truth table has eight rows. Write them all out and mark where the output is 1. For majority, that happens on exactly four rows: 011, 101, 110, and 111. The four ways two or more voters can say yes.

Move two — build a key for each 1-row

Now the heart of the recipe. For each 1-row, you build an AND term that is 1 for that row and 0 for every other row. Think of it as cutting a key. Each row of the table is a lock, and you are cutting a key that fits exactly one of them.

The cutting rule is simple. Walk across the row. If an input is 1 in that row, use the input directly. If it is 0, use the input through a NOT. AND all three together.

Do it once, slowly, for the row 011, where A=0, B=1, C=1. A is 0 in this row, so use NOT A. B is 1, so use B. C is 1, so use C. The key is (NOT A) AND B AND C. Now check that it fits only its own lock. Feed it the row it was cut for: NOT A is NOT 0, which is 1. B is 1. C is 1. The AND of 1, 1, 1 is 1. The key turns. Now feed it any other row, say 111. NOT A is NOT 1, which is 0, and an AND with a 0 anywhere in it is dead. Output 0. Try 010: C is 0, the AND dies again. Every one of the other seven rows disagrees with 011 in at least one input, and whichever input disagrees kills the AND. That is why the key fits one lock and no other.

Cut the other three keys the same way. Row 101 gives A AND (NOT B) AND C. Row 110 gives A AND B AND (NOT C). Row 111 gives A AND B AND C, no NOTs needed because nothing in that row is 0. Each of these terms has a proper name, a minterm, but the name matters less than the property: one term, one row, guaranteed.

Move three — OR the keys together

You now hold four keys, one per 1-row. OR them. The OR outputs 1 whenever at least one of its inputs is 1, and by construction exactly one key is 1 on each of the four target rows, and none of them is 1 anywhere else. So the OR is 1 on precisely the four rows where majority says 1, and 0 on the other four. The circuit and the truth table now agree on all eight rows, which is the only sense in which two functions can be equal.

out = (NOT A)·B·C + A·(NOT B)·C + A·B·(NOT C) + A·B·C

An expression in this shape, an OR of AND terms, is called a sum of products. The sum is the OR, the products are the ANDs. Every function you will ever meet can be written this way.

Do not take my word for it. Here is the circuit, live. Toggle the three inputs and watch which key fires, and confirm that the OR of the keys agrees with a straight majority count on every one of the eight combinations.

Why this can never fail

Nothing about those three moves depended on majority. Rerun the argument for any function at all. Its truth table has some set of 1-rows. For every 1-row, the cutting rule always produces a key, because all it does is read the row and place NOTs on the zeros. Every key fits its own row and no other, because any other row differs in at least one input, and that one disagreement kills the AND. So the OR of the keys is 1 on exactly the 1-rows and 0 everywhere else, which means it is the function, reproduced perfectly.

Hand me any truth table, however strange, and I can hand you back a circuit of AND, OR, and NOT that computes it. And since AND, OR, and NOT are all built from NAND, I can hand you back a circuit of nothing but NAND. This is why the space of circuits and the space of truth tables are the same space. Anything you can specify as a table of outputs, you can build. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, comparison, encoding, decoding. All of it is truth tables. All truth tables are circuits. All circuits are transistors.

This is what Shannon showed in 1937. The boundary between logic and electronics does not exist. They are the same thing, described in two different languages.

One consequence is worth noting before we move on. The truth table for addition will be the next class. You already know enough now to read it. Two bits in, two bits out: a sum bit and a carry bit. The sum bit is XOR. The carry bit is AND. Those two operations together with the circuits we now know how to build will give us a machine that adds. That is Class 4.

But there is still a question on the table from Class 2. We chose binary because two voltage states have a wide gap between them and are almost impossible to confuse. AND, OR, and NOT all operate on 0s and 1s. But the machine does not have one bit. It has eight bits in a byte, 32 bits in a register, billions of bits in memory. Do these gates work on multi-bit numbers?

Yes, and the way it works is simpler than you might expect. AND, OR, NOT, and XOR are all bitwise operations. When you apply AND to two 8-bit numbers, you apply AND independently to each pair of corresponding bits. Bit 0 of the first number ANDed with bit 0 of the second. Bit 1 with bit 1. And so on. No bit talks to any other bit. The gate just runs eight times in parallel.

Bitwise AND — the gate runs independently on each bit position

This is why bitwise operations are cheap. AND, OR, XOR on a 64-bit register costs the same as AND, OR, XOR on a single bit. Sixty-four gates running simultaneously. One clock cycle. No carry between positions, no interaction, no dependency on adjacent bits. Contrast this with addition, where a carry from one bit affects the next, which affects the next. Bitwise logic is embarrassingly parallel. Addition is not. That distinction will matter enormously when we build the adder in Class 5.

The question this class leaves open

We now have three things that were not available at the start of this class.

We have a mathematical framework. Boolean algebra tells us how to combine true and false values, and it is complete: any function you can describe with a truth table can be expressed in Boolean algebra.

We have a physical implementation. Transistors implement Boolean operations. NAND and NOR are universal. From a single gate type you can build any circuit.

We have bitwise operations on binary numbers. AND, OR, XOR run in parallel across all bit positions simultaneously.

What we do not yet have is addition. The simplest arithmetic operation, the one that everything else builds on, has not appeared yet. We know that XOR looks promising for the sum bit and AND looks right for the carry. But combining them into something that correctly adds two multi-bit binary numbers, that handles carries propagating across bit positions, is the work of the next class.

The open question
We have the mathematics and the physical gates. We can do logic. But logic is not arithmetic. AND and OR on two 8-bit numbers produce an 8-bit result, no interaction between positions. Addition requires carries. A 1 in the rightmost position can ripple all the way to the leftmost. How does a circuit handle that? That is Class 4.

One more thing worth noting before we get there. The four stories from Class 1 are all, at some level, stories about arithmetic going wrong. The wallpaper color exceeded the maximum byte value. The float stored 0.1 as the nearest representable binary fraction. The Unix timestamp overflowed a 32-bit signed integer. The Apollo 1202 was an executive scheduler running out of cycles. All of them are arithmetic failures. All of them happen in hardware built from exactly the gates described in this class.

The gates do not make mistakes. They cannot. A transistor in a well-designed circuit behaves exactly as its truth table predicts, every single time. The errors in Class 1 were not hardware failures. They were the correct execution of the wrong representation, or the correct arithmetic producing a result that exceeded the container holding it. Understanding exactly how the arithmetic works, at the level of gates and carries, is what makes those errors legible rather than mysterious.
