How Does a Machine Add?

Class 3 ended on a confession. We have gates that do logic, but logic is not arithmetic, and the gap between them is one stubborn detail: the carry. This class closes that gap. By the end of it you will have built, gate by gate, the exact circuit that every processor on earth uses to add, and you will know precisely why it is slower than it looks.

Here is where we stand. Binary can represent any whole number. Gates can compute any truth table. XOR looked suspiciously like the sum digit of one-bit addition, and AND looked exactly like the carry. But a suspicion is not a circuit. A carry born in the rightmost column can ripple all the way across a number, flipping bits as it goes, and nothing we built in Class 3 knows how to pass anything sideways.

Before we build it, I want to introduce the man who built it first, because his story makes a point I do not want you to miss.

The adder in the living room

KZ
Konrad Zuse
1910 – 1995  ·  Berlin, Germany
Civil engineer who hated the endless, repetitive structural calculations his job demanded, so he decided a machine should do them. Between 1936 and 1938 he built the Z1, a mechanical binary computer, in his parents' living room. His Z3 of 1941 is widely regarded as the first working programmable computer. He worked in near-total isolation, unaware of Shannon's thesis or Turing's papers, and arrived at binary, floating point, and the adder circuit on his own. He also designed what may be the first high-level programming language, Plankalkül, before anyone had a machine that could run one.

Zuse's starting complaint should sound familiar by now. It is the shepherd's complaint from Class 2 and the table-makers' complaint from the 17th century: humans doing repetitive arithmetic make errors, and the errors matter. His answer was to build a machine, and the very first thing that machine needed, before memory, before programs, before anything else, was a circuit that could add two binary numbers.

He built it from sliding metal plates. Later versions used telephone relays, the same two-state switches Shannon was analysing an ocean away at the same moment, neither man knowing of the other. Two people, working independently, forced to the same design by the same logic. That is usually a sign the design is not an invention so much as a discovery. The adder we are about to build is the thing they both found.

And the strangest part is that you already know the algorithm. You have known it since you were seven years old.

The answer was in your pencil

Add 47 and 38 on paper. You line the numbers up in columns. You add the rightmost column: 7 plus 8 is 15. That does not fit in one digit, so you write down the 5 and carry the 1, a small pencil mark above the next column. Then the next column: 4 plus 3 is 7, plus the carried 1 makes 8. Write 8. Answer: 85.

Look at the structure of what you did, not the numbers. You processed one column at a time, right to left. Each column took in two digits plus a possible carry from the column before, and produced one digit plus a possible carry to the column after. Every column ran the identical procedure. The only thing connecting the columns was that thin pencil mark.

That is the entire algorithm. It works in any base, because carrying is a property of positional notation, not of base ten. In binary the columns overflow sooner, at 2 instead of 10, but the procedure does not change at all.

The same algorithm in two bases — the pencil carry is the only thing moving sideways

So the plan for this class writes itself. Build a circuit that does one column. Then chain copies of it, one per bit, connected by a wire that plays the role of the pencil mark. If one column works and the chaining works, addition works, at any width.

One column of binary

Start with the simplest possible column, the rightmost one, which has no carry coming in. Two bits enter. What comes out?

There are only four cases, so write them all. 0 plus 0 is 0. 0 plus 1 is 1. 1 plus 0 is 1. And 1 plus 1 is 2, which in binary is 10: a sum digit of 0 and a carry of 1. So the column has two outputs, a sum bit and a carry bit, and its complete behaviour is a truth table with four rows.

This is the payoff of the Class 3 preview, and it is worth saying plainly. The sum bit is not similar to XOR. It is XOR, the identical truth table. The carry bit is AND, the identical truth table. Which means the circuit for one carry-free column is two gates. Feed A and B into an XOR for the sum. Feed the same A and B into an AND for the carry. Done. This little two-gate circuit has a name: the half adder.

Why it is only half an adder

The name is honest. The half adder handles the rightmost column and no other, because every other column receives three things: the two bits being added and the carry arriving from the column to its right. Three inputs, not two. The half adder has no third input. It is half of what a real column needs.

So write the truth table a real column obeys. Three input bits, A, B, and the carry-in, each 0 or 1. Their arithmetic sum can be 0, 1, 2, or 3, which in binary is 00, 01, 10, or 11. Two output bits again: a sum bit and a carry-out. Eight rows, all of them below.

Read the patterns. The sum bit is 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1. The carry-out is 1 when two or more inputs are 1, which is the majority function you built from minterms in Class 3.

You could build this straight from the table with the sum of products recipe, and it would work. Class 3 guarantees it. But there is a construction that is smaller and far more instructive, because it reuses the piece we already have. A column is really two little additions in a row: first add A and B, then add the carry-in to that result. Each of those is a two-bit addition, and we own a circuit for two-bit addition. Use two half adders.

The first half adder takes A and B, producing a partial sum s1 and a carry c1. The second half adder takes s1 and Cin, producing the final sum bit and a second carry c2. Then one question remains: what is the carry-out of the whole column? A carry happened if either little addition overflowed, so Cout is c1 OR c2. Two half adders and one OR gate. This is the full adder, and it is the single most-replicated circuit in your processor.

Set all three inputs to 1 and trace it. The first half adder sees 1 and 1, so s1 is 0 and c1 is 1. The second sees s1=0 and Cin=1, so the sum is 1 and c2 is 0. The OR of the carries gives Cout=1. Sum 1, carry 1, which is binary 11, which is 3. Three ones in, three out. The circuit agrees with arithmetic on this row and, if you work the toggles, on all eight.

One subtlety deserves a paragraph, because it looks like luck and is not. The OR gate merges c1 and c2 as if they could never both be 1 at once, and they never can. If c1 is 1, then A and B were both 1, which forces s1 to 0. And if s1 is 0, the second half adder's carry, s1 AND Cin, is 0 no matter what Cin is. So c1 being 1 guarantees c2 is 0. The two carries are mutually exclusive by construction, which is exactly why a plain OR is enough to combine them. Nothing in a well-designed circuit is an accident.

Chaining the columns

Now the pencil mark becomes a wire. Take one full adder per bit position. Connect the carry-out of each column to the carry-in of the column to its left, exactly the way your pencil carried the 1 leftward on paper. The rightmost column has no carry arriving, so tie its carry-in to 0. The leftmost column's carry-out pokes out of the top of the whole circuit, and we will have words about it shortly.

This arrangement is called a ripple carry adder, and the name describes its behaviour honestly. Set two numbers on the inputs and the answer does not appear all at once. The rightmost column settles first. Its carry ripples into the next column, which can only then settle, and its carry ripples onward. The answer flows across the circuit right to left like a wave. Run the worst case and pay attention to the order of events. 1111 plus 0001. The only 1 in the second number sits in the rightmost column, yet every single column changes. The rightmost adds 1+1, writes 0, carries. The next column adds 1+0 plus that carry, writes 0, carries again. The carry marches through every position and finally falls out of the left end. Fifteen plus one is sixteen, 10000, five bits.

Then run the no-carries case, 0101 plus 1010. Every column adds a 0 and a 1, no carry is ever generated, and each column's answer never depended on its neighbour. In that case the columns could all have settled simultaneously. The wave only exists when carries exist.

The price of the ripple

That last observation is the door to something important. In Class 3 I made a point of saying that bitwise AND on a 64-bit register costs the same as AND on one bit, because all 64 gate pairs work in parallel with nothing passing between them. Now you can see exactly what addition loses. Column 5 cannot produce its correct answer until it knows its carry-in, which comes from column 4, which is waiting on column 3, all the way down. In the worst case, the leftmost column of a 64-bit adder is sitting idle until a carry born in column 0 has physically travelled through 63 stages of gates.

Gates are not instant. Each one takes a small but real time to respond to its inputs, a gate delay, and delays in a chain add up. A 64-bit ripple adder in the worst case is a chain of roughly a hundred and thirty gate delays, against the single gate delay of a bitwise AND. Both are fast in human terms. But a processor executing billions of additions per second lives and dies by exactly this kind of accounting, and the ripple adder's carry chain is the slowest path through it.

Engineers did not accept this. There is a family of designs, carry-lookahead adders, that compute the carries directly from the inputs, in advance, instead of waiting for them to ripple. The idea is that you can tell whether a column will generate a carry or propagate one just by looking at its own two bits, and clever circuitry can combine those signals across the whole word in a few gate delays. Real processors use these. The ripple adder remains the design to learn first, because it is the honest one: it is your pencil algorithm, wired.

Bitwise logic is a crowd working in parallel. Addition is a bucket brigade. The bucket is the carry.

The bit with nowhere to go

One loose wire is still dangling. The leftmost full adder produces a carry-out, and in a fixed-width machine there is no column to its left. What happens to that bit?

Nothing happens to it. That is the problem. The register is 8 bits, or 32, or 64, and the carry out of the top column simply has no home. Watch it in the widget: 1111 plus 0001 produces a fifth bit. In our toy adder I display it. In a real 4-bit register it would be gone, and the register would read 0000. Fifteen plus one, in a 4-bit machine, is zero.

Now connect this to Class 1, because two of the four stories just became mechanical rather than mysterious. The wallpaper pixel arithmetic produced 271 in an 8-bit channel. 271 needs nine bits. The ninth bit is precisely the top carry-out of an 8-bit adder, and it had nowhere to land. Whether the surrounding code then wraps the value, clamps it, or, as Android found, marches off and crashes on it, the birth of the error is this exact wire. And the 2038 timestamp is not an abstraction either. Somewhere in the machine, once per second, a 32-bit adder computes time plus one. It has been running that same circuit since 1970, and it is carrying its way steadily toward the top of the register.

But notice I said the timestamp will land in 1901, not zero. A wrapped adder should give zero, or something near it. Negative 1901-ness is stranger than a wraparound. The explanation is that the top bit of that register is not an ordinary column at all. It carries a meaning we have not built yet.

The open question
We can add. But ask this machine to compute 7 minus 5 and it has nothing, because there is no minus sign anywhere in it, only bits. Yet real processors subtract billions of times a second, and almost none of them contain a subtractor circuit. The trick is to turn subtraction into addition, and the trick requires an answer to a question we have been walking past since Class 2: what is a negative number in binary? Answer that, and subtraction is free, and the 2038 timestamp's plunge to 1901 stops being strange. That is Class 5.

Keep the image of the ripple in your head as we go. Every arithmetic circuit we build from here on is this adder wearing different clothes. Subtraction will be this adder with a twist on one input. Multiplication, in Class 7, will be this adder used repeatedly. The pencil mark you learned at seven turned out to be one of the load-bearing ideas of the century.
