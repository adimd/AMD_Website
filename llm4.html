1. Rewinding the Story: What Co-occurrence First Taught Us

In the previous chapter, we built a tiny co-occurrence table from a tiny universe of words. Even though the vocabulary was small, something remarkable happened: patterns of meaning began to shine through simply by looking at which words tended to appear near each other.

“Dog” and “cat” behaved similarly. “I” and “you” behaved similarly. “My” interacted frequently with nouns. “Love” stood in the middle of many relationships.

Without using any machine learning, without any training loops, the co-occurrence table quietly whispered:

“A word is known by the company it keeps.”

This idea by itself is powerful. But today, we are not here to celebrate it. We are here to ask: Why isn't co-occurrence enough? And more importantly: How do we move from static counts to representations that learn?

Small toy co-occurrence matrix showing similar rows for “dog” and “cat” placeholder

Even a tiny co-occurrence matrix reveals similarity — but cannot learn it.

2. The Moment Co-occurrence Begins to Crack

At small scale, co-occurrence tables feel magical. At large scale, they begin to fall apart. Understanding why they break naturally leads us toward W and C.

2.1 The scale problem

A real vocabulary is not 6 words — it is 50,000 or 100,000 words. The co-occurrence matrix becomes a monster:

100,000 × 100,000 = 10 billion cells.

Not only is this enormous, but most entries are zero because many word pairs never meet. We now have a gigantic, expensive, sparse object.

2.2 The lack-of-learning problem

But the deeper limitation is conceptual: The co-occurrence table cannot improve itself.

Once built, it freezes time. It cannot look at a mistake and correct itself. It cannot shift its understanding when new sentences appear. It is a summary, not a learner.

Co-occurrence shows structure, but cannot discover structure.

If we want a system that learns meaning like humans do — incrementally, through countless experiences — we need something more dynamic.

3. Looking Behind the Matrix: The World of Micro-Events

Let’s imagine how the co-occurrence matrix is actually produced. It is not magically formed — it emerges from a long sequence of tiny observations:

  • At this moment, “love” touched “my”.
  • At this moment, “my” touched “dog”.
  • At this moment, “dog” touched “my”.

These are not numbers yet — they are events. Moments in a sentence where two words find themselves next to each other.

In fact, the co-occurrence matrix is nothing but:

All events, counted.

But we can do something much more powerful. Instead of throwing away the individual events and keeping only the counts, we can learn from the events directly.

Diagram: stream of (center, context) events leading into a table placeholder

Before the matrix exists, a river of events flows through the corpus.

This is the turning point of the entire chapter:

Instead of summarising meaning, we want to learn meaning from raw experience.

4. From Events to a Training Dataset

Let’s take the sentence:

I love my dog

With a context window of 1, we extract pairs:

  • (center = love, context = I)
  • (center = love, context = my)
  • (center = my, context = love)
  • (center = my, context = dog)
  • (center = dog, context = my)

Every pair says:

“Please remember that these two words appeared together.”

When we do this for millions of sentences, we build a training dataset, a long list of all the little word-neighbourhood events that happened in real language.

And this dataset will guide how the model forms meaning.

5. A Leap Forward: Two Learnable Dictionaries

Now we take the same idea that the co-occurrence matrix hinted at — the idea that meaning emerges from usage — but we build it in a way that learns.

We introduce two matrices:

  • W: When the word is the center.
  • C: When the word is the context.

Why two matrices? Because words behave differently depending on how they appear: as something being described (center) vs. something that helps describe another word (context).

These matrices start as random vectors. They know nothing. They contain no meaning.

But with enough training examples, meaning begins to take shape — not because we force it, but because language teaches it.

W and C do what the co-occurrence table could not: they learn from mistakes and adapt over time.

6. How a Single Example Changes the Model

Take one training pair:

(dog, my)

What happens inside the model?

  1. It retrieves W[dog] — how “dog” behaves as a center word.
  2. It retrieves C[my] — how “my” behaves as a context word.
  3. It updates them so that they become slightly more compatible.

Nothing dramatic happens with just one example. But imagine doing this millions of times, across millions of words.

The model gradually shapes the geometric landscape of meaning.

Each event reshapes the embedding space by an infinitesimal amount — meaning is carved slowly, like a river shaping rock.

7. Why Similar Words Become Geometrically Close

Now consider all events with dog:

  • (dog, my)
  • (dog, the)
  • (dog, big)
  • (dog, barking)

Each event nudges W[dog] toward its corresponding context vector.

Now consider cat:

  • (cat, my)
  • (cat, the)
  • (cat, cute)
  • (cat, purring)

Because “dog” and “cat” share many similar contexts, they receive similar nudges.

Over time, these nudges accumulate into geometry:

W[dog] and W[cat] drift toward each other naturally.

This is not a hack. This is not something we programmed explicitly. We never said:

“Dog and cat are animals — please make them close.”

Instead, the language itself delivered countless tiny hints, and the embedding space simply rearranged to honour them.

Illustration: dog & cat vectors drifting closer through shared context nudges placeholder

Words drift together if the training data repeatedly treats them similarly.

This emergent geometry replaces the static co-occurrence matrix with a living, learnable representation of meaning.

8. A Deep Intuition: How Meaning Emerges From Repeated Corrections

Here is the real philosophical jump in this chapter:

Meaning is not assigned. It is accumulated.

No single event gives the model complete knowledge. Meaning emerges as the average effect of thousands of micro-corrections.

This is exactly how humans learn words. You do not learn the word “apple” from a dictionary definition. You learn it from hearing it, seeing it, touching it, tasting it — over and over again, in different contexts.

Models learn the same way: repeated exposure to patterns of context causes similar words to cluster, opposite words to separate, and rare relationships to take shape gradually.

Co-occurrence tables capture this pattern, but W and C actually learn it.

9. Summary: Why W and C Are the First Step Toward Modern NLP

  • Co-occurrence reveals structure but cannot adapt.
  • Events let us treat language as a sequence of teachable moments.
  • W and C are two learnable dictionaries that respond to training signals.
  • Meaning emerges gradually from repeated vector adjustments.
Co-occurrence tables showed us that context carries meaning.
W and C let us learn that meaning directly from data.

In the next chapter, we will go deeper: How do we convert compatibility into probability? How does a model decide which contexts are likely? And how does loss instruct embeddings where to move?

For now, we have completed the most important conceptual leap: moving from counting meaning to learning meaning.
