How RTOS Works

What "Real-Time" Actually Means

It does not mean fast. That is the whole lesson, really. But the reason it does not mean fast is worth understanding properly.

Start with brakes

You are driving and you hit the brakes hard. Somewhere in your car, a computer receives that signal and has to decide how to modulate brake pressure across each wheel to stop them from locking up. It has to make that decision and send that command fast enough that the physics of the situation has not already played out by the time the command arrives.

Now imagine the same computer is also streaming music over Bluetooth. The song skips for half a second. Annoying, but you are fine. The car is fine. Nothing bad happened.

Those two jobs have a fundamentally different relationship with time. Not just in how fast they need to respond, but in what happens when they are late. That difference is what real-time actually means, and it has nothing to do with speed.

Late is wrong

In most software, a slow answer is still a correct answer. A web page that loads in 3 seconds instead of 1 is slower, but the content is right. A search that takes 500ms is frustrating, but the results are valid. You got the answer, just later than you wanted.

In a real-time system, a late answer is not a slow correct answer. It is a wrong answer. The timing is part of the correctness. If the brake command arrives 200ms after it should have, it does not matter how perfectly it was computed. The wheels may already be locked. The physics moved on without it.

This is the definition. A real-time system is one where the correctness of a result depends not just on what the result is, but on when it arrives.

Timing is part of correctness

A result that arrives before the deadline is correct. The same result, computed identically, arriving after the deadline is wrong. Not slower. Wrong. The deadline is not a performance target, it is a correctness boundary.

Notice that nothing here says anything about speed. The brake system's deadline might be 10ms. A nuclear reactor safety shutoff might have a deadline of 200ms. A satellite manoeuvre might have a deadline of several seconds. None of these are fast in absolute terms. What they share is that missing the deadline constitutes failure, regardless of how good the actual computation was.

Not all deadlines end the same way

Once you accept that timing is part of correctness, the next question is what actually happens when the deadline is missed. And here the answer varies quite a bit depending on the system.

Hard deadlines

Missing it is a failure with no recovery. The result after the deadline is not just less useful, it is dangerous or actively wrong. A pacemaker sends an electrical pulse to the heart every 800ms. A late pulse by 50ms can cause an arrhythmia. The heart may have already done something wrong by the time the late pulse arrives, and there is no correcting it after the fact. A fly-by-wire aircraft that misses its control surface deadline does not just fly less smoothly. At certain critical moments, it does not fly at all.

The key characteristic of a hard deadline is not just that missing it is bad. It is that missing it even once is a failure, and no amount of previously meeting the deadline makes up for it.

Firm deadlines

Missing it makes the result completely worthless, but the system survives and carries on. A GPS receiver computes your position from satellite timing measurements. Each measurement is only valid within a narrow window because the satellite is moving and the geometry changes. A measurement that arrives 50ms late is simply thrown away. It cannot contribute to the current position calculation. The receiver continues with the remaining valid data, gives you a slightly less accurate position, and tries again next cycle. Nothing catastrophic happened, but that late result contributed exactly nothing. Zero value, not degraded value.

Soft deadlines

Missing it degrades quality, but the result is still somewhat useful. A video call codec tries to encode and send each frame within 33ms. If one frame takes 60ms, it arrives late and the video stutters slightly. The late frame is still shown. Miss enough of these in a row and the call becomes unwatchable, but there is no single moment of total failure. The system degrades gracefully rather than snapping to a failure state.

What happens after the deadline passes

The difference between the three types is not how strict the deadline is. It is what the late result is worth. For hard deadlines it has negative value — it can cause harm. For firm deadlines it has zero value — discarded. For soft deadlines it has reduced but non-zero value — still usable, just worse.

The 99.99% trap

Here is something that catches people out. A system that meets its deadline 99.99% of the time sounds extremely reliable. And for a soft real-time system, it probably is. But for a hard real-time system, it is a system with a guaranteed failure mode. It just has not triggered yet.

Do the arithmetic. A pacemaker fires roughly 100,000 times a year. At 99.99% reliability, that is 10 failures per year. For a device keeping someone's heart beating, that number is completely unacceptable. Hard real-time means the tail of the distribution is cut off entirely, not trimmed. Zero failures is the only acceptable answer, not low-probability failures.

This is also why the question "how fast does your system respond?" is the wrong question for real-time. The right question is "what is the worst case, and does the worst case still meet the deadline?" A system that responds in 1ms on average but occasionally spikes to 500ms is not a real-time system for a 200ms deadline. That spike is the system failing, just infrequently.

Real-time does not mean fast. It means a late result is a wrong result, and the system is designed so results are never late — not usually, not 99.99% of the time, never.

Why Linux is not real-time

Your laptop runs Linux or something similar. It manages hundreds of processes at once, feels responsive, handles everything you throw at it. But it is not real-time, and the reason is instructive.

Linux was designed to be fair and to maximise throughput across everything running on it. When your browser needs CPU time, Linux gives it some. When a background update also needs it, Linux shares. When the kernel is in the middle of handling a system call and something urgent arrives, Linux often has to finish the current work before dealing with the new thing. The time between something becoming urgent and it actually getting CPU attention has no hard upper bound. Usually it is tiny. Occasionally it is not. And "occasionally it is not" is exactly the problem.

An RTOS makes a deliberately different trade-off. It gives up fairness and average throughput in exchange for a guarantee: when the highest-priority task needs to run, it will run within a known, tested, documented maximum time. That time is usually in microseconds. It is not a statistical claim. It is a design guarantee that the hardware and software together enforce every single time.

The distribution that matters

A general-purpose OS has a long tail — rare but real spikes in execution time that can blow past any deadline. An RTOS has a hard cutoff at T_max. Above that line the probability is exactly zero, not approximately zero. That is determinism.

That hard cutoff is called determinism. Given any input, under any system load, the execution time is provably bounded. Not probably bounded. Provably. For safety-critical systems like flight control software, proving this bound on every function is not optional. It is a certification requirement.
