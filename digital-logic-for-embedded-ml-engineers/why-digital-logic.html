Digital Logic for the Embedded ML Engineer

Why You're Here

Before we touch a single bit, a small story about a small device, and the only three things you actually need to understand about a chip.

A small story

Imagine you've trained a tiny neural network. Nothing fancy. It listens to a microphone and decides whether someone said "yes" or "no". On your laptop it works perfectly. You're proud of it.

Now you want to put it on a small device. Something the size of a coin. A microcontroller that costs about two dollars and runs on a watch battery. You want this thing to sit there and listen, all day, for weeks, without you having to charge it.

So you take your model, flash it to the chip, and run it. One of three things happens.

The first thing that can happen is that it doesn't fit. The model is bigger than the chip's memory. The compiler quietly refuses, or the firmware boots and immediately crashes. Either way, you're not running anything yet.

The second thing that can happen is that it fits, but it's slow. A prediction takes half a second. The user says "yes" and the device responds three syllables later. Technically working. Practically broken.

The third thing that can happen is that it fits and it's fast, and you're feeling great, until you check the battery the next morning and it's already dead. The model runs, but it's burning so much energy that the watch battery you wanted to last a month lasts a day.

This is the moment every embedded ML engineer faces, over and over. The model worked in theory. Now make it work for real, on this specific small chip, with these specific small resources, without burning through the battery before lunch.

The model worked in theory. Now make it work in reality.

The natural question

When you hit one of those three problems, the natural question is just: why?

Why is my model too big? Why is each prediction slow? Why is it draining the battery? Most people, at this point, do what feels obvious. They try a smaller model. They google. They try a different framework. They try int8 quantisation because somebody on Twitter said it helps. Sometimes one of these works. Mostly they're guessing, and it feels like guessing because it is.

The alternative is to actually understand what's going on inside the chip. Once you can see the machine, the why becomes obvious, and so does the how. You stop trying random things and start making decisions.

That understanding is what this whole series is about. And the first surprise is how simple the picture really is.

What's actually inside the chip

Here's a thing most software people never get told properly. A microcontroller is not magic. It's a small piece of silicon with three things inside, and once you see them, the mystery mostly evaporates.

It has somewhere to store numbers. We call that memory. It has somewhere to do things to numbers, like adding two of them or multiplying them. We call that the math unit. And it has a clock that ticks at some speed, and on every tick, the chip does one little thing. Read a number. Multiply two numbers. Write a number back. That's it. That's the whole show.

Everything else, every fancy feature you've ever seen on a datasheet, is just a clever rearrangement of those three pieces.

A chip is a calculator with memory and a clock. That's the whole thing.

When your model runs on the chip, it's just doing a lot of multiplications and additions, one tick at a time. The "too big" problem is because each weight in the model takes up space, and there are a lot of weights. The "too slow" problem is because there are millions of these tiny operations and the clock isn't fast enough to fit them all into the time you have. The "battery" problem is because every tick burns a tiny bit of energy, and millions of ticks add up.

You can poke at this directly. The visualisation below is the world's simplest chip. Two numbers in memory, a math unit that multiplies them and adds the result to a running total, and a clock that ticks. Press the button. Watch a multiply-and-add happen. That's the same operation a real chip does inside a neural network, except a real one does it billions of times.

Each tick reads one weight, one activation, multiplies them in the math unit, and adds the result to the accumulator. A real Cortex-M chip can do this in a single tick using a special instruction called a multiply-accumulate. A real neural network is millions of these, stitched together. Once you see one, you've seen them all.

Three things, three problems

Once you see the chip as just memory plus a math unit plus a clock, every embedded ML problem becomes a question about one of those three things.

Memory shows up as: how much space do my weights and activations take, and is there enough? The math unit shows up as: what kinds of math is the chip actually fast at, and am I using them? The clock shows up as: how many ticks does my model need, and how many ticks does the chip get per second?

Energy is hiding in there too. Every tick costs a little bit of energy, and every memory access costs a little bit more. Add it all up over an inference, multiply by however often you run it, and you have your battery life.

So when your tiny "yes/no" model fails on the small chip, you can now ask sharper questions. Is it too big in memory? Is it too slow because the chip can't do the math you're asking for in fewer ticks? Is it too hot on the battery because of all the memory traffic? These aren't separate mysteries. They're three sides of the same picture.

Why "digital logic" specifically

The phrase sounds intimidating. It really isn't. Digital logic is the study of two things. How a computer represents information using 0s and 1s, and how it does things to that information using simple building blocks. That's the whole field. People have made it sound scary because most courses teach it through truth tables and Karnaugh maps, but the actual ideas underneath are not complicated. Most of them you could draw on a napkin.

It happens to be the foundation of everything else. Once you understand it, things that used to feel like dark magic start to feel obvious. Why is int8 quantisation a thing, and not int7? Why does keeping data in a small buffer make code faster? Why does this chip have a special instruction for multiply-and-add? Why does my model fit on this chip but not that one? These all have crisp answers once you can see the hardware.

You stop being a person who uses embedded ML tools and start being a person who understands what those tools are doing. Which, conveniently, is also the difference between someone who can deploy a model and someone who can make it actually work in the field.

What I'm not promising

I'm not going to tell you that after this series you'll be designing chips at Google. That's a much longer journey, and a different one.

What I am promising is that by the end of this, you will look at a chip's spec sheet and have a real opinion about whether it can run your model. You'll understand why your model takes up the space it does, and what makes it smaller. You'll be able to read code in libraries like TFLite Micro and CMSIS-NN and know what it's doing instead of trusting it blindly. And you'll have the foundation for the deeper stuff that comes after, the CPUs and accelerators and DSPs that the series after this will get into.

That's enough to take you from "I know how to call a model" to "I understand the machine my model is running on". Which is a bigger jump than it sounds.

The road ahead

We're going to take this slowly and concretely. We start with the simplest things: how numbers are written in binary, how computers store them, why hex exists. Then we build up. By the end of the series, you'll have walked, on paper and in code, through the same kind of tiny hardware that runs neural networks on real chips. You won't just be told it works. You'll have built one yourself.
