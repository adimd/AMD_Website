Digital Logic for the Embedded ML Engineer

Two's Complement

Why every quantised int8 weight has a slightly asymmetric range, and the small mathematical trick that made signed arithmetic almost free.

The question we kept dodging

For three lessons now, we've been reading bytes without saying what they actually mean as numbers. We took 0x9C and turned it into 1001 1100. We talked about high nibbles and low nibbles. We even noticed which bits were set. But every time someone asked "yes, but what number is that?", we sort of changed the subject.

The reason we changed the subject is that the question doesn't have a single answer. As an unsigned 8-bit value, 0x9C is 156. As a signed 8-bit value, it's −100. Same eight bits. Two completely different numbers. The hardware doesn't know which interpretation you want. It just stores switches, and the meaning lives in the type system of your code.

This post is about the signed interpretation. Specifically, it's about the convention that every modern CPU uses, that every quantised neural network's weights are stored in, and that quietly powers half the optimisations in embedded ML. It's called two's complement, and once you see how it works, a lot of things that used to feel arbitrary will start to feel obvious. Including the strange detail that an int8 ranges from −128 to +127, instead of the symmetric range you'd probably have designed yourself.

The real problem: representing negatives

Computers need to represent negative numbers somehow. Hardware just stores bits, so we need a convention: an agreement about which bit patterns mean which numbers. The hardware doesn't know about negatives. It just adds and subtracts according to fixed rules. So we need a convention where those rules give the right answers when negative numbers are involved.

People have tried several. Three of them are worth knowing about, because each one teaches you something about why the winner won. We'll look at all three in turn, and you'll see exactly what makes two's complement special.

Attempt one: sign-magnitude

The most obvious approach, the one a beginner would invent on the spot, is to take one bit and use it as a sign flag. Zero means positive, one means negative. The remaining bits encode the magnitude.

So in 8-bit sign-magnitude:

  • 0000 0101 means "positive, magnitude 5", which is +5
  • 1000 0101 means "negative, magnitude 5", which is −5

This feels intuitive. It's how you might explain it on a whiteboard. But it has problems that turn out to be expensive in hardware.

The first problem is that there are two zeros. 0000 0000 is "positive zero" and 1000 0000 is "negative zero". Both represent the same number, but they're different bit patterns. Now every equality comparison has to remember that there are two valid forms of zero.

The second, worse problem is that addition stops being a single operation. If I add +5 and −3, the hardware can't just add the bits, because the MSB is a sign flag, not a numeric digit. A regular adder would produce garbage. You'd need special-case logic that examines the signs first, decides whether to actually add or subtract the magnitudes, and figures out the sign of the result. That's a lot of extra silicon for one of the most common operations in any program.

Sign-magnitude survives in some niches (it's how IEEE 754 floating-point handles its sign bit, for unrelated reasons). But for integer arithmetic, it lost the war.

Attempt two: one's complement

The next idea was tidier. To negate a number, just invert all its bits. So +5 is 0000 0101, and −5 is 1111 1010.

This is cleaner. Negation becomes a bitwise NOT, which is one of the cheapest operations in hardware. And addition almost works. If we add +5 and −5:

0000 0101 (+5) + 1111 1010 (-5) = 1111 1111 (this is "negative zero" in one's complement)

The result is "negative zero", which is conceptually correct, just annoying.

Two problems remain. There are still two zeros (0000 0000 and 1111 1111). And when adding two numbers produces a carry-out from the top bit, the hardware has to take that carry and feed it back in at the bottom. They called this an "end-around carry" and built circuitry for it. The PDP-1, one of the earliest minicomputers, used one's complement and lived with this complication. The world tried it and moved on.

Attempt three: the trick that won

Two's complement uses a tiny tweak that, on paper, looks barely different from one's complement. To negate a number: invert all the bits, then add 1.

To negate +5:

  • Start with +5 as 0000 0101
  • Invert all bits: 1111 1010
  • Add 1: 1111 1011

So −5 in two's complement is 1111 1011. Tiny change from one's complement. Enormous change in what it lets the hardware do.

That single tweak fixes both problems with one's complement and gives the hardware a pile of free conveniences.

There's only one zero now. Negating 0000 0000: invert to 1111 1111, add 1, you get 0000 0000 again with a carry-out that gets discarded as overflow. So zero is its own negation, and there's exactly one bit pattern for it.

Addition just works, with no special cases. Let's add +5 and −5 in two's complement:

0000 0101 (+5) + 1111 1011 (-5) = 1 0000 0000 (carry-out is discarded; result is 0)

The hardware did nothing special. It just added the bits and threw away the overflow carry. The whole system is designed so that ordinary unsigned binary addition produces the correct signed result. That's the killer feature.

Subtraction is just addition. To compute a - b, the hardware computes a + (-b), where -b is the two's complement of b. The same adder does both jobs. No special subtractor circuit. Half the silicon, twice the value.

Two's complement is designed so that ordinary binary addition produces correct signed results. Same hardware, no special cases.

Why it actually works: the clock-face view

"Invert all the bits, then add 1" sounds like a magic trick. It looks like someone fiddled with bits until subtraction happened to come out right. But there's a much deeper reason it works, and once you see it, the whole system stops feeling like a trick and starts feeling inevitable.

The deeper reason is that fixed-width binary arithmetic is already modular arithmetic, whether you wanted it to be or not.

Here's what that means. When you add two 8-bit numbers and the result doesn't fit in 8 bits, the carry-out gets discarded. That's not a bug, it's just what fixed-width hardware does. So 100 + 200 in 8-bit unsigned arithmetic gives you not 300, but 44 (because 300 mod 256 is 44). The hardware sets a carry flag to tell you it happened, but the result you actually get is the modular one.

In other words, 8-bit hardware operates natively modulo 256. It can't do anything else. The 256 possible byte values aren't a line stretching from 0 to 255 — they're a circle. After 255, you wrap back to 0. Like a clock face, but with 256 positions instead of 12.

Now here's the clever part. Two's complement takes that circular number line and just relabels half of it with negative numbers. The unsigned values 0 to 127 stay as themselves. But the unsigned values 128 to 255 get relabelled as −128 to −1. We're not changing the bit patterns. We're not changing the hardware. We're just choosing to read the second half of the circle as negative numbers.

And the magic is: this relabelling is exactly the right one for arithmetic to keep working. The unsigned value 251 and the signed value −5 are the same byte: 1111 1011. Why? Because in mod-256 arithmetic, −5 ≡ 251 (since 251 + 5 = 256 ≡ 0). The "invert and add 1" recipe is just a fast way to compute 256 − x, which is x's modular negative.

So when you add +5 and −5 in two's complement, the hardware actually adds 5 and 251, gets 256, discards the carry-out (which is the same as "take mod 256"), and gives you 0. Signed addition is just unsigned addition wearing a different label.

Two's complement isn't imposed on the hardware. It's the unique signed representation that the hardware was already doing for free.

Let's see this with a tiny example first, before extending to a real chip. Imagine a chip with only 3 bits to store a number. That gives it 8 byte slots, with bit patterns from 000 through 111. That's it. The chip can't physically store anything else.

Now we have a choice about how to read those 8 bit patterns. Read them as unsigned, and they're 0 through 7. Read the same 8 patterns as two's complement, and the upper half flips: 000 through 011 are still +0 through +3, but 100 through 111 become −4, −3, −2, −1. Same bits. Different interpretations.

What's beautiful is that both readings work with ordinary modulo-8 arithmetic. The chip just adds bit patterns and lets the result wrap. Whether you read the result as unsigned or signed, the math comes out right. That's the whole magic of two's complement.

Each slot stores 3 bits. That's all the chip can hold. The number written next to the bits is just our interpretation of those bits. Read them as unsigned and you get 0 to 7. Read the same bits as two's complement and you get +0, +1, +2, +3, then −4, −3, −2, −1. The hardware doesn't care which label we use — it just stores bits and adds them with wraparound. The "negative numbers" exist only in our heads.

Why these particular signed labels? Toggle to two's complement mode and notice: slot 111 is labelled −1, slot 110 is −2, slot 101 is −3, slot 100 is −4. Each negative number −k lives at slot (8 − k). So when the chip computes 3 + (−1), the bits actually added are 011 + 111 = 1010. Mod 8, that wraps to 010, which is +2. The right answer falls out of pure modulo arithmetic — no special sign-handling needed.

An 8-bit chip works exactly the same way: 256 slots, with the upper half (10000000 through 11111111) labelled as −128 through −1 in two's complement. The wheel just has 256 positions instead of 8.

So that's the whole idea. An 8-bit chip is the same picture, just with 256 slots instead of 8. The wraparound from 7 to 0 in the 3-bit example becomes the wraparound from 0xFF to 0x00 in 8 bits. And once you've decided that the second half of the slots (the upper 128) get negative labels, the wraparound from 0x7F (+127) to 0x80 (−128) is just the same crossing, in different clothes.

This view also makes it obvious why sign-magnitude and one's complement don't work cleanly. They impose a sign convention onto the circle that doesn't match the natural way the hardware counts. Adding two numbers in those systems sometimes requires going "the other way", which is why they need special hardware. Two's complement doesn't impose anything. It just reads off the labels the hardware was already using as it walked the circle.

A small detour into where this came from

Two's complement wasn't invented by one person in one place. It evolved over decades, but the earliest known implementation was almost lost to history.

In 1941, a German engineer named Helmut Schreyer was building an electronic version of Konrad Zuse's mechanical computer, the Z3. Schreyer's prototype, which has no agreed-upon name, was the first electronic computing machine to use two's complement for negative numbers. The machine itself was destroyed in an Allied bombing of Berlin in 1943, along with most of Zuse's lab. Schreyer survived the war, emigrated to Brazil, and his role in computing history was largely forgotten until the 1990s.

For the next twenty years, computers experimented. Sign-magnitude was common in early American machines (the IBM 7090). One's complement powered the influential PDP-1 in 1959, and lived on in the CDC 6600 supercomputers. But by the late 1960s, two's complement had quietly won. The reason was practical, not theoretical: integrated-circuit designers building ALUs in silicon counted gates, and two's complement needed fewer gates than either alternative for the same functionality.

The IBM System/360 in 1964 helped lock it in. The DEC PDP-11 in 1970 cemented it. By the time microprocessors arrived in the mid-1970s — the Intel 8080, the MOS 6502, the Motorola 6800 — two's complement was the only choice anyone seriously considered. Today, every chip you'll touch as an embedded ML engineer (ARM, x86, RISC-V, ESP32, every accelerator) uses it. There's no real alternative anymore.

The mental model that unlocks everything

Now I want to give you the model that, in my experience, makes two's complement go from "weird trick" to "obviously correct." It's the bit-weight model.

In two's complement, each bit position has a numerical weight. The value of the number is just the sum of the weights of the bits that are set. The weights are powers of two, exactly like in unsigned binary, with one critical exception: the most significant bit has a negative weight.

For 8-bit two's complement:

bit: 7 6 5 4 3 2 1 0 weight: -128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1

The MSB (bit 7) has weight −128. Every other bit has its normal positive weight. To find the value of a byte, just add up the weights of the set bits.

Try it on 1111 1011, which we said was −5:

bit 7: 1 → -128 bit 6: 1 → +64 bit 5: 1 → +32 bit 4: 1 → +16 bit 3: 1 → +8 bit 2: 0 → 0 bit 1: 1 → +2 bit 0: 1 → +1 sum: -128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 2 + 1 = -5 ✓

The same mechanical "add up the weights" procedure that works for unsigned binary works for signed two's complement, with one tiny adjustment to the MSB. You will use this constantly. It's the move I want you to reach for whenever you see a signed byte and want its value.

Notice that 0x7F and 0x80 sit right next to each other in the byte values, but they're at the extreme opposite ends of the int8 range. 0x7F is +127 (max). 0x80 is −128 (min). One bit-flip and you've gone from the largest positive to the most negative number representable. This little fact is the source of countless wrap-around bugs.

Why int8 is asymmetric

Now we can answer a question that confuses every newcomer to quantisation. Why does int8 go from −128 to +127, instead of −127 to +127 (which would be symmetric, and frankly nicer)?

The bit-weight table tells you immediately. The smallest possible int8 is the one where only bit 7 is set:

1000 0000 = -128 + 0 + 0 + ... + 0 = -128

The largest possible int8 is the one where bit 7 is clear and all the others are set:

0111 1111 = 0 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = +127

So the range is exactly −128 to +127. That's 256 distinct values, which is the most you can fit in 8 bits.

The asymmetry is a direct consequence. There are 128 patterns where bit 7 is set, and they're all negative (from −128 up to −1). There are 128 patterns where bit 7 is clear, but one of them is zero, leaving 127 positive numbers. So negatives outnumber positives by exactly one.

Another way to see it: there's no bit pattern for +128, because the only way to express that magnitude with bit 7 set would require pattern 1000 0000, which is already taken by −128. So +128 has nowhere to go in 8 bits.

You can see this asymmetry directly on a number line. As you slide through all 256 possible byte values, watch what happens at the boundary between 0x7F and 0x80. The signed value flips from +127 to −128 in a single step.

How quantisation lives with the asymmetry

The asymmetry isn't academic. It shows up directly in how quantised models are stored.

When you quantise a layer of weights to int8, the values get mapped onto the range [−128, +127]. But this range has 128 negative values and only 127 positive values. That bothers people, because the original float32 weights probably had a roughly symmetric distribution around zero. Mapping a symmetric distribution onto an asymmetric range is awkward.

So a common quantisation scheme deliberately throws away the value −128 and uses only [−127, +127]. This is called symmetric quantisation. You give up one of your 256 patterns (you pretend −128 is illegal), and in exchange you get a clean symmetric range, which makes some math operations simpler. Specifically, multiplications can be done without worrying about a special case for −128.

The other scheme keeps all 256 values and uses an asymmetric range. This is asymmetric quantisation, and it tracks the natural int8 range exactly. It's slightly more efficient with the bits, but the math is a touch fussier.

Both schemes are real. TFLite Micro supports both. Which one a model uses is a hyperparameter of the quantisation, often chosen per-layer based on what the weight distribution looks like. But you cannot reason about this choice — or even understand the names — without knowing why int8 is asymmetric in the first place. And now you do.

Sign extension: the bug that bites everyone once

One last critical concept, and then we land. Whenever you copy a small signed value into a wider register, the hardware (or the compiler, or you) has to handle the sign correctly. Get this wrong and your math goes silently sideways.

Suppose you have an int8 with value −5, which is 1111 1011. You want to put it into a 32-bit register so you can use it in wider arithmetic, like accumulating a dot product. What should the 32 bits look like?

The naive answer is to pad with zeros on the left:

0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 1111 1011 // WRONG

This bit pattern, interpreted as int32, is +251, not −5. By padding with zeros, we made the MSB of the wider value clear, so the negative weight didn't apply. We've silently turned −5 into a positive number, and there's no warning anywhere.

The correct way is sign extension: pad with copies of the original sign bit.

1111 1111 1111 1111 1111 1111 1111 1011 // CORRECT

Now the MSB of the int32 is 1, the negative weight (−2³¹) kicks in, and adding up all the set bits gives −5.

This isn't optional. Every time you cross a width boundary with a signed value, sign extension has to happen. The C compiler does this automatically when you write (int32_t)an_int8. ARM has dedicated single-cycle instructions for it (SXTB sign-extends a byte to a word). Hardware MAC units do it as part of their pipeline. But the moment you start writing custom inference code by hand, especially anything with packed narrow values, you have to know this exists.

For embedded ML this matters because every MAC operation in a quantised network sign-extends both operands. CMSIS-NN does this. TFLite Micro does this. The hardware MAC instructions on Cortex-M4 (SMLAD) do this. If you ever skip it, your model is broken in a way that doesn't crash and doesn't error. It just produces wrong answers.

Where this lands

Five things to take with you.

Two's complement is the convention for signed integers in modern hardware. Every CPU you'll touch as an embedded ML engineer uses it. Every quantised neural network's int8 weights are stored in it. There is no real alternative anymore.

Negation is "invert all the bits, then add 1". That single small rule fixes everything that was wrong with sign-magnitude and one's complement, and gives the hardware free subtraction.

The bit-weight model is your superpower. The MSB has weight −2N−1. Every other bit has its normal positive power-of-two weight. Add up the weights of the set bits. That's the value. Use this every time you see a signed byte.

Int8 ranges from −128 to +127, asymmetrically, and that's a direct consequence of two's complement. This is why quantisation schemes have to choose between symmetric (sacrifice −128, get clean math) and asymmetric (use the full range, slightly fussier math).

Sign extension preserves the value when you go to a wider type. Replicate the MSB into the high bits. The compiler does this automatically with proper casts. Every MAC operation in a quantised network sign-extends both operands. Skipping it is a silent, model-breaking bug.

Next post we look at what happens when arithmetic almost goes wrong. Two's complement gives us correct addition, but only up to the point where the result still fits. What happens beyond that is the story of overflow, carry, and the saturation arithmetic that quantised networks rely on to not produce garbage at every layer boundary.
