Digital Logic for the Embedded ML Engineer

Overflow and Saturation

The math that almost goes wrong, why hardware tracks two kinds of "almost," and the small mercy that keeps quantised neural networks from producing nonsense.

Where we left off

Last lesson we saw that fixed-width arithmetic always wraps around. Add +127 + 1 in int8 and you get −128. Add +100 + +100 and you get −56 instead of +200. The hardware adds the bits and discards the carry-out. The mathematical truth doesn't fit in 8 bits, and the chip silently gives you something wrong-looking but consistent with its modular reality.

This post is about the next two questions. When does that wrap-around behaviour matter? And what does the hardware do to either tell us it happened, or to prevent the catastrophe in the first place?

This matters enormously for embedded ML. Every neural network layer is, at its heart, a giant sum of products. If those sums silently wrap when they get too big, your model produces garbage. The fact that quantised inference works at all in practice depends on the hardware giving us tools to manage this. Those tools are the topic of this post.

What overflow actually is

Overflow is what happens when the true mathematical result of an operation doesn't fit in the register's width, so the hardware loses some of the high bits and you end up with a wrapped result.

Take a simple example. Add 200 + 100 as 8-bit unsigned. The math says 300, but uint8 can only hold 0 to 255. Doing the bit-level addition:

1100 1000 (200) + 0110 0100 (100) = 1 0010 1100 (would-be 300, but in 9 bits)

The result wants to be 9 bits long. Our register only holds 8. So the high bit is discarded, and we're left with 0010 1100, which is 44 as an unsigned byte. The chip stores 44.

That high bit didn't just vanish. The chip knows it threw a bit away, and it records the fact in a one-bit register called a flag. Specifically, this kind of overflow goes into the carry flag. The flag's job is to remember: "did the last addition produce a carry-out from the top of the register?" If yes, the carry flag becomes 1. If no, it stays 0.

So for our example: the chip stores 44 in the result register, sets the carry flag to 1, and moves on. If your code checks the carry flag right after the operation, it knows overflow happened. If you don't check, the chip continues happily with the wrapped result.

Overflow isn't a hardware error. It's a normal consequence of fixed-width arithmetic. The hardware tells you it happened by setting a flag, and what you do with that information is your problem.

The complication: there are two kinds

Everything I just said was about unsigned overflow. The carry flag detects when an unsigned result doesn't fit. But you know from the last lesson that the same bit patterns can also be read as signed two's complement. And the question "did this overflow?" has a different answer depending on which interpretation we're using.

Here's a small example that makes the point. Take +90 + +60 as int8 signed:

0101 1010 (+90) + 0011 1100 (+60) = 1001 0110 (still 8 bits, no carry-out)

The result fits in 8 bits. Nothing got carried out. The carry flag is 0, because as unsigned arithmetic, this was just 90 + 60 = 150, which is fine in 0–255.

But the bit pattern 1001 0110 as int8 is −106. Two positive operands produced a negative result. We expected +150 but +150 doesn't fit in int8 (max is +127), so it wrapped past the +127 / −128 seam and landed at −106. As signed arithmetic, this overflowed.

So the same operation can overflow as signed and not overflow as unsigned. The hardware has to track these separately. It does, with a second flag called the overflow flag, sometimes called the V flag (for "oVerflow"). Its job is to detect signed overflow specifically.

For our +90 + +60 example: result 1001 0110, carry flag = 0 (no unsigned overflow), V flag = 1 (signed overflow happened).

Why both flags exist

The hardware doesn't know whether you intended signed or unsigned arithmetic. It just adds bits. So it computes both overflow conditions and exposes them as two separate flags. Your code (or the type system in your compiler) decides which flag actually matters based on what type the operands were declared as.

On ARM Cortex-M chips, these flags live in the APSR register: C for carry, V for signed overflow, plus N (result was negative) and Z (result was zero). These four flags drive every conditional branch on the chip.

A small detour into where the carry flag came from

The carry flag goes back almost as far as electronic computers. Tom Kilburn, working at the University of Manchester in 1948, designed what's now called the Manchester Baby — the first stored-program computer. Its successor, the Manchester Mark 1 (operational in 1949), was one of the earliest machines to expose a "C bit" to programmers, recording the carry-out from arithmetic operations. The idea was simple but consequential: rather than silently wrapping, the chip would tell you it happened, so you could chain arithmetic across multiple words.

The signed overflow flag came later, as a refinement. Through the 1960s, as ALU designers added native two's complement support, they realised the carry flag alone wasn't sufficient — you needed a second bit to detect the case where the signed interpretation wrapped without an unsigned carry. The V flag entered standard ISA designs with machines like the IBM System/360 (1964) and was firmly entrenched by the time of the DEC PDP-11 (1970). Every modern chip — ARM, x86, RISC-V — descends from this design lineage. The four-flag set (N, Z, C, V) is so universal at this point that you can read assembly code from almost any chip and recognise the pattern.

The rule of thumb that catches signed overflow

You don't need to understand the gates that compute the V flag (it's an XOR of two carries inside the adder). You just need a rule of thumb that lets you spot signed overflow by looking at the operands and the result.

Signed overflow happens when you add two numbers of the same sign and the result has the opposite sign.

Some quick examples:

  • +90 + +60 = -106. Two positives, negative result. Overflow.
  • −100 + (−50) = +106. Two negatives, positive result. Overflow.
  • +100 + (−30) = +70. Different signs. Can never overflow.
  • +50 + +50 = +100. Two positives, positive result. No overflow.

Why? Because adding same-sign numbers is the only way to walk off either end of the signed range. Two positives can only push you past +127. Two negatives can only push you past −128. Mixed signs partially cancel and stay safely inside.

This is why every modern chip computes both flags simultaneously and lets the program choose which one matters.

Why this is dangerous in practice

Overflow becomes a problem because most code doesn't check the flags. C doesn't expose them directly. When you write int8_t y = x + 1;, the addition just happens. If x was 127 and the addition overflows, y is now −128. Your code keeps running. There's no error, no warning, no log entry. The math is silently wrong.

For ordinary code this is annoying but manageable, because overflow only happens at very specific edge cases. For ML inference, it's catastrophic, because the math is constantly pushing against the limits.

Where exactly the danger is in inference

Let me walk through what happens inside one layer of a quantised neural network and pinpoint the dangerous moment, because the danger isn't where most people initially expect.

Each output neuron in a layer is computed as a sum of products: w₁·a₁ + w₂·a₂ + ... + wₙ·aₙ, where the weights and activations are int8 values, and N might be hundreds or thousands.

Stage by stage, here's what the magnitudes look like:

Stage 1: One product, w·a. Maximum magnitude is roughly 127 × 128 ≈ 16,000. Doesn't fit in int8, but fits comfortably in int16 (range up to 32,767). Hardware multiplies into a wider register. No overflow here.

Stage 2: Sum of products into the accumulator. Hundreds of int16 products get summed. The sum can grow to millions. Hardware uses an int32 accumulator, which can hold values up to about 2 billion. Plenty of headroom. No overflow here either.

Stage 3: Convert the int32 accumulator to int8 for the next layer. The next layer expects int8 inputs. We have to compress our big int32 sum down to a single int8. This is the cliff. The accumulator is regularly far outside the int8 range, and we have to choose how to handle that.

So the dangerous moment isn't inside the math. The math has plenty of room to grow. The cliff is at the layer boundary, when we're forced to fit a 32-bit accumulator value into 8 bits to feed the next layer.

The naive fix: truncation, and why it fails

The simplest way to compress an int32 to int8 is to keep the low 8 bits and throw the rest away. In C:

int8_t output = (int8_t)acc; // keep low 8 bits, reinterpret as int8

This is called a truncation cast. Mathematically, it's the same as taking acc mod 256 and reinterpreting those bits as signed. And the result is essentially random in a way that destroys the model.

Watch what happens for a real-looking accumulator value of +450. Its 32-bit binary is:

0000 0000 0000 0000 0000 0001 1100 0010

The low 8 bits are 1100 0010. As int8, that's −128 + 64 + 2 = −62. So truncation tells the next layer this neuron's output is −62. But the actual accumulator was a large positive number. The sign is wrong. The magnitude is wrong. The next layer reads "slightly negative signal" when the truth was "very strongly positive."

Here's what makes truncation truly broken: the result has no relationship to magnitude. If the accumulator had been +449, truncation would give −63. If it had been +700, truncation would give +(−68). If it had been +5000, truncation would give some other unrelated value. The output cycles through all 256 int8 values as the input grows, with no relation to whether the input was large or small, positive or negative.

Truncation isn't approximate. It's random-looking, with no relation to the magnitude or sign of the original. For ML inference, this destroys the model.

The right fix: saturation

Saturation is a different way of doing the compression. Instead of keeping low bits, it clamps:

  • If the value is greater than +127, return +127.
  • If the value is less than −128, return −128.
  • Otherwise, return the value as-is.

For our accumulator value of +450, saturation gives +127. The largest representable int8.

Why is +127 a much better answer than −62? Think about what each result means to the next layer.

Truncation said −62: "this neuron is firing slightly in the negative direction." That's a complete lie. The actual computation said this neuron is firing very strongly positive.

Saturation says +127: "this neuron is firing maximally in the positive direction." That's the closest the int8 representation can come to the truth. We've lost some magnitude (we can't tell the next layer whether the real value was +450, +500, or +5000 — they all saturate to the same +127), but we've kept the sign and the character of the signal. The next layer can do something useful with "strong positive signal." It cannot do anything useful with "slightly negative" when the truth is the opposite.

Truncation can flip the sign. Saturation can't. For neural networks, that's the whole game.

This single sign-flip on a single neuron's output is enough to destroy a model.

How real chips do this in one cycle

The C code for saturation works, but it has two branches in it (the two if statements). Branches are slow on simple chips because they confuse the pipeline. So real chips designed for signal processing or ML have a single instruction that does the saturation in one cycle.

On ARM Cortex-M4 and M7, there's an instruction called SSAT. You write SSAT R0, #8, R1 and it saturates the value in R1 to fit in 8 bits, putting the result in R0. One instruction. One cycle. No branches.

This is part of a whole family of saturating instructions on the Cortex-M4, all prefixed with Q:

  • QADD8 — adds four pairs of int8 numbers in parallel, all with saturation.
  • QADD16 — same idea for two pairs of int16.
  • QSUB8, QSUB16 — saturating subtraction.
  • QADD — saturating 32-bit add.

These are part of ARM's DSP extensions. Critically, not every ARM chip has them. The Cortex-M0 and M0+ don't. The Cortex-M4, M7, M33, and M55 do. This is one of the concrete reasons a Cortex-M4 is dramatically faster at neural network inference than an M0+: it has dedicated hardware for the operations that quantised inference depends on most, and saturation is one of those operations.

ESP32 has its own equivalents (the Xtensa LX7 DSP instructions, and the newer RISC-V variants have similar). Modern accelerators (Coral Edge TPU, ARM Ethos-U, custom NPUs) bake saturation directly into their MAC pipelines. The accelerator doesn't even give you the option to skip it.

On the software side, ARM's CMSIS-NN library uses saturation everywhere. A typical line in their convolution kernel looks like this:

out[i] = (int8_t)__SSAT(acc, 8);

The __SSAT(acc, 8) is a compiler intrinsic that emits an SSAT instruction. On a Cortex-M4 this compiles to one cycle. On a Cortex-M0+, the compiler falls back to a software implementation with branches, taking many more cycles. The same library, the same code, but a 4–8× performance difference depending on the chip.

Where this lands

Five things to take with you.

The hardware is always doing modular arithmetic. When the true result doesn't fit, the chip wraps. We saw this last lesson; this lesson was about what we do about it.

There are two kinds of overflow, signed and unsigned, and the hardware tracks them with separate flags. The carry flag (C) signals unsigned overflow. The overflow flag (V) signals signed overflow. The same operation can set one without the other.

The wraparound bug is dangerous because it doesn't crash. Your code keeps running, producing wrong values that look superficially reasonable. In ML inference this means broken models with no error message.

Saturation arithmetic clamps to the max or min instead of wrapping. Cortex-M4 and M7 provide saturating instructions (the Q family and SSAT). Modern accelerators bake saturation into their MAC pipelines. CMSIS-NN uses it everywhere.

Quantised neural network inference depends on saturation at every layer boundary. When the int32 accumulator gets compressed back to int8 for the next layer, that compression must saturate. Skip it and your model produces nonsense — not subtly wrong, but completely meaningless, with neuron outputs that can have the opposite sign from the truth.
