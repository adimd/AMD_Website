Digital Logic for the Embedded ML Engineer

Reading the Language of Hardware

Binary, hex, and the moment when a wall of numbers stops being noise and starts telling you a story.

A scene at a debugger

You're trying to figure out why your quantised model is producing garbage on a tiny chip. You attach a debugger, dump the memory where the weights are supposed to live, and you see this.

0x08010000:  7F 81 00 FF C3 3C 80 7F  40 C0 01 FE 5A A5 33 CC

To one engineer, this is sixteen pairs of letters and numbers. Noise. They might open a calculator, type in each pair, slowly translate, and by the time they're done they've forgotten what they were even looking for.

To another engineer, this same dump is information. They glance at it and think: that 7F at the start is the largest int8, the 80 in the middle is the smallest int8, the 00 is zero, the FF is going to turn out to be a small negative number when we get there, and 40 next to C0 looks suspiciously like a near-mirror pair. Ten seconds. They've already learned that the model's weights are using the full int8 range, that the quantisation looks healthy, and that the bug is probably somewhere else.

Same dump. Two completely different reads. The second engineer has fluency in the language of hardware. That language is binary, but nobody actually reads binary with their eyes. We read its compressed form, hex.

Fluency in hex is what turns hardware noise into hardware information.

This post is about how that fluency works, and why it's built on one elegant little fact about the number 16.

Why binary at all

Before we touch hex, it's worth grounding binary in physical reality. Every digital chip is built from transistors, and a transistor is essentially a tiny electronic switch. Either it lets current through or it doesn't. There's no halfway. So every piece of information stored on a chip lives, ultimately, as the on-or-off state of some switch.

People have tried building hardware with three or even ten distinguishable voltage levels. It was a disaster every time. Manufacturing variation kills you. A bit of noise on a wire turns a 7 into an 8. With only two states, you can leave a huge gap between "low voltage" and "high voltage", and you can drop a fairly noisy signal into a digital circuit and it still gets read correctly. The only question being asked is "high or low?", and that's a question even messy electronics can answer reliably.

So binary won not because it's mathematically pretty but because it's robust. Two states is the most reliable way to push information through unreliable physical hardware. The whole digital world rests on this one engineering compromise.

A bit is one switch. A byte is eight switches arranged in a row. A 32-bit register on a Cortex-M chip is thirty-two switches you can read and write together. When you write 01011010 on paper, you're describing the state of eight tiny physical things on a piece of silicon. That's the foundation we'll keep coming back to.

Why we don't read binary directly

Try this:

01001000 01100101 01101100 01101100 01101111

That's "Hello" in ASCII. It's correct. It's exactly what a chip stores when you give it that string. And it's nearly unreadable.

Three problems. It's long, and even short values fill up your line. You can't count bits at a glance, so figuring out which bit is "the fifth one from the right" turns into a chore. And long runs of 0s and 1s blur into each other, so two bytes side by side end up looking like one big featureless stripe.

We need something more compact. Something easier to read. But it shouldn't lose any information, because eventually we will want to look at individual bits, and we need to be able to. The notation has to compress without flattening.

That something is hex.

The trick that makes hex work

The whole reason hex exists is one small fact that I want you to commit to memory:

16 = 24

Sixteen is two to the fourth. Which means each digit in base 16 corresponds to exactly four binary digits. Always. No fractional digits. No awkward boundaries. One hex digit packs exactly four bits.

This sounds dry written down. It's actually the elegant move that makes the whole notation work. Computers think in bytes (8 bits) and words (16, 32, 64 bits), and all of these are multiples of 4. Hex divides them cleanly. A byte is exactly two hex digits. A 16-bit halfword is exactly four hex digits. A 32-bit word is exactly eight hex digits. The byte boundaries are visible at a glance because they fall right in the spacing between hex digits.

Compare that to decimal. Decimal is base 10, and 10 is not a power of 2, so a byte (range 0 to 255) ends up taking "1 to 3 decimal digits". Look at the number 1234567 and try to tell me where the byte boundaries are. You can't, because there aren't any in decimal. In hex you'd write it as 0x12 D6 87 and the boundaries are right there in the spaces.

Other power-of-two bases were tried. Octal (base 8) gives you 2.67 hex digits per byte, which is awful. Base 32 gives you 1.6 digits per byte, also awful. Base 256 would give one digit per byte but you'd need 256 distinct symbols, which would be unreadable. Sixteen sits in the sweet spot: small enough to write with familiar symbols, big enough to compress meaningfully, divides byte widths cleanly. That's the whole story of why we use it.

Conversion in both directions

Once you've got the table, the conversions are mechanical. Both directions are pure substitution, not arithmetic. If you find yourself multiplying or adding anything, you've taken a wrong turn.

Hex to binary

Take each hex digit independently and replace it with its 4-bit pattern. That's the whole procedure.

0xC3 → C is 1100, 3 is 0011 → 1100 0011.

0x5A3F → 5 is 0101, A is 1010, 3 is 0011, F is 1111 → 0101 1010 0011 1111.

I always put spaces between the 4-bit groups when writing binary by hand. The spaces are visual punctuation. They show you the byte boundaries, and they keep your eyes from drifting.

Binary to hex

Group the binary digits into chunks of four, starting from the right, and convert each group.

11010110 → group from the right as 1101 0110 → 1101 is D, 0110 is 6 → 0xD6.

The "from the right" part actually matters. If your binary string isn't a multiple of four bits, the leftover bits go on the left and get padded with zeros. Take a 9-bit number like 110110110. Group from the right: 1 1011 0110. Pad the leftmost group with zeros to make it four bits: 0001 1011 0110. Convert: 0x1B6. If you'd grouped from the left instead, you'd have multiplied your value by some power of two and gotten a completely wrong answer.

The nibble

Half a byte. Four bits. One hex digit's worth. The reason this little word exists is because of hex itself: a nibble is the natural unit when you're reading hardware.

Visualise a byte as two nibbles side by side. The high nibble lives in bits 7 through 4. The low nibble lives in bits 3 through 0. Each one shows up as one hex digit when you write the byte out.

The byte shown above is 10011100, which is 0x9C. Bit 0 is on the right. Bit 7 is on the left. This is the convention everywhere in modern hardware. The high nibble holds the upper four bits and shows up as the first hex digit. The low nibble holds the lower four and shows up as the second.

This matters because every microcontroller datasheet you'll ever read describes registers in nibble-aligned fields. Once you can mentally split a byte into its high and low nibble, datasheets stop looking like hieroglyphics and start looking like structured information.

Reading a real byte, slowly

Let me walk through one example end to end so you see the mental motion.

Suppose I tell you a quantised neural network weight is stored as the byte 0x9C. You want to know what it's doing.

First, hex to binary. 9 is 1001, C is 1100. So 0x9C is 10011100.

Now you can read what each bit is doing. Bit 7 is set. Bit 6 is clear. Bit 5 is clear. Bit 4 is set. Bit 3 is set. Bit 2 is set. Bit 1 is clear. Bit 0 is clear.

In the next post, we'll learn how to interpret this as a signed number, and the answer is going to come out to -100. For now, just notice what you did. You went from 0x9C to a fully transparent view of every bit, without ever opening a calculator. That's fluency. That's the whole point of this lesson.

The diagnostic mindset

Here's why this is worth practising. Once you can read hex bytes at a glance, hardware artifacts become readable. Memory dumps, register descriptions, oscilloscope decoders, debugger output, weight buffers. They all speak this same language, and they all become legible.

The dump tells a story: this layer's weights span the full int8 range, with rough symmetry around zero. The quantisation is healthy. Whatever bug you're chasing, it's somewhere else.

Three traps

Three things will bite you if you don't see them coming. They're all small, and they're all common.

The prefix matters. In C code, hex literals start with 0x. So 0xFF is 255. But what about 0FF without the x? That's a syntax error. And what about 017? In C, a number that starts with just 0 is octal, not hex. So 017 in C is octal seventeen, which is decimal 15. Real bug source. Always use the 0x prefix for hex literals in your code. In datasheets, you'll see hex written as 0xFF, FFh, or sometimes just FF with the base implied by context. Always check.

Bit numbering starts at 0. The rightmost bit of a byte is bit 0. The leftmost is bit 7. So when code says (value >> 3) & 1, it extracts bit 3, which is the fourth bit from the right. When a datasheet says "bit 7 of GPIOA_MODER", it means the eighth bit from the right. This is the convention everywhere in ARM, ESP32, x86, RISC-V. Burning this in early saves you a year of off-by-one bugs later.

The same byte means different things in different contexts. The byte 0x80, which is 10000000, can mean three different things. As an unsigned 8-bit number it's 128. As a signed 8-bit number it's -128. As a flag pattern it's "bit 7 set, all others clear". Three different interpretations of the same eight switches. Which one is right depends entirely on how the variable is typed in your code or defined in the datasheet. The hardware just stores bits. The meaning lives in your head and your code. This is exactly why int8_t and uint8_t exist as separate types in C: they make the interpretation explicit. In embedded code, never use plain int or char for hardware-facing work. The signedness of char is implementation-defined, which is a polite way of saying "different compilers will silently disagree about what your code means". Always be explicit.

Where this lands

Five things to take away from this lesson.

Binary is the language of hardware because two-state circuits are the most reliable way to store information physically. A bit is one transistor's state.

Hex exists because 16 = 24, which means every hex digit corresponds to exactly four bits, and every byte to exactly two hex digits. It's a compression of binary that preserves bit-level visibility.

Conversions between hex and binary are pure substitution, not arithmetic. Each hex digit maps to a 4-bit pattern, both directions.

A nibble is four bits, one hex digit. Datasheets are written in nibble-aligned fields. This is the natural unit of hardware.

Fluency is diagnostic. A trained eye scans a hex dump and extracts information about ranges, symmetry, and patterns. An untrained eye sees noise. The whole point of this lesson is to start that training.
