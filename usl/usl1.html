Uncovering Patterns in Data : An Intuition-First Guide to Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised Learning Through the Lens of Data Compression

Why understanding data begins with storage, memory, and reconstruction

Let me start this series in a way that may feel slightly… unromantic. Not with “learning”, not with “patterns”, not with “AI”, and definitely not with clustering.

Let’s start with something brutally practical: memory.

Because if you think about it long enough, unsupervised learning is not a mystical branch of machine learning. It’s a very engineer-ish question wearing a math costume: “Given a pile of data, what is the smartest way to store it and still be able to reconstruct something meaningful?”

In other words: unsupervised learning is compression. And compression is just understanding with a storage budget.

Rethinking “learning” as a storage problem

Here’s the mental shift I want you to make right now. For this series, don’t imagine yourself as a data scientist staring at scatter plots and vibes. Imagine yourself as a computational engineer with limited RAM, limited bandwidth, and a very annoying constraint:

You have data. You need to store it. Later, you need to reconstruct it (or at least reconstruct what matters).

That’s it. That’s the whole setup. No labels. No “correct answers”. No teacher giving you an output column.

And since we’re talking like engineers, we begin with a question that sounds trivial but is actually the doorway into everything:

How many real numbers do I need to store this dataset?

Starting simple: a tiny dataset (no abstraction yet)

Let me give you an intentionally small dataset. Four points. Two dimensions. Each point is a vector in \( \mathbb{R}^2 \).

Figure 1 — A toy dataset: four points in \( \mathbb{R}^2 \).

\[ x_1 = [-7,\; -14], \quad x_2 = [2.5,\; 5], \quad x_3 = [0.5,\; 1], \quad x_4 = [0,\; 0] \]

Nothing fancy. Just numbers. Now I want you to pause and genuinely answer this without skipping:

If you had to store this dataset on a machine, how many real numbers do you need?

The naive answer: brute force storage

Your first instinct is completely reasonable: four vectors, two numbers each. That’s 8 real numbers.

This is the most straightforward representation: store every coordinate of every point. No compression. No cleverness. Just memory.

Figure 2 — Naive storage: 4 points × 2 coordinates = 8 numbers.

And to be fair: this approach works. You can reconstruct the dataset perfectly, because you literally stored everything.

But here’s the uncomfortable part: this is not understanding. This is memorization.

If the dataset becomes 4 million points instead of 4 points, your strategy does not become smarter — it just becomes more expensive. More storage. More bandwidth. More pain.

So the engineer in you should ask: “Are we wasting memory by storing redundant information?”

Look again: is the data really using all its dimensions?

Let’s stare at the points again:

\[ [-7,-14],\; [2.5,5],\; [0.5,1],\; [0,0] \]

Do you notice something? The second coordinate seems suspiciously predictable from the first.

In fact:

\[ -14 = 2 \cdot (-7), \quad 5 = 2 \cdot (2.5), \quad 1 = 2 \cdot (0.5), \quad 0 = 2 \cdot (0) \]

That means every point lies on the line: \( y = 2x \).

Figure 3 — All points lie perfectly on the line \( y = 2x \).

Pause here. If the data lies on a line, then the second coordinate is not carrying independent information. It is completely redundant.

And redundancy is exactly what compression exploits.

A smarter representation: store structure, not repetition

Once you see the line, a more intelligent storage plan appears. Instead of storing both coordinates of every point, you store:

Plan:

1) Store the slope of the line (which is 2)

2) Store only the x-coordinate of each point

Let’s count again:

\[ \text{Total storage} = 1 \; (\text{slope}) + 4 \; (x\text{-values}) = 5 \text{ numbers} \]

Compressed Representation

We went from 8 numbers to 5 numbers with zero information loss.

Nothing magical happened. We didn’t predict anything. We simply noticed structure and stopped storing the same information twice.

Now that we’ve tasted compression… we get greedy

A natural question follows: can we compress even more?

The line \( y = 2x \) can also be represented by a direction vector, for example \( [1,2] \).

Every point in the dataset is a scalar multiple of this vector:

\[ x_1 = -7 \cdot [1,2], \quad x_2 = 2.5 \cdot [1,2], \quad x_3 = 0.5 \cdot [1,2], \quad x_4 = 0 \cdot [1,2] \]

Figure 4 — Each point is a scalar multiple of a single representative direction.

Now count again: 2 numbers for the representative vector, 4 numbers for the coefficients. That’s 6 numbers.

Which is worse than our slope-based representation. Compression is not automatic — representation choices matter.

The uncomfortable realization

We started with 8 numbers. We compressed to 5. We tried a different representation and landed at 6.

So here’s the uncomfortable question:

What if 5 numbers is the best possible lossless representation of this dataset?

What if there is no more redundancy to exploit? What if we’ve hit the intrinsic cost of exact reconstruction?

The fork in the road

Once you think in terms of memory and reconstruction, there are only two paths forward.

Option 1: Exact reconstruction (lossless)

You store fewer numbers than the naive approach, but insist on perfect reconstruction. Eventually, you hit an irreducible limit.

Option 2: Remember less (lossy)

You accept approximation. You store fewer numbers by discarding details. Reconstruction is no longer perfect — but may still be useful.

This trade-off is the heart of unsupervised learning.

Where we stop

This first post does only one thing. It reframes unsupervised learning as a storage problem.

In the next post, we will take a dataset that is almost compressible — and see why approximation is no longer optional.
