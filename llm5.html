1. Looking at the Previous Chapter Through a Mathematical Lens

In the previous chapter, we stayed mostly at the intuitive level. We talked about:

  • treating each (center, context) pair as a tiny teaching signal,
  • giving every word two roles – as a center (W) and as a context (C),
  • and the idea that repeated updates slowly shape a meaningful embedding space.

In this chapter, we will re-tell exactly the same story, but now we will put on a mathematical lens. We will use a tiny universe of sentences and make every step explicit: how we extract pairs, how we assign vectors, how we compute a score, and what it means to “correct” W and C over time.

We are not adding new concepts here.
We are simply writing down, in equations, what the previous chapter described in words.

To keep things concrete and visual, we will:

  • fix the embedding dimension to 2 (d = 2), so every word is a 2D vector,
  • use only four simple sentences as our full training universe.

2. Our Tiny Training Corpus: Four Sentences

Our entire world of language is:

I love my dog.
I love my cat.
You love my dog.
You love my cat.

From these four sentences, our vocabulary is:

  • I
  • You
  • love
  • my
  • dog
  • cat

We will give each of these words:

  • a 2D vector when it is the center of an event (this lives in W),
  • a 2D vector when it is a context word (this lives in C).

Before we assign vectors, we first need to understand what the training data looks like from the point of view of the model. That means extracting the (center, context) events.

3. From Sentences to Training Events

We choose a simple rule: context window = 1. That means each word looks only at its immediate left and right neighbours. For each sentence, we slide this window and record (center → context) pairs.

3.1. Sentence: “I love my dog”

Tokens (ignoring punctuation): [I, love, my, dog]

  • Center = love → contexts = I (left), my (right)
  • Center = my → contexts = love (left), dog (right)
  • Center = dog → context = my (left)

So from this sentence we get:

  • (love → I)
  • (love → my)
  • (my → love)
  • (my → dog)
  • (dog → my)

3.2. Sentence: “I love my cat”

Tokens: [I, love, my, cat]

  • (love → I)
  • (love → my)
  • (my → love)
  • (my → cat)
  • (cat → my)

3.3. Sentence: “You love my dog”

Tokens: [You, love, my, dog]

  • (love → You)
  • (love → my)
  • (my → love)
  • (my → dog)
  • (dog → my)

3.4. Sentence: “You love my cat”

Tokens: [You, love, my, cat]

  • (love → You)
  • (love → my)
  • (my → love)
  • (my → cat)
  • (cat → my)

3.5. Merging all events

If we merge everything (just conceptually, without counting yet), we see:

  • love → I, I, You, You, my, my, my, my (with repetitions)
  • my → love, love, love, love, dog, dog, cat, cat
  • dog → my, my
  • cat → my, my

Already, the patterns are visible:

  • “dog” and “cat” behave in the same way – they both follow “my”.
  • “I” and “You” behave in the same way – they both precede “love”.
  • “my” sits between “love” and a pet.
This is exactly the kind of structure the previous chapter described in words. Now we want to encode it in vectors and numbers.

4. Two Matrices: W and C (with d = 2)

Now we bring in the mathematical objects:

We choose embedding dimension d = 2.

That means:

  • Each W[word] is a vector in ℝ² (2 numbers).
  • Each C[word] is also a vector in ℝ² (another 2 numbers).

If we index the vocabulary as:

  1. I
  2. You
  3. love
  4. my
  5. dog
  6. cat

Then:

  • W is a 6 × 2 matrix – each row is a center-role vector for one word.
  • C is a 6 × 2 matrix – each row is a context-role vector for one word.

At the very start of training, W and C are typically initialised randomly. For our toy example, let’s choose specific numbers (just to be concrete):

4.1. Center-role vectors W (each row is ℝ²)

  • W[I] = (1.2, -0.1)
  • W[You] = (1.1, -0.2)
  • W[love] = (0.7, -0.8)
  • W[my] = (0.3, 0.9)
  • W[dog] = (-0.6, 1.0)
  • W[cat] = (-0.7, 1.1)

4.2. Context-role vectors C (each row is ℝ²)

  • C[I] = (1.0, 0.2)
  • C[You] = (1.1, 0.1)
  • C[love] = (0.5, -0.7)
  • C[my] = (0.3, 0.8)
  • C[dog] = (-0.3, 1.3)
  • C[cat] = (-0.4, 1.2)

These values are not “magical” or “correct”. They are just initial guesses. The whole point of training is:

Use the events from our corpus to keep correcting these vectors until they reflect the patterns in the data.

5. What Each Event Wants: A Compatibility Score

Consider a single event, for example:

Event: (love → I)

From the model’s point of view, this event is saying:

“According to the language, when the center word is ‘love’, the context ‘I’ is a natural neighbour.”

We want a mathematical function that takes W[love] and C[I] and returns a single number – a score – that measures how compatible they currently are.

This score should be:

  • higher when the vectors point in similar directions,
  • lower when they point in opposite directions,
  • smooth, so we can adjust W and C gently over time.

The simplest choice that satisfies this is the dot product:

score(center → context) = W[center] · C[context]

Because our embedding dimension is 2, this dot product is just:

If W[center] = (a, b) and C[context] = (c, d), then W[center] · C[context] = a·c + b·d.

Let’s compute some scores in full detail.

6. Step-by-Step Score Calculations in Our Tiny World

6.1. score(love → I)

We use:

  • W[love] = (0.7, -0.8)
  • C[I] = (1.0, 0.2)

Dot product:

score(love → I) = (0.7 × 1.0) + (-0.8 × 0.2)

Break it down:

  • 0.7 × 1.0 = 0.7
  • -0.8 × 0.2 = -0.16

Add them:

0.7 + (-0.16) = 0.54

So:

score(love → I) = 0.54

6.2. score(love → You)

Use:

  • W[love] = (0.7, -0.8)
  • C[You] = (1.1, 0.1)

Dot product:

score(love → You) = (0.7 × 1.1) + (-0.8 × 0.1)

  • 0.7 × 1.1 = 0.77
  • -0.8 × 0.1 = -0.08

0.77 + (-0.08) = 0.69

score(love → You) = 0.69

Even with random initial values, the scores for (love → I) and (love → You) are already in the same ballpark. Training will later refine this, but the structure is already visible.

6.3. score(my → dog)

  • W[my] = (0.3, 0.9)
  • C[dog] = (-0.3, 1.3)

score(my → dog) = (0.3 × -0.3) + (0.9 × 1.3)

  • 0.3 × -0.3 = -0.09
  • 0.9 × 1.3 = 1.17

Add:

-0.09 + 1.17 = 1.08

score(my → dog) = 1.08

6.4. score(my → cat)

  • W[my] = (0.3, 0.9)
  • C[cat] = (-0.4, 1.2)

score(my → cat) = (0.3 × -0.4) + (0.9 × 1.2)

  • 0.3 × -0.4 = -0.12
  • 0.9 × 1.2 = 1.08

-0.12 + 1.08 = 0.96

score(my → cat) = 0.96

These two scores are very close – again mirroring the symmetry in our corpus: “my” is equally happy to be followed by “dog” or “cat”.

7. Geometric Intuition: Alignment in 2D

Because the embedding dimension is 2, each W[word] and C[word] is literally a point in the plane, or a small arrow from the origin.

When we take the dot product W[center] · C[context], we are measuring how aligned these two arrows are:

  • if they point in a similar direction → dot product is large and positive,
  • if they are perpendicular → dot product is near zero,
  • if they point in opposite directions → dot product becomes negative.
Sketch: 2D arrows for W[my], C[dog], C[cat], showing similar directions placeholder

In 2D, “compatibility” is literally how much two arrows point in the same direction.

So, in geometric language:

The score is just: “how much does the center vector point along the context vector?”

This is why dot product is such a natural choice: it turns geometry (angles and lengths) into a single real number that can be increased or decreased during learning.

8. The Role of Correction: Why W and C Are Never Final

Right now, all scores are computed from random initial W and C. There is no guarantee they match the patterns in the data. In fact, at the start, they mostly don’t.

The whole job of training is:

Look at the events from our corpus, compare what the model believes (via scores) with what the data says, and then nudge W and C so that future scores become more sensible.

For our tiny corpus, “more sensible” means:

  • scores for (love → I) and (love → You) should be high,
  • scores for (my → dog) and (my → cat) should be high,
  • scores for weird, unseen pairs like (dog → You) should be low.

We are not yet defining how to nudge W and C — that will involve probabilities and loss in future chapters. But conceptually, you can already see the loop:

  1. Use current W and C to compute scores for observed pairs.
  2. Decide whether those scores are “too low” or “too high” compared to what the dataset suggests.
  3. Correct W and C a little bit.

Over many corrections, W and C stop being random matrices and start becoming a compressed, geometric summary of our language.

9. Summary: Blog 4, Now Written as Equations

Let’s tie this back to the previous chapter:

  • Previously, we said: “Events like (center, context) teach the model which words belong together.”
  • Here, we formalised that by extracting events from four sentences.
  • Previously, we said: “Each word has a center-role vector and a context-role vector.”
  • Here, we encoded that as two matrices W and C in ℝV×2.
  • Previously, we said: “The model needs to know how compatible two words are.”
  • Here, we turned that into a score: W[center] · C[context].
  • Previously, we talked about adjusting W and C over time.
  • Here, we saw that the whole point of the score is to give us a number we can push up or down during training.
This chapter is the mathematical mirror of the previous one.
Same idea, but now every step lives as a vector in ℝ² and as a dot product score.

In the next chapter, we will ask: “How do we turn these raw scores into something we can interpret and optimise?” That will naturally lead us to ranking contexts, normalising scores, and eventually to the idea of a probability.
