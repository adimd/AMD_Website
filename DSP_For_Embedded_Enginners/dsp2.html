DSP for Embedded Engineers — Topic 2

Sampling and the Nyquist Theorem

How many snapshots per second do you actually need — and why the answer is more subtle than any textbook admits.

The goal is reconstruction, not just measurement

There is a difference between measuring a signal and capturing it. Measuring means getting a number at a moment in time. Capturing means getting enough numbers that you could reconstruct the original smooth wave perfectly from nothing but that list. The second goal is what DSP demands. And the entire theory of sampling is built around answering one question: how many snapshots per second do you need to make that possible?

A sine wave has exactly two unknowns

Start with the simplest possible signal, a pure sine wave at a known frequency. A sine wave can be written as y = A · sin(2π · F · t + φ). F is the frequency, which we treat as known. t is the time of each sample, which you also know because you control the clock. That leaves exactly two things unknown: A, the amplitude, meaning how tall the wave is, and φ, the phase, meaning where in its cycle the wave happens to be at time zero.

Two things. That is all. Two sine waves at the same frequency can still be completely different from each other if one is taller or if one is shifted in time relative to the other. Amplitude is the height. Phase is the shift. Feel that with the visual below before reading on.

Each sample is one equation — two unknowns need two samples

When you take a sample at time t, you get a measured value. Plugging into the formula gives you one equation with two unknowns, A and φ. You cannot solve one equation for two unknowns. There are infinitely many combinations of amplitude and phase that could produce that exact reading at that exact moment.

Take a second sample at a different moment and you have two equations with two unknowns. That has exactly one solution. You have pinned down the wave completely. That is where 2F comes from — not from intuition, not from a rule someone chose, directly from the fact that a sine wave has two free parameters and you need one independent measurement per unknown.

The zero crossing trap — why exactly 2F fails

The theorem says strictly greater than 2F, not equal. Here is exactly why the equality case breaks. Imagine sampling a 100 Hz sine wave at exactly 200 Hz. If your first sample lands at the exact moment the wave crosses zero going upward, the next sample lands exactly half a cycle later at the zero crossing going downward. Both are zero. The one after that is zero again. Every sample forever is zero. You would conclude there is no signal at all, when a full amplitude wave was there the entire time. Your samples kept landing at the exact moments the wave crossed zero, and the wave never had a chance to show you its height.

This is a phase alignment problem. It has nothing to do with hardware. It is pure mathematics. Exactly 2F is the knife edge where this can happen. Greater than 2F means your sampling interval and the signal period are slightly misaligned, so samples drift across the wave's phase over time and you cannot stay stuck at zero forever.

At exactly 200 Hz sampling, slide the phase offset to 0° or 180° and watch all samples collapse to zero. The wave is invisible. Increase the sampling rate and the problem disappears regardless of phase.

Non-zero samples at barely above 2F — still not enough

Here is the question that stopped me when I first learned this. If both my samples are non-zero, I have two non-zero equations and two unknowns, and I can solve them algebraically. So why do I need anything more than barely above 2F?

The answer is noise. Every sample your ADC gives you is the true value plus some small error. Quantization rounding, power supply noise, electrical interference — it is always there. There is no such thing as a perfectly clean sample.

When you sample at barely above 2F, your two samples per cycle are taken very close together in time. On a smooth curve, two points close in time have nearly the same value. Two nearly identical equations are almost as useless as one when solving for two unknowns, because tiny errors in either value wildly change the answer. Think of it like trying to determine the shape of a hill by taking two measurements one metre apart on the same slope. Any measurement error and you have completely the wrong hill. Take measurements on opposite sides and errors barely matter because the two points constrain the answer from completely different directions.

At 10F you have ten samples spread across the full cycle, catching the wave at many different points. Noise in any one sample barely moves the answer because nine others are holding it in place. The theorem gives you the floor for a perfect noiseless world. Noise gives you the real answer for your actual hardware.

At low sampling rates the noisy samples are bunched together and the reconstruction is wildly wrong. Increase the rate and samples spread across the wave — reconstruction stabilises even with the same amount of noise.

Why real signals follow the same rules as sine waves

Everything above assumed a pure sine wave. Real signals — a vibration from a motor bearing, audio from a microphone, a current sensor reading — look nothing like clean sine waves. So why does any of this apply?

Because of a fact called the Fourier theorem, which we will spend a full topic on later in this series. Any signal, no matter how complicated, can be broken down exactly into a sum of pure sine waves at different frequencies, amplitudes and phases. Not approximately. Exactly. A square wave, a noisy vibration trace, your voice recording, all of them are secretly just collections of sine waves added together.

This means capturing any signal faithfully is the same as capturing every sine wave component it contains. Each component needs 2F samples per second where F is that component's own frequency. The hardest one to capture is always the highest frequency component. So if you sample fast enough for the highest frequency, you automatically capture everything below it too. That is the complete justification for the Nyquist rule. Sample at at least twice the highest frequency in your signal.

The Nyquist frequency is a contract

When you set your sampling rate you are making a commitment about your signal. You are saying: I will faithfully capture everything up to Fs divided by 2. That upper boundary is called the Nyquist frequency. For an Arduino sampling at 9600 Hz it is 4800 Hz. For an STM32 sampling a vibration sensor at 25600 Hz it is 12800 Hz. These are not soft guidelines. They are hard limits set by mathematics.

Most embedded engineers pick a sampling rate based on how fast their main loop runs, or what the example code used. That is the wrong instinct. The sampling rate should be chosen based on the signal — specifically, based on the highest frequency in that signal that you actually need to capture. Everything downstream follows from that one decision.

What happens above Nyquist — a warning for the next topic

One thing worth naming before we close. Frequencies above your Nyquist frequency do not quietly disappear from your data. They fold back in, disguised as lower frequencies that were never in your original signal. This phenomenon is called aliasing, and it deserves its own full treatment. We will take it apart completely in Topic 4, where we will see exactly how it happens, what it looks like in real sensor data, and how to prevent it.

Sampling rate is not a performance setting. It is a contract with your signal about what you can and cannot faithfully represent. Choose it based on the physics of what you are measuring, not the speed of your main loop.
