What is a Signal?

Before filters, before frequency, before any of it — what are we actually working with?

Start with something you already know

Forget DSP for a moment. You walk into a room and it feels warm. You come back an hour later and it feels warmer. You come back at night and it has cooled down. Without realizing it, you have just been experiencing a signal. The temperature of that room has a value right now, it had a different value an hour ago, and it will have a different value tomorrow morning.

That is all a signal is. A physical quantity that has a value, and that value changes over time.

Sound is air pressure doing the same thing, except instead of changing over hours it changes hundreds or thousands of times per second. The voltage across a resistor in a motor circuit rises and falls as the motor works harder or easier. The tiny silicon structure inside an accelerometer bends by different amounts as your board moves, producing a changing voltage. Every one of these is a signal. Any quantity you can measure, that has a value at each moment in time, qualifies.

DSP is entirely about operating on these streams of changing values. Before you can filter something, analyze it, or extract information from it, you have to understand what kind of thing you are actually working with.

The world is smooth

Physical quantities in the real world do not jump. Temperature does not leap from 22 degrees to 23 degrees in an instant with nothing in between. It passes through every possible value along the way, continuously, at every single moment in time. The same is true for sound pressure, for voltage, for acceleration. The physical world is smooth.

We call this kind of signal an analog signal. The word analog here means that the signal is directly analogous to the physical thing it represents. It has the same continuous, smooth character. A microphone produces an analog voltage that rises and falls exactly as air pressure rises and falls. A thermocouple produces an analog voltage that follows temperature with the same smoothness. If you were to draw an analog signal on paper, you would draw a smooth, unbroken curve — defined at every single instant, no gaps, no steps.

This is beautiful mathematically. It is also completely useless to your microcontroller, which is a digital machine that can only work with numbers at specific moments in time. It cannot hold a smoothly varying voltage in its processor and compute on it. It needs something else entirely.

What your microcontroller actually sees

Your microcontroller has a component called an ADC — an Analog to Digital Converter. At a specific moment in time it takes a snapshot of whatever voltage is at its input pin and converts that into a binary number. Then it waits a fixed amount of time. Then it takes another snapshot. Then another, at a completely regular rhythm.

The result is no longer a smooth curve. It is a list of numbers. Each number represents the voltage at one particular moment. On an Arduino with a 10-bit ADC reading a 0 to 5 volt signal, each number is somewhere between 0 and 1023. On an STM32 with a 12-bit ADC it is between 0 and 4095. These numbers arrive at a fixed rate, one after another, like a heartbeat. That list of numbers is a digital signal, and this is what every single DSP operation you will ever write actually operates on.

The rate at which those snapshots are taken is called the sampling rate, written Fs, measured in Hz — which just means times per second. If your ADC takes 1000 snapshots per second, Fs is 1000 Hz. If it takes 44100 snapshots per second like CD audio, Fs is 44100 Hz. This one number is one of the most consequential design decisions in any DSP system, and we will keep returning to it throughout this entire series.

Two things get lost in translation

When you go from the smooth analog world to a list of numbers, something is lost. Actually two things are lost, in two different ways.

The first is that you no longer have the signal at every instant in time. You only have it at the moments you sampled. What happened between two samples is completely unknown to you — it is gone. If something interesting happened in that gap, your system will never know about it. This is called time discretization, and it is the reason sampling rate matters so much. The faster you sample, the smaller those gaps are, and the less you miss.

The second is that each snapshot gets rounded. Your ADC cannot represent every possible voltage exactly. A 10-bit ADC divides its voltage range into 1024 equally spaced steps. Whatever the actual voltage is, it gets rounded to the nearest step. A 12-bit ADC gives you 4096 steps so the rounding error is smaller, but it is never zero. This rounding introduces a tiny error into every single sample. It is called quantization noise, and it is as real as any other noise source in your system.

A digital signal is an approximation of the original analog signal — approximated in time because you only have snapshots, and approximated in amplitude because each snapshot is rounded.

How sampling rate connects to the signal itself

The sampling rate is not an arbitrary setting you pick from a datasheet. It is a response to the physical nature of the signal you are trying to capture. A temperature in a room changes lazily, over seconds and minutes. Audio changes violently, hundreds to thousands of times per second. Your sampling rate has to keep up with how fast the signal is actually moving.

More precisely — and this distinction will matter a great deal later — it is not about how fast the signal changes in some general sense. It is about the highest frequency present in the signal. Audio contains frequency components up to about 20,000 Hz. Room temperature changes contain frequency components only up to a fraction of a hertz. Your sampling rate needs to be high enough to capture the highest frequency in the signal, not just fast enough to follow its general shape.

Similarly, how many bits your ADC has is not a fixed specification to accept blindly. It is a judgment about the signal. If you are measuring rough room temperature for a thermostat, 1024 steps across your voltage range is probably more than enough. If you are trying to detect a tiny strain gauge deflection riding on top of a large DC offset, every extra bit of resolution is precious. The bit depth question is always: how small is the smallest change in this signal that I actually care about?

The art of embedded DSP involves understanding and managing both approximations — in time and in amplitude — so that they do not corrupt the information you are trying to extract.

Why any of this matters before touching a filter

Sensors lie. Not maliciously, but the raw numbers coming out of an ADC are almost never what you want directly. They contain noise from power supplies, interference from nearby switching circuits, mechanical vibrations you are not trying to measure, DC offsets from component tolerances, and frequency content that your algorithm does not care about. DSP is the toolkit for cleaning all of that up and extracting the signal you actually want.

But you cannot clean up something you do not understand. Every filter design decision, every FFT parameter, every fixed-point scaling choice — all of it comes back to properties of the signal: how fast it changes, what range it occupies, how much precision it contains, how it was sampled. This first topic is not an introduction. It is the ground everything else is built on.
