Why One Number Per Word Isn't Enough

Trying to figure out, from scratch, why a language model represents a single word with hundreds of numbers.

Okay, so I want to talk about something that confused me for a long time.

If you load up a language model like GPT-2 and pull out its internal representation of the word "king", what you get back is 768 numbers. A list of floats. Not a definition. Not a category. Just numbers.

768 of them.

That always felt like a lot to me. Why 768? Why not 10? Why not a million? And what could those individual numbers possibly mean?

None of that makes sense yet. So we're going to start by trying simpler things and watching them fail. Where they fail will tell us what we actually need.

Try the dumbest thing first

What's the simplest possible way to represent a word as a number? Just give every word a number. Open a dictionary, count from one to whatever. "Aardvark" is 1. "Apple" is somewhere around 4,000. "King" lands near 47,000. "Queen" further along, maybe 71,000. One word, one integer, done.

This sounds fine for about ten seconds. Then you try to use it.

Pick a pair. See what the number line decides about them.

Try "king · kingdom" first, then "king · queen". The line says king is closer to kingdom than to queen. That gap is alphabetical, not semantic. Two words that share their first three letters always end up next to each other, whether their meanings have anything to do with each other or not.

The line is making things up

Here's what's actually going wrong. A number line isn't a neutral container. It has structure. Distance on a line means something. Order means something. Subtraction means something. That's what a number line is.

So when we put "king" at position 47,193 and "kingdom" at 47,194, the line is implicitly saying: these two are almost the same thing. It's saying that whether we want it to or not. The geometry comes for free with the line, and we don't get to opt out.

And the geometry it gives us is wrong. It reflects whatever we sorted by, which in this case is the alphabet. If we sorted by frequency instead, common words would become neighbours regardless of meaning. There's no sort order that maps onto semantics, because semantics doesn't fit on a one-dimensional line in the first place.

So our problem isn't that the numbers are arbitrary. It's that they're confidently wrong. The encoding has opinions it can't back up.

A number line forces a geometry. If your data doesn't fit that geometry, the line lies.

Okay, what if we refuse to claim anything?

If the problem is that the line invents relationships, the natural fix is to use an encoding that refuses to invent them. Don't put words in any order. Don't claim any word is closer to any other word. Just keep them all separate.

This has a name. It's called one-hot encoding. Instead of giving "king" the number 47,193, we give it a vector with one slot for every word in the vocabulary. Slot 47,193 gets set to 1. Every other slot stays at 0. "Queen" gets a different vector with slot 71,204 set to 1, zeros everywhere else. Each word lights up its own slot and stays out of everyone else's way.

It's the honest version. No hidden claims about which words are similar. Pick any two words, see what the encoding says about them.

One-hot is honest. It also says nothing.

This is the opposite failure. The number line was loud and wrong. One-hot is quiet and useless. Compute the distance between any two distinct one-hot vectors and you always get √2. King is at distance √2 from queen. Also from pizza. Also from carburetor. There's no notion of similarity at all.

We went from a representation that confidently makes up relationships to one that refuses to acknowledge any. Both are broken. And there's a side problem worth flagging.

cost-check To represent a 50,000-word vocabulary with one-hot, every word becomes a vector of 50,000 numbers, of which 49,999 are zero. We just blew up our memory by a factor of 50,000 and the new representation tells us less than the old one did. From a deployment angle this is the worst of both worlds. Huge memory footprint, no useful structure.

So we've tried both extremes. The number line says too much that's wrong. One-hot says nothing at all. What now?

What if we use more than one number?

Both failures share something. They're stuck with one axis of variation. The number line has order along one direction. One-hot has 50,000 slots but the slots themselves don't relate to each other. In both cases we've squashed the data into a structure that can't express what we need.

So what if we used more than one number per word? Two numbers. Coordinates on a plane. Now "king" can sit at position (3.2, 1.7) and "queen" at (3.5, 1.8) right next to it. "Carburetor" goes way over at (-4.1, -3.0). Two numbers gives us two axes to play with, and that's qualitatively different from one.

That helps. Just not enough. Let's see why.

Switch between 1, 2, and 3 dimensions. Watch the squeeze.

In 1D, every word fights for the same line. In 2D, you can group "king" with "queen" but try placing "running" near both "marathon" and "campaign" while keeping those two apart. The plane runs out of room. Add a third axis and the squeeze loosens. Every dimension you add is another direction meaning can spread out in.

Two dimensions still aren't enough

The plane breaks for a specific reason and it's worth pinning down. Take a word like "running". It's related to a lot of other words. "Run" obviously, but also "marathon", "exercise", "fast", "campaign" (as in running for office), "managing" (running a company), "flowing" (water running). All of those should sit near "running" in some sense. But they shouldn't all sit near each other. "Marathon" and "campaign" share almost nothing except their relationship to "running".

On a plane, you can't pull this off. Wherever you put "running", you have two choices. Put both "marathon" and "campaign" near it, in which case they end up near each other (which is wrong). Or put one of them far from "running" (also wrong). The plane doesn't have enough independent directions to encode all the different ways "running" relates to other words.

Three dimensions help. You can separate "marathon" and "campaign" along the new axis while still keeping both near "running". Push further into a real vocabulary though, and you hit the same wall again. There are more independent kinds of similarity than three axes can hold. Tense. Formality. Domain. Sentiment. Word class. Style. Each one wants its own direction to live in.

Every dimension we add is a new direction meaning can spread out in. Eventually we reach a number where the vocabulary has enough room. For modern language models, that number is in the hundreds to thousands. GPT-2 small uses 768. LLaMA-7B uses 4,096. GPT-3 uses 12,288. Bigger models use more dimensions because they're trying to capture more independent kinds of similarity at once.

Each new dimension is another direction meaning can live in.

So what is a vector, really?

Here's where the word "vector" starts to earn its keep. Up to this point we've been saying "many numbers per word" without giving the thing a name. A vector is just that. A list of numbers we treat as a single coordinated object. A point in some many-dimensional space.

The shift from "list of 768 numbers" to "point in 768-dimensional space" is small but it changes everything we can do. As long as we treat [0.04, -0.18, 0.23, ...] as 768 separate values, we can only do separate-value things to them. Once we treat them as a single position, a whole new set of operations opens up. We can ask about distance. About direction. About what's between two positions. About what arithmetic on positions might mean.

This is what's happening inside an LLM. Every word gets mapped to a vector. The vectors live in a shared space. Geometric relationships between vectors carry semantic meaning. Similar words sit near each other, related words point in related directions. The model spends most of its compute manipulating these vectors. The intelligence of the model, such as it is, lives in the geometry of where every word sits relative to every other word.

We haven't shown how that geometry actually gets built, or why it works. That's Part 2. But we've earned the demand. Words need many numbers because:

  • One number on a line lies about which words are related
  • One-hot encoding has no concept of relatedness at all
  • Two or three dimensions don't have enough room for language
  • Many dimensions give us room without the lying

The 768 numbers for "king" stop feeling like a lot once you see what the alternatives cost.

One last thought before we wrap

We've talked about vectors as abstract mathematical objects throughout this. But every one of those numbers eventually has to live somewhere physical, and that's where things get interesting from an engineering angle.

cost-check A 768-dimensional FP32 vector is 3,072 bytes. GPT-2's full embedding table (50,257 words times 768 dimensions times 4 bytes) is around 154 MB. That's just the lookup table, before any transformer layers exist. Doubling the dimension doubles the memory of every embedding, doubles the bandwidth needed to load them, and (because most operations on vectors are matrix multiplications) more than doubles the compute. We come back to this in Part 5, where we look at what a vector actually is on real silicon.

For now, just notice that "many dimensions" isn't a free choice. It's a tradeoff between representational quality and what you can actually run. Almost every choice in this series is going to have one of those tradeoffs lurking underneath it.

Quick check

Three short questions. Answer in your head, then click to check.

1. What's actually wrong with putting words on a number line?
The line forces a geometry the data doesn't have. Distance and order on a line carry meaning by construction. So when we put "king" at 47,193 and "kingdom" at 47,194, the line is implicitly claiming these two words are nearly identical. That claim comes from the alphabet, not from the words themselves. The encoding has opinions about meaning, and those opinions are based on spelling.
2. One-hot doesn't lie. So what's wrong with it?
It doesn't say anything either. Every pair of distinct one-hot vectors sits at distance √2. King is exactly as far from queen as it is from carburetor. There's no concept of similarity at all. We went from saying too much that was wrong to saying nothing at all, and we paid for the privilege. Every word now needs a vector with thousands of slots, almost all zeros.
3. Why isn't a 2D plane enough room for words?
Language has more independent kinds of similarity than two axes can hold. Try placing "running" near both "marathon" and "campaign" while keeping those two apart. You can't, because in a plane every position forces a tradeoff. Tense, formality, domain, sentiment, word class. Each is its own kind of relationship and wants its own direction. Two axes can encode two of these at most. That's why real models use hundreds or thousands of dimensions.

What's coming next

We've answered the first piece of the puzzle. Words need many numbers because the simpler encodings either lie or stay silent, and a few dimensions can't capture how language actually works. Vectors aren't excessive. They're the smallest honest answer.

What we haven't shown is how the geometry actually encodes meaning. We keep saying "similar words should sit close together" without saying what "close" means when you have 768 numbers, or how that arrangement gets built in the first place. That's where Part 2 picks up. We have many numbers per word. Now we figure out how those numbers do real work.
