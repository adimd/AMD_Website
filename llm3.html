Architecting Intelligence: Building LLMs From First Principle

Part 3 — Meaning From Neighbours: Co-Occurrence & Embeddings

How we move from “just words in a row” to vectors that quietly know dog and cat belong together.

Why meaning is really about behaviour

If you ask a philosopher where the meaning of a word lives, they might say something like:

“The meaning of dog lives inside the word dog itself.”

That’s a beautiful thought. It’s also completely useless if you’re a computer.

Machines don’t have childhood memories of chasing dogs or being barked at. They don’t have smell, or fur, or “aww cute” reactions. All they see is a sequence of symbols: "d", "o", "g".

The real secret is much more down-to-earth:

We understand words because of the situations they appear in and the neighbours they keep.

Every time you read or hear a sentence, your brain quietly updates an internal model:

  • “Dog” tends to appear near “pet”, “walk”, “bark”, “leash”.
  • “Cat” tends to appear near “pet”, “purr”, “climb”, “couch-ownership”.
  • “Quantum field theory” tends to appear nowhere near “cute Instagram story”.

The pattern of who appears next to whom slowly turns into your sense of meaning. Linguists gave this idea a name in the mid-20th century.

Distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

Zellig Harris hinted at it. J. R. Firth gave it the classic line: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps.” Modern embeddings, Word2Vec, GloVe, transformers — they’re all very fancy ways of operationalising that one sentence.

A tiny universe of six words

Let’s build a tiny language. Not English. Not Kannada. Just a six-word universe:

  • I love my dog
  • I love my cat
  • You love my dog
  • You love my cat

Vocabulary (with a fixed ordering so we can build matrices later):

  • 0 → I
  • 1 → You
  • 2 → love
  • 3 → my
  • 4 → dog
  • 5 → cat

Even without doing any math, your brain has already noticed a few things:

  • “Dog” and “cat” feel very similar.
  • “I” and “You” play similar roles at the start.
  • “love my” is basically welded together everywhere.

Now the challenge is: can we get a computer to notice this without us telling it explicitly “dog and cat are both pets”?

The only thing we’ll let the machine look at is neighbours. Nothing else.

Defining neighbours properly (window size = 1)

There are many possible definitions of “context”: same sentence, a window of 2–5 words, whole paragraph, etc. For now, we deliberately keep things very strict and clean:

Rule: two words are neighbours if they are adjacent in a sentence. This is a context window of size 1.

So in the sentence:

I love my dog

The adjacent pairs are:

  • (I, love)
  • (love, my)
  • (my, dog)

Here is one more important design choice:

For every adjacent pair \((a, b)\), we increment both directions: \((a, b)\) and \((b, a)\). That way, our co-occurrence matrix is symmetric.

We’re not yet interested in order (who comes first, who comes second). We just care that these two words bump into each other as neighbours.

Counting neighbours in each sentence

Sentence 1: I love my dog

  • (I, love), (love, I)
  • (love, my), (my, love)
  • (my, dog), (dog, my)

Sentence 2: I love my cat

  • (I, love), (love, I)
  • (love, my), (my, love)
  • (my, cat), (cat, my)

Sentence 3: You love my dog

  • (You, love), (love, You)
  • (love, my), (my, love)
  • (my, dog), (dog, my)

Sentence 4: You love my cat

  • (You, love), (love, You)
  • (love, my), (my, love)
  • (my, cat), (cat, my)

Aggregated neighbour counts

Now we add up how many times each unordered pair meets as neighbours:

  • I – love : 2 times
  • You – love : 2 times
  • love – my : 4 times
  • my – dog : 2 times
  • my – cat : 2 times

Every other pair (like “I – dog”, “You – cat”, “dog – cat”) never sit next to each other, so their co-occurrence count is 0.

Suggested image: a tiny graph of six nodes (“I”, “You”, “love”, “my”, “dog”, “cat”) with edges between neighbours, labelled with counts: 2 between I–love, 2 between You–love, 4 between love–my, 2 between my–dog, 2 between my–cat.
Neighbour graph

In this miniature universe, “dog” and “cat” have exactly the same neighbour (“my”), with the same strength.

Building the 6×6 co-occurrence matrix

Now we turn those counts into a proper matrix. We fix the vocabulary order as:

\[ V = [\text{I},\ \text{You},\ \text{love},\ \text{my},\ \text{dog},\ \text{cat}] \]

Then we build a matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 6}\) where:

  • \(M[i, j]\) = number of times word \(i\) appears next to word \(j\) (with window size 1),
  • and we’ve made it symmetric by adding counts in both directions.
I You love my dog cat
I 0 0 2 0 0 0
You 0 0 2 0 0 0
love 2 2 0 4 0 0
my 0 0 4 0 2 2
dog 0 0 0 2 0 0
cat 0 0 0 2 0 0

A few nice observations immediately fall out of this matrix:

  • The rows for dog and cat are exactly identical: [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0].
  • The rows for I and You are also identical: [0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0].
  • love and my sit in the middle, linking subjects to pets.
Without any dictionary, annotations, or labels, the matrix has quietly learned that “dog” and “cat” behave the same way in this little world.

And notice: we got this just from one-step neighbours. No big window. No sentence-level context. Just adjacency.

Suggested image: a heatmap of the 6×6 co-occurrence matrix, where the “dog” and “cat” rows look identical, and “love–my” is the brightest connection.
Co-occurrence heatmap

A simple matrix, but it already captures that dog and cat live in the same neighbourhood.

Seeing meaning as geometry

Each row of this matrix is now a vector. That means each word has a little “address” in a 6-dimensional space:

  • dog → [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0]
  • cat → [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0]
  • love → [2, 2, 0, 4, 0, 0]
  • my → [0, 0, 4, 0, 2, 2]

If we plotted this in some intelligently flattened 2D picture, “dog” and “cat” would sit on top of each other. “I” and “You” would sit together somewhere else. “love” and “my” would sit in the busy crossroads of this universe.

This is the first glimpse of what we’ll eventually call an embedding space:

Similar words have similar vectors. Similar vectors live near each other in geometry.

Our current space is tiny and clumsy — 6 dimensions isn’t much, and the data is toy-sized. But the pattern is already visible.

There is, however, a huge problem waiting when we scale this up to real language.

Why co-occurrence matrices don’t scale

In real NLP, our vocabulary \(V\) is not 6 words. It might be:

  • \(V = 50{,}000\) (a modest model), or
  • \(V = 200{,}000\) (if you’re being generous with rare words).

Our co-occurrence matrix \(M \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times V}\) suddenly looks like:

\[ 50{,}000 \times 50{,}000 = 2.5 \text{ billion cells}. \]

That’s not a cute table anymore. That’s the Excel sheet that makes your laptop cry.

And the issues don’t stop there:

  • It’s huge. Even storing it densely is expensive.
  • It’s sparse. Most word pairs never co-occur, so most entries are zero.
  • It’s noisy. Rare words and weird contexts create fragile statistics.
  • It’s awkward to use. Each word’s vector is 50,000-dimensional. Computing similarities is slow.

So the good news is: co-occurrence captures something real about meaning. The bad news is: it’s like keeping your entire life’s information in raw CCTV footage. All the meaning is in there somewhere, but it’s not convenient.

Suggested image: a massive, mostly-empty matrix labelled “V × V co-occurrence matrix”, with a few tiny non-zero dots to show sparsity.
The matrix that ate your RAM

Co-occurrence matrices are information-rich but also large, sparse, and painful to store.

From sparse counts to dense embeddings

The natural next question is:

Do we really need all 50,000 numbers in each word’s vector?

Intuitively, no. We only need enough dimensions to capture:

  • which kinds of contexts this word tends to live in, and
  • how that compares to other words.

Instead of a giant, sparse vector like:

\[ \text{dog}_{\text{sparse}} = [0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, \dots, 0] \in \mathbb{R}^{V}, \]

we’d like a much smaller, dense vector, say:

\[ \text{dog}_{\text{embed}} = [0.72,\; -0.11,\; 0.83,\; 0.21,\; \dots] \in \mathbb{R}^{d}, \]

where \(d\) is maybe 50, 100, or 300 — much smaller than \(V\).

These smaller, dense vectors are what we call word embeddings.

They are still trying to encode “the company a word keeps”, but in a compressed, learnable format.

Suggested image: Left: a tall, mostly-zero sparse vector labelled “Co-occurrence row for ‘dog’”. Right: a short, dense vector labelled “Embedding for ‘dog’”.
Compression of meaning

We trade an unwieldy, sparse fingerprint for a compact, dense one that still preserves similarity relationships.

From a matrix perspective, you can imagine we’re trying to approximate our huge co-occurrence matrix \(M\) with something like:

\[ M \approx W C^{\top}, \]

where:

  • \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) — one \(d\)-dimensional vector per word (as “center”),
  • \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d}\) — one \(d\)-dimensional vector per word (as “context”),
  • and \(d \ll V\).

In practice, we don’t actually build \(M\) and then factor it. That would still be huge. Instead, modern methods like Word2Vec learn the matrices \(W\) and \(C\) directly by asking the model to predict neighbours.

Co-occurrence gave us the idea of “meaning as a vector”. Embeddings make that idea computationally tractable.

Where this is going next

In the first part of this series, we saw how text turns into raw numbers (ASCII, Unicode, UTF-8) with zero meaning. In the second, we saw how geometry starts to create meaning for images and scores.

In this part, we’ve made a crucial move for language:

  • We treated words as nodes in a graph of neighbours.
  • We built a co-occurrence matrix using only one-step adjacency.
  • We saw that “dog” and “cat” naturally acquire identical vectors.
  • We realised that this approach does not scale, but points directly towards embeddings.
The big conceptual shift: Meaning = patterns of usage, compressed into vectors.

What we haven’t done yet is show you how to learn those vectors from real text.

That’s where we go next:

  • Turn “who are my neighbours?” into a prediction game.
  • Use simple neural models to guess a word’s neighbours (or guess the word from its neighbours).
  • Let gradients slowly sculpt the embedding space.

Once we’re comfortable with embeddings and prediction, we’ll finally be ready to talk about tokens, attention, and why transformers feel so natural once all these building blocks are in place.
