Probability as a Computational Object

Naming What Happens

Outcomes, events, sample space, and the modelling choice hiding in every if statement.

The boring class, and why we are doing it anyway

Last class I was a bit sloppy with my language. I kept saying things like "the motor is failing" and "given what I just measured." I used those phrases as if they all meant the same kind of thing. They do not. There are at least three different objects mixed up inside, and the rest of this series needs them kept separate.

So today is the vocabulary class. Most probability courses skip this in five minutes or breeze through it on page two, and then six weeks later students cannot tell you why their math is producing weird answers. We are going to spend a full post on the words, because every confusion in the next few classes traces back to this stuff being shaky.

Three words: outcome, event, sample space. But before I can name them cleanly, I have to back up and explain what kind of thing we are even putting names on.

What "random experiment" actually means

The phrase probability people use is "random experiment." Most courses treat that phrase as if it explains itself. It does not. What is "random" about it? What is an "experiment"? If I do not answer that first, the rest is fog.

Forget the word random for a second. Start with experiment.

An experiment, in the way we are about to use the word, is just something you do that produces a result. Flipping a coin. Reading an ADC. Asking someone their height. Running your motor classifier on a vibration window. All experiments. You do the thing, and a result comes out.

Now the harder word.

A process is random when, even with complete care in setting up the experiment, you cannot predict with certainty which result will come out.

Notice what that does and does not say. It does not say "random means uncaused." Coin flips are not uncaused. If you knew the exact force, air currents, and surface elasticity, you could in principle calculate which side it lands on. The coin obeys physics. Nothing magical happens during the flip.

It does not say "random means uniform." A weighted coin that lands heads 90% of the time is just as random as a fair one. It just has different probabilities attached.

What it says is: even though you set up the experiment as carefully as you can, the result is not fully determined by what you can see and control. There are factors that influence the result that are either too small to track, too numerous to track, or genuinely hidden from you. Those factors take what would have been a deterministic process and turn it into one whose result you can only describe with probabilities.

The coin flip is random because the outcome depends on micro-details of force, angle, air, and table that you do not measure. The ADC reading is random because thermal noise inside the silicon perturbs the value at the bit level. The motor classifier is random across motors because each motor has manufacturing variance, load history, and wear that you cannot observe from the vibration alone.

Randomness is about what you can know and predict, not about what is or is not caused.

This is the same lesson as last class, restated. Class 1 said probability lives in your description of the world, not in the world itself. Random experiments are the formal version of that. You set up the experiment, you cannot see all the causes, so you describe the result with probabilities. The randomness lives in the gap between what causes the outcome and what you can observe.

The three objects

Now we can be precise. A random experiment is a process where you can list every result that is possible to come out, and you cannot say in advance which of those possible results will come out on a particular run. Once you accept that, three vocabulary words fall out naturally.

Outcome. An outcome is one specific result that happened on one specific run of the experiment. You flipped the coin once. It came up heads. "Heads" is the outcome of that run. An outcome is atomic. It is the smallest indivisible "thing that came out." You cannot break "heads" into smaller pieces. Each time you run the experiment, exactly one outcome happens. Not zero. Not two. One.

Sample space. The sample space is the set of all outcomes the experiment could have produced. For the coin: {heads, tails}. Two elements. That is the entire universe of possibilities for this experiment. People sometimes write the sample space with a capital omega, like a horseshoe: Ω = {heads, tails}. You do not need the symbol. You do need the idea. The sample space is a catalogue. Before the experiment, the result will land somewhere in the catalogue. After, you know exactly which entry.

Event. An event is a group of outcomes you collected together because they share a property you care about. For the coin this is almost trivial: "the coin landed heads" is an event containing one outcome. Events get interesting when the sample space is bigger.

Roll a die. The sample space is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Six outcomes. Now I ask: what is the probability that the die came up even? "Even" is not in the sample space. The die never lands on the result "even." It lands on 2, 4, or 6. "Even" is a group of outcomes that share a property, and that group is an event. The event "even" contains three outcomes: {2, 4, 6}.

Outcomes are the language of what actually happened. Events are the language of the questions you actually ask.

You almost never care about exactly which atomic outcome happened. You care about categories. Did the die roll even. Did the temperature exceed 30 degrees. Did the motor classifier say "failing." Events are how you ask those questions. Every event is a subset of the sample space. The sample space itself is an event (the trivially true event "anything happened"). The empty set is an event (the trivially false event "nothing happened"). Most events you care about live somewhere in between.

The trap that catches almost everyone

Now I want to show you the place where almost every beginner trips, because I tripped on it, and the people I have taught this to tripped on it, and you will probably trip on it too. It is sneaky because the wrong answer feels natural.

Roll two six-sided dice. Look at the sum of the values on top. What is the sample space?

The natural answer is "the sum can be 2, 3, 4, all the way up to 12, so the sample space is {2, 3, 4, ..., 12}." That is wrong, and the way it is wrong is the most important thing to understand in this class.

The random experiment is rolling two dice. What does the experiment physically produce? Two faces. One on each die. The atomic thing that happens is a pair: (face on die 1, face on die 2). The dice do not produce sums. They produce pairs. You compute the sum afterwards.

So the real sample space is the 36 ordered pairs:

Ω = { (1,1), (1,2), (1,3), ..., (6,5), (6,6) }

Why does this matter? Because if you build your probability on {2, 3, ..., 12} and assume each is equally likely, you get nonsense. There is one pair that gives sum 2: (1,1). There are six pairs that give sum 7: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). Pairs are equally likely. Sums are not. Pick a target sum below and watch which pairs match it.

The sums are not equally likely, even though the underlying pairs are. If you had built your sample space as the eleven possible sums and treated them as equally likely, every probability you computed afterwards would have been wrong.

The takeaway is one line.

The sample space is built from what the experiment physically produces, not from what you care about computing from the result.

A useful test: ask yourself, what is the smallest amount of information about one run of the experiment that tells me everything that happened? For two dice, the pair of face values tells you everything. The sum does not, because many pairs collapse to the same sum. The smallest complete description of one run is the outcome. The sample space is the set of all such complete descriptions.

This is going to matter a lot in the next class, where we introduce the random variable. The random variable is going to be exactly the function that takes a pair like (3,4) and produces a number like 7. If you understand that the dice produce pairs and the sum is something computed on top, the random variable is going to feel natural. If you keep mushing them together, the random variable will feel arbitrary.

The bigger trap: framing is a choice

There is a second confusion that runs deeper than the dice one, and it is the lesson I most want you to leave with today.

I have been talking about "the random experiment" as if it were an objective thing. Pick up two dice, that is your experiment, here is your sample space. But that framing is hiding a choice. The same physical act can be many different random experiments depending on what question you are asking.

Here is the example I always use. Imagine you walk into a coffee shop, order a coffee, and stick a temperature probe in. It reads 23.4 °C. That is one physical act, one reading.

Now I am going to ask you two different questions about that act, and your job is to notice that they are different questions with different sample spaces.

Same reading. Different question. Different sample space.

Toggle between the two framings. The physical act is identical. What changes is which question you decided to ask, and that single choice rewrites the sample space, the source of randomness, and what counts as "the answer." Neither framing is wrong. They answer different engineering questions, and a lot of confusion in deployed ML comes from people mixing them without realising it.

Look at what happened. Same probe. Same reading. Same physical event. But the question changed, and changing the question changed everything downstream. The sample space, the source of randomness, what counts as the answer. None of these came from the device. They came from how you decided to describe what was happening.

"Random experiment" is not a property of the world. It is a property of the model you choose to put on the world.

This is the same lesson as Class 1, restated in the new vocabulary. Class 1 said probability lives in your description, not in the world. Class 2 now adds: the very choice of what counts as a random experiment is part of that description. Two engineers reading the same sensor can disagree about what the sample space is, and both can be right, because they are answering different questions.

The engineer instinct is Framing A. There is a real thing in the world, the sensor is trying to measure it, the noise is a nuisance. This is the framing of instrumentation, calibration, signal processing. It is the framing most embedded engineers default to, because that is what their training emphasises.

The ML instinct is Framing B. The data is the variability. The distribution across the fleet is the thing being modelled. The randomness is the signal, not the bug. This is the framing of statistics, ML training, deployment monitoring.

The skill we are building is being able to switch between framings consciously, depending on what question you are trying to answer. A lot of deployed ML failures come from people building a model assuming Framing A (there is a true label) and then running it on data that was implicitly Framing B (the labels themselves vary in messy ways). The model breaks in surprising ways, and the reason it breaks is buried in this framing choice that nobody made explicit.

Applied to embedded

Let me make this concrete on hardware you know.

You have a 12-bit ADC reading a thermistor. One conversion. The sample space, if we take Framing A and treat the experiment as "one conversion of this specific input," is the set of possible 12-bit codes the converter can produce. That is 4096 elements, 0 through 4095. What makes this random is thermal noise, supply variation, all the micro-causes you cannot track. You read the same input twice and get slightly different codes.

Any subset of that sample space is a valid event. The events you actually care about are the ones that mean something:

  • "The reading is above 3000" is the event {3001, 3002, ..., 4095}. 1095 outcomes grouped together.
  • "The reading is in the noise floor" might be {0, 1, ..., 15}. 16 outcomes.
  • "The reading is even" is the event {0, 2, 4, ..., 4094}. 2048 outcomes.

Now look at your firmware. Every time you wrote if (adc_reading > THRESHOLD), you were testing whether an event occurred. The variable held an outcome. The comparison defined an event. The if body ran when the outcome landed inside that event. You have been working with random experiments, sample spaces, outcomes, and events your entire engineering career. Nobody told you the words.

But here is the catch, and this is where the framing lesson bites again. From the firmware's point of view at the instant the if runs, the variable holds a known value. There is no uncertainty about what is in memory. The check is deterministic. The randomness only exists if you zoom out to "across all runs of this firmware, what fraction of the time does this branch take the alarm path?" Then it is a probability question. Zoom in to one specific run, it is just arithmetic.

Same code. Two framings. Whether it is a random experiment depends on which lens you put on it.

Two events together: partitions

One last concept to plant, because we will use it later.

When you have two or more events, two questions about them matter often enough to have names.

Two events are mutually exclusive if they share no outcomes. If one happens, the other did not. Their intersection is empty. "Die rolled 2" and "die rolled 5" are mutually exclusive. They cannot both be true on the same roll.

Two events are collectively exhaustive if together they cover the entire sample space. There is no outcome that lies outside both of them. "Die rolled even" and "die rolled odd" are collectively exhaustive. Every die roll falls into one or the other.

These are different properties, and you can have one without the other. {2} and {5} are mutually exclusive but not collectively exhaustive (1, 3, 4, 6 are missing). {1,2,3,4} and {4,5,6} are collectively exhaustive but not mutually exclusive (they overlap on 4).

When a set of events has both properties together, we say they form a partition of the sample space. The sample space has been split into clean non-overlapping pieces that cover everything.

Try the classic two-event partition: put healthy in A and put degrading and failing in B. That is a partition: the motor is either fine, or it needs attention, and nothing else. Now try putting degrading in both A and B. The exclusivity check fails. Try putting degrading in neither. The exhaustiveness check fails. Partitions are picky.

Partitions are the structure that makes a lot of later math work. When you want to compute a probability by breaking it down across cases, the cases need to form a partition. If they overlap, you double-count. If they miss something, you under-count. We will use this when we get to Bayes' rule, but the seed is being planted now.

Good engineering classifications are often partitions on purpose. When you split motors into {healthy, degrading, failing}, you are committing to the claim that every motor falls into exactly one of these categories. That is a partition by design. The moment your classification is not a partition (overlapping classes, missing classes), your downstream probabilities get weird, and the math starts producing results that do not sum to one.

What you should leave with

Four things.

First, what makes an experiment random. It is not magic. It is not lack of cause. It is not uniformity. It is that the experiment is set up as carefully as you can manage, and the result still depends on factors you cannot track. Randomness lives in your description, given what you can and cannot observe.

Second, the three objects. An outcome is one specific atomic result of one run of the experiment. The sample space is the set of all outcomes the experiment could produce. An event is a subset of the sample space, grouping outcomes by a property you care about.

Third, the trap. The sample space is built from what the experiment physically produces, not from what you compute from it. The dice produce pairs, not sums. The smallest complete description of one run is the outcome. Get this wrong and every probability downstream is wrong.

Fourth, the deeper move. "Random experiment" is a property of the model you choose, not of the world. The same physical act can be many different experiments with different sample spaces depending on which question you are asking. The skill is being able to pick the framing consciously, and being honest about which framing you are using.

Next class, we put a function on top of the sample space. The function takes outcomes (like the pair (3,4)) and produces numbers (like the sum 7). That function is the random variable, and it is the object that finally lets us do arithmetic on probabilities.

Check yourself

Five questions. Pick the option you think is right. If you got it, that option turns green. If you missed, the wrong option turns red and the right one lights up too. An explanation appears underneath either way. These are not testing recall. They are testing whether the ideas have actually settled.

Question 1
In your own words, what is the core property that makes something a random experiment?
Why Right. Two parts have to be true together. You know the full set of possible outcomes, and you cannot predict which one will come out on this run, because the relevant causes are too small, too numerous, or too hidden for you to track. Randomness is a feature of your description given limited information, not a feature of the world being uncaused.
Why Random does not mean uncaused. The coin obeys physics. Random does not mean uniform either: a weighted coin is just as random as a fair one. The core idea is that you can list the possible outcomes and you cannot predict the specific one in advance, because the relevant causes are out of your reach. The randomness is in your description, not in the world being magical.
Question 2
You roll two six-sided dice and look at the sum. What is the sample space of this experiment?
Why Yes. The dice physically produce pairs of faces. The sum is something you compute from the pair afterwards. The sample space is built from what the experiment physically produces, not from what you derive from it. There are 36 pairs, not 11 sums, and that matters because the pairs are equally likely while the sums are not.
Why This is the most common trap in beginner probability. The dice do not produce sums. They produce pairs of faces. You compute the sum afterwards. The sample space is whatever the experiment physically produces, which is 36 ordered pairs. If you treat the 11 sums as equally likely, you will get wrong probabilities for everything downstream: there is one pair that gives sum 2, but six pairs that give sum 7. The sum is a function applied to the outcome, not the outcome itself.
Question 3
A colleague says "every if statement on a sensor value is testing whether an event occurred." Is this fully correct?
Why Exactly the right kind of pushback. At the instant the if runs, the variable holds a known value. The check is deterministic. Randomness only enters if you zoom out to "across all runs of this firmware, what does the distribution of values look like?" That zoom-out is a modelling choice. "Random experiment" is a lens you put on top of the code, not a feature of the silicon. Both framings are valid; the trick is knowing which one you are using.
Why The honest answer involves the framing lesson. At the instant the if runs, the variable in memory holds one specific value. The check is deterministic. It only becomes an "event test" if you choose to model the variable as the result of a random experiment, like "across the distribution of readings my sensor produces over time." That choice is a lens, not a feature of the code. Whether something is a random experiment depends on the question you ask, not on the hardware.
Question 4
Your motor classifier produces one of three labels: healthy, degrading, failing. Consider these two events: A = {healthy, degrading} and B = {degrading, failing}. Which is true?
Why Right. A and B together cover every outcome (their union is the full sample space), so they are collectively exhaustive. But they both contain degrading, so they overlap. They are not mutually exclusive. A partition needs both properties together: no overlap, and full coverage. Missing either disqualifies the set from being a partition.
Why Look at the outcomes carefully. Both A and B contain degrading. So they overlap, which means they are not mutually exclusive. But every outcome (healthy, degrading, failing) appears in at least one of them, so they are collectively exhaustive. The two properties are independent: you can have either one without the other, and both have to hold for the set to be a partition.
Question 5
You are reading a temperature probe in a coffee shop. The reading is 23.4 °C. Your colleague asks "is this a random experiment?" What is the right answer?
Why Yes. This is the deepest lesson in the class. "Random experiment" is a property of the model you choose, not of the physical act. The same probe reading is a noisy measurement of a fixed truth in one framing, and a sample from a population in the other. The mathematics, the sample space, and even what counts as "the answer" all change with the framing. The skill we are building is being able to pick the framing consciously.
Why Each of the other options forces a single framing onto a situation that actually permits two. The "noise versus resolution" answer is real, but it lives entirely inside the "measure this specific cup" framing; it does not get you to the deeper point that you could have asked a completely different question (about your fleet of probes) and gotten a completely different sample space. Random experiment is a lens, not a fact about the world. Two engineers can disagree about the sample space and both be right, if they are answering different questions.
