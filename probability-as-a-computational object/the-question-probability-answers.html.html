Probability as a Computational Object

The Question Probability Answers

Why the deterministic `if` statement runs out, and what we reach for when it does.

Two cats on a couch

My friend has two cats. Their names are Idli and Dosa. Idli is grey. Dosa is also grey. They are roughly the same size. From across the room, looking at one of them curled up on the couch, you genuinely cannot tell which cat you are looking at. They are both grey blobs.

And yet my friend can tell them apart. She has lived with them for three years. She will glance over and say "that's Dosa" without thinking about it. So I asked her once, how do you know? She paused, and she said: "I don't know. It's just that Dosa sits more on the left side of the couch. And her tail does a thing. And the way she's curled up right now, that's a Dosa shape. It's probably Dosa."

Probably Dosa. Not definitely Dosa.

There is no rule that says "if cat is on the left, then Dosa." Idli sits on the left sometimes too.

What my friend is doing is something computers used to be very bad at. She has a fuzzy collection of clues, none of which is decisive, and she weighs them against each other to land on "probably Dosa." Sometimes she is wrong. She will look closer and go "oh wait, that's Idli." But she is right most of the time, and the way she gets to right is not by following a rule. It is by combining evidence under uncertainty.

If you tried to write firmware to identify the cat from a camera, you would discover something uncomfortable very quickly. There is no threshold you can set. There is no if statement that works. The two cats overlap in every feature you can measure. The best your code can do is the same thing my friend does. Collect evidence, weigh it, and end up with "probably Dosa, but I'm not sure."

That "probably, but I'm not sure" is a probability. And learning how to compute one, store one, and ship one on a microcontroller is the whole game.

The engineering version

Let me give you the version of this you would actually meet at work.

You are building a thing that goes on a motor. The motor sometimes fails. You want to catch the failure before it actually fails, because when motors fail in the field, expensive things happen. Basements flood. Production lines stop. Customers get angry. So you stick an accelerometer on the motor, you record the vibration, and you try to figure out from the vibration whether the motor is healthy or about to die.

You sit down. You are an engineer. You do the engineer thing. You look at a bunch of vibration recordings from healthy motors and from failing motors, and you try to find the difference.

And there is a difference. Failing motors vibrate harder, on average. If you compute the RMS of the vibration over a two-second window, healthy motors land around 0.4 g and failing motors land around 0.9 g. So you write:

if (rms > 0.65f) classify_as_failing(); else classify_as_healthy();

You ship it. You catch most of the failures, but not all. And sometimes the alarm screams at a perfectly healthy motor. So you go back to the data and you plot the histograms on the same axes. The RMS distribution for healthy motors next to the RMS distribution for failing ones.

The two humps overlap. There is a region in the middle where healthy motors and failing motors are both showing up. Some healthy motors vibrate at 0.7. Some failing motors vibrate at 0.5. The vibration does not determine the state of the motor. It just leans one way or the other. And this is not a noise problem you can filter out. It is structural. Two physically different motor states produce overlapping vibration signatures, because vibration depends on a hundred things at once. Load, temperature, mounting torque, bearing wear, manufacturing variance. All of these collapse into the one number you read off the accelerometer. Once they have collapsed, you cannot un-collapse them.

Here is the thought experiment that finally made this click for me. Imagine I gave you a perfect, infinite-precision, zero-noise accelerometer. The problem does not go away. Two different motors, one healthy and one failing, can produce literally identical traces, because the vibration is a function of so many other things besides the motor's internal state. Better sensors do not fix this. More features do not fix this. The ambiguity is in the world, not in your equipment.

Once you see this, you start seeing it everywhere. Your phone trying to tell walking from running off the IMU is the same problem. Your watch flagging atrial fibrillation from heart rate is the same problem. A keyword spotter deciding whether you said "Alexa" or just made a noise that sounds a bit like it is the same problem. The measurement is consistent with multiple possible truths, and no clever threshold is going to fix that.

Here is the test I use to spot one of these problems in the wild. Is there a fact about the world I am trying to guess, that I would find out for certain if I had a better view? If yes, and the only thing standing between me and that fact is evidence I have to interpret, I am in probability country. If the question is really just arithmetic on inputs I already have, I am not.

Giving up on the rule

You give up on the rule. That is the first move.

You stop trying to write if measurement > threshold then state_A else state_B. You accept that no such rule exists for this kind of problem, and you reach for a different shape of answer.

The different shape looks like this. Instead of saying "this motor is failing," you say:

Given what I just measured, I am 73% confident the motor is failing, and 27% confident it is healthy.

That sentence is a distribution. It is a statement about how the evidence pulls. It does not commit. It carries both possibilities along, with weights.

And it does something else, which is the move everything in machine learning is built on. It separates two things that used to be glued together:

  • What I believe about the world, given the evidence. The distribution.
  • What I am going to do about it. The decision.

In your old firmware, those were one thing. The if statement was both the belief and the action, fused together. Now they are separate. The model produces a belief. The application logic decides what to do with that belief.

This sounds like a small thing. It is not a small thing. Watch what happens when you actually try to ship the motor classifier across two different customers.

Suppose your model says "70% chance this motor is failing." Should you ring the alarm? It depends on what an alarm costs and what missing a failure costs. If the motor is in an ICU and missing a failure kills someone, you ring at the slightest hint. If the motor is in a non-critical pump and false alarms cost the customer a service call they resent, you wait for much stronger evidence.

Same model. Same 70%. Completely different actions, set by something the model itself does not need to know about. A deterministic classifier cannot do this. The threshold is baked into the model. To change the alarm behaviour for one customer, you would have to retrain a different model. The probabilistic version lets one model serve every customer, with the application layer deciding when to act.

Why accuracy is not enough

The separation of belief and action also closes the most common objection to probabilistic models. The objection sounds like this. My classifier is right 95% of the time. I do not need confidence values, just give me the label.

The colleague who says this has a point, sort of. Accuracy is real. 95% is good. But there is one thing accuracy cannot tell you, and it is the thing that ends up mattering most in deployment.

Accuracy is an average. It tells you how often you are right across all your predictions. It does not tell you, for any specific prediction, whether this one is the easy 95% or the hard 5%.

Every individual prediction looks the same to your deterministic classifier. The textbook case and the borderline case come out as the same label, with the same implicit confidence of "this is the answer." You eat the 5% error on every prediction, because you cannot see which ones are the risky ones.

A probabilistic classifier hands you the borderline cases marked as borderline. It says 92% when it is confident and 56% when it is not. You can route the borderline cases to a slower model, a second sensor, a human reviewer. Your effective accuracy on the cases you actually act on goes way above 95%, because you have stopped acting on the cases you are unsure about.

The 95% number stops being a ceiling. It becomes a starting point.

What it costs

Now the price, because there is always a price.

A deterministic answer is one number. "Healthy" is one byte. Maybe a bit, if you are packing.

A probabilistic answer over two classes is two numbers. "73% failing, 27% healthy" is two floats, eight bytes. Over 1000 classes, like an image classifier picking one of a thousand objects, it is a thousand floats. Four kilobytes just to hold one answer.

On a laptop, who cares. On the ESP32-C3 with 400 KB of SRAM, where you are already squeezing model weights and sensor buffers and a network stack into that 400 KB, you care a lot. 4 KB is not free.

But here is the thing that surprised me when I first saw it. The "uncertainty costs more memory" story is not always true. It depends entirely on the shape you store the distribution in.

If the distribution follows a shape we have a formula for, storage is tiny and fixed. If it does not follow any nice shape, you have to write out probabilities value by value, and the cost grows with how many values you care about.

There are two families of representation. The first is tabular. You store one number per possible value. It is flexible, in that it can represent any shape you want, but it is expensive, because it grows linearly with the number of values, and for continuous things it does not work at all.

The second is parametric. You store the parameters of a formula that describes the whole distribution. A Gaussian over the entire real number line is two numbers: mean and standard deviation. Eight bytes. That is it. Cheap and fixed-cost, but only for shapes the formula allows.

So the right question to ask, every time someone hands you a probabilistic model, is not "is this expensive?" It is "what kind of distribution does it emit, and which family does it fit into?"

The image classifier case is the painful one: 1000 floats per prediction, no way to compress without losing the per-class probabilities you actually need. The temperature case is the surprising one: the parametric Gaussian is roughly the same size as the deterministic point estimate, so representing your uncertainty is almost free.

What you should leave with

I did not teach you any probability today. I am aware of that. I told you a story about two cats, and a story about a motor, and I tried to convince you that there is a class of problem where your normal tools do not work.

That is all I wanted from this post. If you leave thinking okay, I see why we need a new tool, the job is done. The actual tools start next time.

Four things to take with you.

First, there exists a kind of problem where measurement does not determine truth, no matter how clever you are. The test is: is there a hidden fact about the world I am trying to guess from evidence? If yes, you are in probability country. If you are just doing arithmetic on inputs you already have, you are not.

Second, probabilistic thinking separates belief from action. The model produces a distribution. The application logic decides what to do with it. That separation is what lets one model serve different customers with different risk tolerances, lets you abstain when you are unsure, lets you combine evidence from multiple sources, and lets you check whether your model is actually as confident as it claims.

Third, accuracy is an average. It hides which predictions are easy and which are hard. A probabilistic model hands you the per-prediction confidence, which is the information you need to do real engineering on top of the classifier.

Fourth, uncertainty has a hardware shadow. It costs memory, sometimes a little, sometimes a lot. The cost depends on which family of representation you pick. Tabular grows with the number of possible values. Parametric stays fixed. Choosing between them is itself an engineering decision, driven by what the distribution looks like and how much RAM you have.

Next time, we start building the vocabulary that lets us talk about all this precisely. Outcomes, events, sample space. The boring but necessary words that the rest of the course rests on. Take your time with today. Everything else is built on it.

Check yourself

Five questions. Read each one, pick what you think is the right answer, and the option you picked will turn green if you got it right, or red if you got it wrong (and the correct one will also light up green). An explanation appears underneath either way. These are not testing recall. They are testing whether the ideas have actually settled.

Question 1
You are building a wearable that flags when its user is having a stress episode, using heart-rate, skin-conductance and motion sensors. Is this a problem for probability, or just a clever if statement?
Why Right. The test is whether the measurement can be produced by multiple different hidden states. Exercise, stress, and excitement all produce elevated heart rate and skin conductance. Even with perfect zero-noise sensors, you cannot tell them apart from those signals alone. That is the structural ambiguity that makes this a probability problem, not the noise level.
Why The reason this is a probability problem is not the noise. It is that exercise, excitement and stress all produce elevated heart rate and skin conductance. Even with perfect zero-noise sensors, the same measurement is consistent with multiple hidden states. That is the structural ambiguity probability is built for.
Question 2
Your model says P(failing) = 70% for a particular motor. What is the right thing to do with that number?
Why Yes. The model's job is to produce an honest belief. The decision of whether to act on that belief depends on what each kind of mistake costs, and that is application logic, not model logic. Same belief, different stakes, different action.
Why The whole point of separating belief and action is that the right threshold depends on the cost of being wrong, which the model does not know about. An ICU pump rings at 10%, a warehouse fan waits until 85%. Same model, same 70%, different actions, because the stakes differ. A fixed threshold like 50% or 95% bakes in an assumption about cost that may be completely wrong for the deployment.
Question 3
A colleague says: "My classifier is 95% accurate. I do not need probabilities, just the label." What is the strongest single point you can make against this?
Why Exactly. Accuracy is an average across all your predictions. It hides per-prediction difficulty. Probabilities give you that information. Once you can spot the borderline cases at inference time, you can route them to a slower model, a second sensor, or a human, and your effective accuracy on the cases you actually act on goes way above 95%.
Why The strongest argument is not about absolute accuracy numbers or downstream metrics. It is that 95% is an average. It tells you how often you are right across all predictions, but it cannot tell you, for any one specific prediction, whether this is the easy case or the hard one. Probabilities do exactly that, and that is what lets you act differently on the borderline cases.
Question 4
You need to represent a probability distribution over a sensor reading that is a real-valued temperature. Tabular storage with 500 bins would cost 2 KB. What is the cheapest correct alternative if your belief about the temperature is roughly bell-shaped?
Why This is the surprising case. When the distribution you actually want is well-described by a formula (here, a Gaussian), the parametric form is dramatically cheaper than the tabular form. 250 times cheaper here, and you get continuous resolution as a bonus. The price you pay is the assumption that the shape really is bell-curved.
Why When your belief is well-described by a known formula, you do not need to bin it at all. A Gaussian is just two numbers: mean and standard deviation. Eight bytes total, infinite resolution, every real number gets a probability density. That is the whole point of parametric representations. They trade flexibility for efficiency, and the trade is worth it when the shape fits.
Question 5
You are trying to decide whether to buy a new GPU. The price is on the website, your budget is in your bank account, the use case is in your head. Probability problem, or not?
Why Correct, and this is the trap most people fall into when they first learn this. "Decision under multiple inputs" is not the same as "decision under uncertainty." You know the price. You know your budget. You know your use case. There is no hidden fact you are estimating from evidence. It is arithmetic on known quantities. Probability shows up when there is something you cannot directly observe that you are trying to guess from things you can.
Why Apply the hidden-fact test from earlier in the post: is there something about the world I cannot directly see, that I am trying to estimate from evidence? Here, you can see everything. Price, budget, use case. There is no hidden quantity. It is a decision under complete information, which is just arithmetic, not probability. If you were buying second-hand from someone whose claims you could not verify, that would be a probability problem. The hidden fact would be "does this GPU actually work as described?"
