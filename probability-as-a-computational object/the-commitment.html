Probability as a Computational Object

The Commitment

A distribution is what turns counts into beliefs. One concept, two file formats, and one shock about densities you have to make peace with.

The thing we keep using

I owe you a confession. Since Class 1, I have been using a particular kind of object without naming it. We said the motor classifier emits "70% confidence the motor is failing." You accepted that. We talked about belief and action, vocabulary and events, random variables. At no point did we say what the 70% actually is.

The 70% is not an outcome. It is not an event. It is not a random variable. It is a different kind of object, and it has been waiting in the wings the entire time. Today it walks on stage.

The object is called a distribution. By the end of this post, you will know what a distribution is as a concept, why we need one, and the two different ways to write a distribution down depending on the situation. The two ways are called the PMF and the PDF, and I want to be honest with you up front: they are not two different things. They are two ways to record the same concept, used in different situations. One concept. Two file formats. Most courses confuse these and the confusion lasts for years. We are going to skip the confusion by being precise from the start.

This is going to be long. I am going to take my time.

The concept, before any formulas

Let me describe a distribution without notation, because the concept is more fundamental than the notation, and the notation has fooled many people into thinking it is the concept.

A distribution is a commitment. Specifically, a commitment about how the probability mass is divided among the possible outcomes of a random experiment.

That sentence is doing real work. Let me unpack it.

When you see a piece of code like if (rand() % 100 < 30) take_path_A();, you understand without being told that path A happens about 30% of the time and path B about 70%. You did not run the code. You did not count anything. You looked at the structure, and you committed to a belief about what it will do. You allocated 30% of your expectation to path A and 70% to path B.

The commitment you made, "30% to A, 70% to B," is a distribution. It exists in your head before any experiment runs. It is a complete statement about how the probability mass is divided. Two outcomes, each one gets a weight, the weights sum to one. That object, taken as a whole, is the distribution.

A distribution is a commitment about how probability mass is divided among the possible outcomes of a random experiment. It is the object that captures "what tends to happen," as opposed to "what just happened" (an outcome) or "what could happen" (an event).

You can hold a distribution before any data exists. You can update one when you get new evidence. The distribution lives at a different level from the individual outcomes. It is a summary of expectation across all possible outcomes.

This concept is the thing. The rest of the post is about how to write it down in two different situations.

Why we need this object

Before I show you how to write a distribution down, I want you to feel the need for one.

You ran a motor classifier 1000 times. You observed {healthy: 920, degrading: 60, failing: 20}. Useful information. But it is history. Now your boss walks over and asks four questions in a row.

"How many failing labels will I get next year?" You cannot answer with the history alone. The history tells you what already happened. To predict the future, you have to commit to the claim that the underlying tendency producing 20 out of 1000 will continue. The commitment is a distribution. Once you have it, the prediction is easy. 10000 windows next year, distribution says 0.02 for failing, you expect about 200. Without the commitment, you have nothing to multiply.

"Is our new classifier better than the old one?" The old classifier produces one set of weights, the new one another. To say one is better, you compare two distributions. The random variables are the same (both output one of three labels), so they cannot distinguish the classifiers. The only thing that distinguishes them is the weights they place on outcomes.

"What is the expected cost per window?" Each failing label triggers a 1000-rupee investigation. Each healthy and degrading label is free. The expected cost is 0.92 × 0 + 0.06 × 0 + 0.02 × 1000 = 20 rupees per window. You cannot compute that without weights.

"Why is Tuesday's failure rate so high?" You have been seeing 0.02 forever. On Tuesday it jumps to 0.18. Something has changed. You can only notice the change because you had a distribution to compare against.

None of these is about counting what already happened. They are all about carrying expectation forward, comparing, averaging, baselining. The distribution is the object that does all four. The counts may have fed the distribution, but once it exists you can throw the counts away and still do the four operations.

Counts describe what happened. A distribution describes what tends to happen. The promotion from one to the other is the move that makes probability useful in engineering.

That promotion is what we are doing today. The visual below makes it physical. Counts stream in from a simulated classifier. As they accumulate, the empirical distribution emerges. Then you can promote that distribution into a commitment, and watch the four operations come online once you do.

Writing it down, discrete case

When the random variable is discrete (only countably many possible values), writing the distribution down is easy. You list each possible value and how much weight sits on it.

For the motor classifier, after enough observation, the distribution looks something like:

P(X = healthy) = 0.92, P(X = degrading) = 0.06, P(X = failing) = 0.02

That is the whole distribution. Three numbers. Total of one. Each tells you how much probability mass sits on that specific outcome. This object, the function that maps each possible value of the random variable to its probability mass, has a name. It is called a probability mass function, or PMF. In code:

float motor_pmf(int x) {
    switch(x) {
        case 0: return 0.92f;   // healthy
        case 1: return 0.06f;   // degrading
        case 2: return 0.02f;   // failing
        default: return 0.0f;
    }
}

I want to be careful about what the PMF is, relative to the distribution, because if I muddle this you will think "PMF" and "distribution" are synonyms. They are not.

The distribution is the commitment. The abstract concept. The PMF is the way we write that commitment down in the discrete case. When you hand someone a PMF, you are handing them a complete description of the distribution in a form they can compute with. The distribution is the idea. The PMF is the file format.

Same as the difference between a melody and sheet music. The melody is the thing. The sheet music is one way to record it. There are other ways: MIDI, audio file, humming. The melody is the object. The recording is the representation.

The distribution is the concept. The PMF is one format for writing that concept down, used when the random variable is discrete.

In a few sections, we will see that for continuous random variables, the PMF format breaks. We will need a different format. The underlying object, the distribution, will still be the same concept. Only the format will change.

What the PMF has to satisfy

The constraints on a PMF fall out of a physical picture, and I want to use the picture, because it sets up the continuous case.

Imagine the number line. The possible values of the random variable are three points on it: 0, 1, 2 (healthy, degrading, failing). You have one unit of mass, like one kilogram of clay. You divide that mass among the three points. You put 920 grams on 0, 60 on 1, 20 on 2.

Three constraints follow immediately, without controversy. Every weight is non-negative, because you cannot put negative clay on a point. The total is exactly one, because you started with one kilogram and distributed all of it. For non-overlapping events, the weights add, because the clay sits on different points and you can sum it. These three constraints are the axioms of probability. They sound grand in textbooks. They are just consequences of dividing mass among points.

The continuous problem

Now I am going to break this.

Consider a random variable that is continuous. The temperature of your room, measured by a high-precision thermometer. The temperature can be 23.4, or 23.41, or 23.4159, or 23.41592653. There is no smallest unit. Between any two temperatures, there is another temperature. The random variable's range is the real number line, or some interval of it.

Try to write down a PMF for the temperature. You want to assign mass to each possible value. What is the mass at the value 23.4?

Let me walk you down to the answer. Suppose we assign some small positive mass m to 23.4. By symmetry, we should assign roughly the same mass to 23.41, to 23.42, to 23.39. There are infinitely many values close to 23.4. If each one gets a tiny positive mass, the total adds up to infinity. That violates the constraint that the total has to be one.

So m cannot be 0.001. It has to be smaller. How small? Smaller than any positive number. No matter how small you pick m, you can fit more than 1/m distinct values into the range, and the total will exceed 1.

The only number smaller than every positive number is zero. So the mass at the value 23.4 must be exactly zero.

For a continuous random variable, the probability of any specific value is exactly zero. Not very small. Zero.

I want you to feel the shock of this. If you skip past it, the rest of this post will feel like magic instead of reasoning. The probability that the temperature is exactly 23.4 degrees is zero.

But wait. The thermometer just read 23.4. Something happened. How can the probability of something be zero?

The resolution is that the probability of any specific reading was zero in the same way that the area of a single point is zero. A point has no width, so no area. But a range of points has positive width and positive area. The probability you actually care about, in the continuous case, is the probability of a range. That the temperature is between 23.35 and 23.45, say. That probability is positive. Any specific value inside the range has probability zero, but the range as a whole has mass.

The discrete picture had mass sitting on points. The continuous picture has mass smeared along the line, like butter on toast. You cannot ask "how much butter is at this exact point?" The question has no useful answer. You can ask "how much butter is on this stretch of toast?" That has a perfectly good answer: integrate the butter thickness over the stretch.

The PDF: writing the smear down

We need a function that records this smear. The PMF will not work because the PMF puts mass at each point, and we just established that mass at any point is zero. The new function tells you the density of the mass at each point, rather than the mass itself. Density is "mass per unit length." It is what you have to integrate to recover mass.

This object is called the probability density function, or PDF. It is a function from values to non-negative numbers, but the numbers it returns are not probabilities. They are densities. The probability of a range is the integral of the PDF over that range. The PDF is the height of a curve. The probability is the area under the curve.

The visual below shows the same concept (a distribution) in both formats. Click between the examples. You will see that the underlying object is the same: a commitment about how mass is divided. What changes is whether the bookkeeping uses bars at points (PMF) or a curve over a range (PDF). The mental shift from "read off a value" to "integrate over a range" is the only thing that fundamentally differs.

The PDF that exceeds 1, and why it is fine

There is one fact about the PDF that always feels wrong on first encounter, and I want to address it head-on. The value of the PDF at a single point can be larger than 1. This sounds contradictory because we just spent half the post saying "probabilities are at most one." The resolution is one sentence.

The PDF is not a probability. It is a density. The two are different objects.

Probabilities are at most one. Densities are not. The PDF can take a value of 5 or 10 or 1000 at a single point, depending on how concentrated the distribution is. What is constrained is the total area under the PDF, which must equal exactly 1.

The physical analogy locks this in. Think about water density versus the amount of water. Water has a density of about 1000 kilograms per cubic metre. That number is large. There is no upper bound on density. A neutron star has density around 10^17 kg/m³. But the mass of water in a glass is small, maybe 0.25 kg. Mass and density are different objects:

mass = density × volume

A small volume of dense water gives a small mass. A huge density times a tiny volume gives a perfectly reasonable mass. Nothing exotic. Same structure for probability:

probability ≈ density × length

A small range times a high density gives a perfectly reasonable probability, in [0, 1]. The PDF is the density. The probability is the integral.

The visual below makes this concrete. Drag the slider to change the width of the interval over which a uniform distribution sits. The total area under the curve is always 1 (because the total mass has to be 1). So as you squeeze the interval, the height has to grow to keep the area constant. Watch the height climb above 1, above 5, above 50, as you make the interval smaller. The height is just density. It can be anything.

It does not mean the probability has somehow exceeded 1.

The 70% from Class 1, finally named

Now I can tell you what the 70% from Class 1 actually was.

When the motor classifier said "70% confident the motor is failing," it was reporting one entry of a PMF over the three motor states. The full PMF might have been {healthy: 0.27, degrading: 0.03, failing: 0.70}. The 70% was the entry corresponding to failing.

The classifier's output, in full, is a distribution. It is a PMF over the three possible motor states. When the model says "I am 70% confident it is failing," the model is reporting one slice of its PMF. The application reads the PMF and decides what action to take.

This is what we meant in Class 1 when we said "the model produces a distribution, the application produces a decision." The distribution is the PMF. We did not have the word yet, so we said "the probabilities" or "the confidence values." Now we have the precise name.

When the random variable is continuous, like a regression model predicting room temperature, the model's output is a PDF instead of a PMF. Same concept, different format. The model is still emitting a distribution. The bookkeeping is different because the values are now continuous.

You have been using distributions, in both PMF and PDF form, since Class 1. We have now named them.

What you should leave with

Three things.

First, a distribution is a commitment about how probability mass is divided among the possible outcomes of a random experiment. It is the abstract concept. It is what the model emits when it says "70% confident." It is what lets you predict, compare systems, compute expected values, and detect anomalies. The promotion from counts (what happened) to a commitment (what tends to happen) is the move that makes probability useful in engineering.

Second, the PMF and PDF are two different ways to write the same concept down, used in different situations. The PMF is used when the random variable is discrete. It returns probability mass directly. The PDF is used when the random variable is continuous. It returns density, and you integrate over a range to recover a probability. They are not two different kinds of distributions. They are two file formats for the same kind of object. The split between them is forced by the structure of the random variable, not by anything intrinsic to distributions.

Third, the continuous case forces a real conceptual shift. For continuous random variables, the probability of any specific value is exactly zero. Only ranges have positive probability. The PDF returns density, not probability, and PDF values can exceed 1 when the distribution is concentrated. A high PDF value just means the mass is packed into a small neighbourhood. The total area under any PDF is exactly 1, which keeps probabilities (the areas) bounded in [0, 1].

Next class, we move from distributions to samples. A sample is one number that came out when the experiment actually ran. The distribution is the recipe. The sample is what comes out of the oven. We will see how samples relate to distributions, what it means to draw a sample, and why this operation is the basic computational primitive of every probabilistic algorithm.

Check yourself

Five questions. Pick the option you think is right. If you got it, that option turns green. If you missed, the wrong option turns red and the right one lights up too. An explanation appears underneath either way.

Question 1
What is a distribution, conceptually? Do not collapse to "PMF" or "PDF."
Why Yes. The distribution is a commitment, an abstract object that lives above any particular file format. It is what lets you do anything forward-looking with probability: predict, compare, compute expected values, detect anomalies. The PMF and PDF are formats for writing this commitment down, but the commitment itself is the concept.
Why The trap is collapsing the concept down to its bookkeeping. A list of probabilities is the PMF, which is one format. A specific outcome probability is one slice of that format. A function from counts to probabilities is how you might estimate one from data. The distribution itself is the underlying commitment: how the probability mass is divided across the possible outcomes. That commitment exists independently of how you write it down, and it is what lets you extrapolate to situations you have not directly observed.
Question 2
Why do we use PMFs in some cases and PDFs in others?
Why Right. The structure of the random variable forces the format. Discrete random variables (countably many possible values) can have mass at each value, so we write a PMF that returns probability directly. Continuous random variables have infinitely many values in any interval, which forces the mass at any single point to be zero, which means we record density (mass per unit length) and integrate to get probabilities. Same underlying concept (a distribution), different format for different structures.
Why The choice is not about ease, accuracy, or recency. It is forced by the structure of the random variable. Discrete random variables can have mass at each point and we record this directly with a PMF. Continuous random variables have infinitely many points and the mass at any single point has to be zero, so we record density (mass per unit length) instead and integrate to get probabilities. The concept being captured (a distribution) is the same. The file format differs because the random variable's structure differs.
Question 3
A PDF for a continuous random variable takes the value 4.0 at the point y = 0.5. Is this a valid PDF?
Why Yes. The PDF is a density, not a probability. The only constraint on a PDF is that it is non-negative everywhere and its total integral is exactly 1. The value at any individual point can be any non-negative number. A high PDF value just means the mass is concentrated in a small neighbourhood. Probabilities are areas under the PDF, and those are bounded by 1; the heights are not.
Why The PDF is a density, not a probability. The two are different objects. Probabilities are at most 1, but densities are not constrained that way. A PDF can take any non-negative value at any single point, including values well above 1. The only constraint is that the total area under the PDF integrates to exactly 1. A PDF value of 4.0 at y = 0.5 just means the mass is concentrated near 0.5. As long as the total area is 1, the PDF is valid. (Physical analogy: water has density 1000 kg/m³, but the mass of water in a glass is a small number. Density can be huge; total mass is bounded by how much you have.)
Question 4
A regression model predicts indoor temperature as a Gaussian centered at some value. What is the probability that the model's predicted temperature equals exactly 23.4 degrees?
Why Exactly. For a continuous random variable, the probability of any specific value is zero. Not very small. Zero. This is forced by the math: infinitely many possible values in any interval, total mass must be 1, so individual values get no mass at all. The meaningful question is always about a range, never about a single value. PDF values are not probabilities; they are densities. Probabilities are integrals of densities over ranges.
Why For a continuous random variable, the probability of any specific value is exactly zero, regardless of σ or the PDF value at that point. The PDF value at 23.4 is a density, not a probability. To get a probability, you integrate the PDF over a range. The meaningful question is always "what is the probability of being in some interval," never "what is the probability of being at this exact value." If you ask the wrong question for continuous random variables, you always get zero. The right question, for continuous, is about ranges.
Question 5
You have a binary classifier whose random variable has values {pass, fail}. The classifier is deployed in two contexts: easy conditions and hard conditions. In easy conditions, the PMF is {pass: 0.95, fail: 0.05}. In hard conditions, it is {pass: 0.60, fail: 0.40}. What does this example illustrate?
Why Right. This is the random-variable-versus-distribution split, in action. The random variable defines the structure: what outcomes can come out. The distribution puts weights on that structure. Two different scenarios produce two different distributions over the same random variable, and the classifier does not need to be retrained or considered "different" between them. This separation is what lets one model serve different contexts with different distributions, and it is the reason why distributions are tracked as separate objects from random variables in real ML systems.
Why The random variable and the distribution are two different objects. The random variable is the structure: what outcomes can come out (pass or fail, two values). The distribution is the weights placed on those outcomes for a specific scenario. Different scenarios produce different distributions over the same random variable. The classifier is not broken, and it does not need to be retrained; it just emits different distributions in different contexts. This separation between structure (random variable) and weights (distribution) is what makes a single model usable across many situations.
