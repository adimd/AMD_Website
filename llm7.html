1. Picking Up the Thread: From Geometry to Decisions

In the last few chapters, we carefully built up three ideas:

  • We can treat every (center → context) pair from the corpus as a tiny teaching signal.
  • Each word has two vectors: one when it is the center (in matrix W) and one when it is the context (in matrix C).
  • Given a center and context, we can compute a geometric score:

    score(center → context) = W[center] · C[context]

All of this was grounded in our tiny universe:

I love my dog.
I love my cat.
You love my dog.
You love my cat.

We also fixed the embedding dimension to 2, so every word is literally a 2D arrow in a plane. The dot product became a way to measure how aligned two arrows are.

Now we will ask a very important question:

We have scores. But what are we actually trying to maximize?

We will find that “maximize the score” is not the right goal. Instead, we will slowly move toward: maximize the probability of the correct context. And to do that, we will gently introduce ex and softmax.

2. Why “Just Make the Score Big” Is Not Enough

Take one real event from our tiny corpus:

(love → You)

Our intuition says:

“If (love → You) is a real thing in the corpus, we want its score to be high.”

So we might naively think:

“Let’s train the model to maximize score(love → You).”

This sounds reasonable at first glance, but falls apart when we examine it carefully. Let’s see why.

2.1. The “blow up the vectors” problem

Suppose the model sets:

  • W[love] = (10⁶, 10⁶)
  • C[You] = (10⁶, 10⁶)

Then:

score(love → You) = 10⁶ × 10⁶ + 10⁶ × 10⁶ = 2 × 10¹²

Enormous score. Has the model learned anything meaningful? Not really.

We have not told the model:

  • how big is “big enough”,
  • how this score should compare to score(love → I),
  • how it should compare to impossible contexts like (love → dog).

Simply inflating vector magnitudes can always make scores larger. But that does not encode structure.

2.2. Language is about choices, not absolute scores

When “love” appears as the center word, the real question is not:

“Is score(love → You) big?”

The real question is:

  • “Given the center word love, which context is more likely: I, You, my, dog, cat, or some other word?”

We care about how “You” competes with other possible neighbours of “love”. In other words:

We care about scores relative to other scores, not scores in isolation.

This “relative” viewpoint is exactly what probability gives us.

3. Reframing the Goal: From Scores to p(context | center)

For each center word, say love, there are many possible contexts the model could imagine. The corpus tells us which ones actually occur:

  • (love → I)
  • (love → You)
  • (love → my)

Notice that we never saw:

  • (love → dog)
  • (love → cat)

So a good model should reflect that behaviour. In probabilistic terms, we want:

  • p(I | love) — reasonably high
  • p(You | love) — reasonably high
  • p(my | love) — very high (it always appears around love in our tiny corpus)
  • p(dog | love), p(cat | love) — very close to zero

So our high-level goal becomes:

When the center is “love”, assign high probability to the contexts that actually appear with “love” in the corpus, and low probability to those that don’t.

We have shifted from:

  • “Maximize scores for true pairs”

to:

  • “Maximize the probability of the true context given the center.”

The scores will still be the raw material — but probability is now the thing we want to maximize.

4. Using Scores as Raw Preferences

We already have:

score(center → context) = W[center] · C[context]

Think of each score as a kind of raw preference:

  • If score(love → I) is higher than score(love → dog), then the model “prefers” I over dog as a neighbour of love.
  • If score(love → my) is the highest among all contexts, then my is currently the model’s favourite neighbour for love.

But these raw scores are just real numbers. They can be negative, positive, large, or tiny. We need to transform them into something that behaves more like a “strength” of belief.

This suggests a two-step plan:

  1. Map each score to a positive strength value.
  2. Normalize these strengths so that they form a probability distribution.

We will do exactly this in the next two sections.

5. Step 1 – From Score to Positive “Strength” via ex

We want a function f(x) that takes any real number x (our score) and returns a positive number:

  • f(x) > 0 for all x,
  • f is increasing: larger score → larger strength,
  • ideally, it should highlight differences; slightly higher scores should become noticeably stronger.

The exponential function does all of this beautifully:

f(x) = ex

Some basic behaviour of ex:

  • e0 = 1
  • e1 ≈ 2.718
  • e2 ≈ 7.39
  • e-1 ≈ 0.367

So:

  • score = 0 → strength = 1
  • score = 2 → strength ≈ 7.39 (much stronger)
  • score = -1 → strength ≈ 0.37 (much weaker)

This has a nice interpretation:

Higher scores do not just increase linearly — they increase multiplicatively in strength.

So we define:

strength(center → context) = escore(center → context)

Now every center–context pair has a positive strength value. But we still don’t have probabilities yet.

6. Step 2 – From Strengths to Probabilities: Softmax

Imagine a fixed center word, say:

center = love

Our vocabulary (in this tiny universe) is:

  • I, You, love, my, dog, cat

We can compute a score for “love” with each possible context:

  • score(love → I)
  • score(love → You)
  • score(love → my)
  • score(love → dog)
  • score(love → cat)
  • score(love → love) – weird, but mathematically allowed

Then convert each into a strength using ex:

strength(love → context) = escore(love → context)

Now we have a bunch of positive numbers, one for each possible context word. How do we make this into a probability distribution p(context | love)?

We use the simplest possible idea: normalize.

p(context | love) = escore(love → context) / Σall c' escore(love → c')

This is the softmax function:

  • Everything is positive (because of ex).
  • Probabilities sum to 1 (because we divide by the sum over all contexts).
  • Higher scores produce much higher probabilities, thanks to the exponential.

Now we have exactly what we wanted:

A way to turn geometric scores into proper probabilities p(context | center).

7. A Tiny Numerical Example with “love”

Let’s imagine concrete (fake) scores for “love” as center:

  • score(love → I) = 2.0
  • score(love → You) = 2.1
  • score(love → my) = 2.5
  • score(love → dog) = -1.0
  • score(love → cat) = -1.2

Turn them into strengths:

  • strength(love → I) = e2.0 ≈ 7.39
  • strength(love → You) = e2.1 ≈ 8.17
  • strength(love → my) = e2.5 ≈ 12.18
  • strength(love → dog) = e-1.0 ≈ 0.37
  • strength(love → cat) = e-1.2 ≈ 0.30

Total strength:

S ≈ 7.39 + 8.17 + 12.18 + 0.37 + 0.30 ≈ 28.41

Now probabilities:

  • p(I | love) ≈ 7.39 / 28.41 ≈ 0.26
  • p(You | love) ≈ 8.17 / 28.41 ≈ 0.29
  • p(my | love) ≈ 12.18 / 28.41 ≈ 0.43
  • p(dog | love) ≈ 0.37 / 28.41 ≈ 0.013
  • p(cat | love) ≈ 0.30 / 28.41 ≈ 0.010

Look at what has happened:

  • “my” has the highest probability – the model really believes in (love, my).
  • “I” and “You” also have reasonable probabilities.
  • “dog” and “cat” are almost zero – they are not directly neighbours of “love” in our corpus.

This matches our tiny dataset extremely well. We did not hard-code any of this behaviour — it emerged from scores and exponentials being turned into probabilities.

Now our model no longer has “just scores” – it has actual beliefs about which words are likely to appear next.

8. What Are We Maximizing Now, Exactly?

We started by saying:

“Maximizing scores alone doesn’t quite make sense.”

Now we can say something more precise:

For each observed pair (center → context) from the training set, we want to maximize p(context | center).

Because p(context | center) is defined via softmax, this indirectly means:

  • we want to increase the score of the true context,
  • and decrease the scores of other possible contexts (so they don’t steal probability mass).

So we are still “maximizing the score”, but now in a relative sense:

We are not trying to make scores huge. We are trying to make the true score bigger than the competing scores.

Probability (via softmax) gives us a clean way to measure how well we are doing at that.

9. The Big Picture: From Raw Geometry to Learnable Beliefs

Let’s stitch Blogs 4, 5, and 6 into one continuous story:

  1. Blog 4 – Conceptual:
    Language gives us events like (center, context). We want word vectors W and C that capture the structure behind these events.
  2. Blog 5 – Mathematical:
    Each word gets two vectors (center + context). A dot product W[center] · C[context] gives a score that measures geometric compatibility.
  3. Blog 6 – Probabilistic:
    Scores alone are not enough. We pass scores through ex, normalize with softmax, and get probabilities p(context | center). Our goal becomes: maximize the probability of the correct context for each center.
Indirectly, by maximizing these probabilities, we are shaping W and C so that the geometry of the embedding space mirrors the statistical structure of language.

In the next chapter, we will answer a natural follow-up:

“If our goal is to maximize these probabilities, what does the explicit loss function look like?”

That path will take us to log-likelihood and cross-entropy — the final piece that connects geometry, probability, and gradient-based learning into one training loop.
