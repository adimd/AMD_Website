How Computers Work at the Lowest Level

Logic Gates: How Silicon Learned to Think

Before a processor can add two numbers or make a decision, it needs something physical to do the work. That something is a transistor. And from transistors, everything else follows.

Start with the transistor

We said in Topic 1 that everything in a computer is numbers. But we never asked what actually does something with those numbers. What is the physical thing that looks at two bits and produces a third? The answer starts with a transistor, and to understand logic gates properly you need to feel what a transistor actually is, not just know the word.

A transistor is a switch. Not a mechanical switch you flip with your finger, but one controlled entirely by voltage. Apply enough voltage to the control terminal and current flows through the device. Remove the voltage and current stops. On and off, controlled electrically, with no moving parts whatsoever.

The specific type that matters for modern chips is called a MOSFET, which stands for Metal Oxide Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor. The name describes its physical structure. It has three terminals. The source is where current enters. The drain is where current exits. The gate is the control terminal, a thin layer of metal separated from the semiconductor beneath it by an insulating layer of oxide, which is essentially glass.

Because of that glass layer, no current actually flows into the gate itself. What controls the transistor is the electric field produced by the voltage on the gate. When you apply a positive voltage, the field it creates attracts electrons in the semiconductor, pulling them toward the surface and forming a thin conducting channel between source and drain. Current flows. Remove the voltage and the channel collapses. The entire switching action happens in a layer just a few atoms thick.

Modern transistors in chips like Apple's M4 or Intel's latest processors have gate lengths of around 3 nanometres. A strand of DNA is about 2.5 nanometres wide. You are switching current on and off across a distance comparable to a single molecule, billions of times per second.

Two flavours of transistor, and why you need both

There are two types of MOSFET and both are essential for understanding how real gates work.

An NMOS transistor behaves like the one described above. When the gate voltage is high and exceeds a threshold, the channel forms and current flows. Apply a 1, the switch is on. Apply a 0, it's off. This is the straightforward kind.

A PMOS transistor is the complement. It switches on when the gate voltage is low. Apply a 0 and current flows. Apply a 1 and it stops. It conducts exactly when the other kind does not.

Real chips use both together in a design called CMOS, Complementary MOS. Every logic gate in your processor pairs one NMOS and one PMOS transistor. When the NMOS is on, the PMOS is off, and vice versa. This matters for two reasons.

The first is stability. Without the complementary pair you'd need a resistor to pull the output to a defined level when the transistor isn't conducting. Resistors draw current continuously and leave the output voltage somewhere in the middle, which is ambiguous and noisy. CMOS actively drives the output to either a clean high or a clean low at all times. There is never a floating, uncertain state.

The second reason is power. This is the critical one. Because one transistor is always blocking when the other is conducting, current only flows during the brief switching moment itself, when one transistor is turning on and the other is turning off. A CMOS gate sitting at rest draws almost no current at all. This is why your phone doesn't drain its battery simply from having fifteen billion transistors exist inside it.

The consequence runs in the other direction too. Every time the clock ticks, every gate that changes state draws a pulse of current during that switching moment. More clock ticks per second means more switching moments per second, which means more power consumed per second. This is why faster processors run hotter. It's not a design flaw. It's physics.

From switches to decisions

A single transistor on its own is just on or off. The interesting thing happens when you connect transistors together in ways that make decisions. Wire two transistors in series, one after the other, and current can only flow if the first switch is on AND the second is on. If either one is off, the circuit is broken. You have just built a physical machine that computes the word "and".

Wire two transistors in parallel instead and current can flow if the first switch is on OR the second is on. Either one being true is enough. You have built "or".

These are your first logic gates: AND and OR. They are not abstractions. They are physical arrangements of transistors that implement logical operations in silicon. And there is a third gate even simpler than both of them: NOT, which has one input and flips whatever it receives. Input 1 gives output 0. Input 0 gives output 1. Engineers call it an inverter.

Three gates: AND, OR, NOT. This is the complete set needed to build any logical computation that can ever exist. Every piece of software ever written, every calculation done on a computer, reduces to combinations of these three operations applied to bits.

NAND: the gate that rules them all

Take an AND gate and attach a NOT gate to its output. The result is NAND, short for Not-AND. Its output is 0 only when both inputs are 1. Everything else gives 1. It looks like a minor variation on AND.

It is actually the most important gate ever invented, and the reason is universality.

It turns out you can build AND, OR, and NOT using only NAND gates. Every single one of them. Which means you can build any logical computation using nothing but NAND gates repeated over and over. This property is called universality and NOR shares it too.

The manufacturing reason this matters is not that having multiple gate types makes fabrication impossible. Modern fabs can build different gates. The real benefit is that chip designers can optimise one cell type extremely well, tune its transistor sizes, minimise its area, reduce its delay, and then use that single optimised cell everywhere. It simplifies the design library and the entire optimisation process. This is why you'll hear chip designers talk about "standard cell libraries" built almost entirely from NAND and NOR combinations.

XOR: the gate built for arithmetic

There is one more gate worth knowing before we can build an adder in Topic 3, and that is XOR, exclusive or.

Regular OR gives a 1 if either input is 1, including when both are 1. XOR gives a 1 if the inputs differ, and a 0 if they are the same. When both inputs are 0, output is 0. When they differ, output is 1. When both are 1, output is 0.

That last case is the crucial one. 1 XOR 1 = 0. Look at it alongside binary addition. In binary, 1 + 1 = 10, which is zero in the current column with a carry of 1 into the next. XOR gives you exactly the sum bit. AND gives you exactly the carry bit. Put XOR and AND together and you have a half adder, the seed of all arithmetic in every processor ever built. We will construct this fully in Topic 3.

XOR has another beautiful property worth knowing: it is its own inverse. XOR any value with a key and you get something that looks random. XOR the result with the same key again and you get the original value back perfectly. This is why XOR sits at the foundation of almost every encryption scheme ever designed, from simple stream ciphers to modern block ciphers like AES.

Propagation delay: gates are fast, but not instant

Here is something textbooks often skip. Logic gates are not instantaneous. When a voltage arrives at a gate's input, there is a small delay before the correct output appears. This is called propagation delay and it's measured in picoseconds, which are trillionths of a second.

The cause goes right back to the transistor structure. The gate terminal, separated from the semiconductor by that thin oxide layer, behaves like a tiny capacitor. Capacitors take time to charge and discharge. When the input switches, the transistor doesn't respond the instant the voltage changes. It responds once enough charge has built up on that capacitance to shift the electric field enough to open or close the channel. Charging takes time, even when we're talking about femtojoules of energy moving in picoseconds.

This matters because gates are chained together. The output of one feeds the input of the next. Delays add up. A single gate might add 10 to 50 picoseconds. Chain 32 of them together and you might accumulate over a nanosecond of total delay. The processor's clock cannot tick again until the slowest chain of gates has fully settled to its correct output. That slowest chain is called the critical path, and shortening it is one of the central challenges of chip design.

This is also why faster clock speeds require smaller transistors. Smaller transistors have smaller capacitances. Smaller capacitances charge faster. Faster charging means shorter propagation delays. Shorter delays mean the clock can tick faster. The entire race to shrink transistors is, at its core, a race against capacitance.

From sand to thought

The material all of this is built from is silicon, which is essentially purified sand. Silicon by itself is a poor conductor. It's a semiconductor, meaning it conducts under some conditions and not others. Doping it with tiny amounts of other elements, phosphorus to create NMOS regions, boron to create PMOS regions, gives you the N-type and P-type areas that make transistors work.

The entire intellectual structure we've built across these two topics, bits, binary, representation, and now logic, sits on top of the fact that sand, refined to extraordinary purity and arranged with atomic precision, can switch billions of times per second and consume almost no power doing it.

A transistor is just a switch. A gate is just transistors making a decision. A processor is just millions of gates making decisions in sequence. The sophistication is entirely in the arrangement, not the components. Sand, organised thoughtfully enough, can run the internet.

In Topic 3 we take gates and connect them into something that can actually do arithmetic. We'll build a 1-bit adder from scratch using only AND, OR, and XOR gates, then chain several together to add any number we like. That's where the leap from logic to computation fully clicks.
