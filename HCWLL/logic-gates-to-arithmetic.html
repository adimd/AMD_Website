How Computers Work at the Lowest Level

Combining Gates: From Logic to Arithmetic

Gates make decisions. But chain enough of them together in the right arrangement and something surprising happens: they can add numbers. This is the step where logic becomes computation.

Why the truth tables are the proof

Before building anything, it's worth pausing on why XOR and AND are the right gates for addition. This isn't something someone decided by convention. It falls directly out of the truth tables.

When you add two single bits A and B, there are exactly four possible cases. Zero plus zero is zero with no carry. Zero plus one is one with no carry. One plus zero is one with no carry. One plus one is two, which in binary is 10, meaning the current column is zero and you carry a one into the next column.

Write out just the sum column across those four cases: 0, 1, 1, 0. Now look at the XOR truth table. It gives exactly 0, 1, 1, 0 for the same inputs. Write out just the carry column: 0, 0, 0, 1. That's the AND truth table exactly. The gates do not need to be adapted or configured for this purpose. Their natural behaviour already describes binary addition perfectly. That match is what makes all of this buildable in silicon.

What the half adder cannot do

The half adder handles the rightmost column of any binary addition perfectly, because nothing sits to its right and there is no incoming carry to deal with. The moment you move one column left, things change. That column receives a carry from the column you just computed. The half adder has only two inputs, A and B. It has nowhere to accept that incoming carry. It is structurally incapable of handling it.

For every column except the rightmost, you need a circuit that takes three inputs: A, B, and Cin. That circuit is the full adder.

The full adder: deriving it from scratch

A full adder has three inputs and two outputs. Inputs are A, B, and Cin. Outputs are Sum and Cout. There are eight possible combinations of three binary inputs.

The sum bit should be 1 whenever an odd number of the three inputs are 1. Think through why. If one input is 1, the total is 1, sum is 1. If two inputs are 1, the total is 2 which is binary 10, so sum is 0. If all three are 1, the total is 3 which is binary 11, so sum is 1. Odd count gives sum 1. Even count gives sum 0. That is exactly what XOR does across three inputs, and you can chain it: first compute P = A XOR B, then compute Sum = P XOR Cin.

The carry out should be 1 whenever two or more of the three inputs are 1. You can write this as three separate conditions joined by OR: A and B are both 1, or A and Cin are both 1, or B and Cin are both 1. That gives Cout = (A AND B) OR (A AND Cin) OR (B AND Cin).

Using the partial sum P = A XOR B, the Cout expression simplifies neatly. Since P is already computed on the way to getting Sum, you can reuse it: Cout = (A AND B) OR (P AND Cin). This is the form used in real circuits because it shares gates between the sum path and the carry path, saving area on silicon.

The full adder is therefore five gates: two XOR, two AND, one OR. Let's verify with the hardest case: A=1, B=1, Cin=1. That's three ones, which should give Sum=1 and Cout=1 since 1+1+1=3 which is binary 11.

P = 1 XOR 1 = 0. Sum = P XOR Cin = 0 XOR 1 = 1. Correct. Cout = (A AND B) OR (P AND Cin) = (1 AND 1) OR (0 AND 1) = 1 OR 0 = 1. Correct.

Chaining full adders: the ripple carry adder

To add two 4-bit numbers you place four full adders side by side. The first one handles bit position 0 (the rightmost). Its carry-in is hardwired to 0 because nothing sits to its right. Its carry-out feeds into the carry-in of the second adder. The second adder's carry-out feeds the third. And so on. The carry-out of the final adder tells you whether the entire addition overflowed.

This arrangement is called a ripple carry adder because the carry signal ripples from right to left through the chain. Each adder waits for the adder to its right to finish before it knows what carry arrived at its input. The result of the whole addition is only valid once the carry has finished its journey through every stage.

Let's trace through a real example. Add 0110 (which is 6) and 0111 (which is 7). The correct answer is 1101, which is 13.

Stage 0: A0=0, B0=1, Cin=0. P=1, Sum=1, Cout=0. Stage 1: A1=1, B1=1, Cin=0. P=0, Sum=0, Cout=1. Stage 2: A2=1, B2=1, Cin=1. P=0, Sum=1, Cout=1. Stage 3: A3=0, B3=0, Cin=1. P=1, Sum=1, Cout=0. Reading the sum bits from stage 3 down to stage 0 gives 1101. That is 13. Correct.

The ripple problem: why it matters at scale

For a 4-bit adder the ripple delay is small enough to feel academic. At 32 or 64 bits it becomes a genuine bottleneck.

The worst case input for a ripple carry adder is not all ones plus all ones. When both inputs are 1 at every position, every stage generates its own carry immediately without needing to wait for anything. The carry does not actually ripple far because each stage produces it independently.

The true worst case is 11111111 plus 00000001, that is 255 plus 1 for an 8-bit adder. Stage 0 gets 1 plus 1, generates a carry. Every stage above it has inputs of 1 and 0, so it is in propagate mode: it will pass on any carry it receives but cannot generate one on its own. The carry from stage 0 has to travel through every single stage in sequence before the final result is valid. Stage 1 cannot finish until stage 0 carries out. Stage 2 cannot finish until stage 1 carries out. All the way to stage 7. That is maximum ripple length, and every step adds propagation delay.

In the worst case the carry visits every single stage. In the best case (both inputs maximum) every stage generates its own carry immediately and the ripple is minimal. The distinction between generate and propagate modes determines how far the carry actually travels.

Carry lookahead: computing all carries at once

The insight behind carry lookahead is that you do not have to wait for a carry to ripple through. You can calculate what every carry will be directly from the original A and B inputs, all at the same time, using a dedicated circuit.

For each bit position i, you compute two signals. Generate (Gi = Ai AND Bi): this position will produce a carry on its own regardless of what carry comes in, because both its inputs are 1. Propagate (Pi = Ai XOR Bi): this position will pass on whatever carry arrives, because exactly one of its inputs is 1. Notice it is XOR not OR: if both inputs are 1 the position generates rather than propagates, so you exclude that case.

With G and P computed for every position simultaneously, you can write a direct formula for every carry:

C1 = G0 OR (P0 AND C0). Carry into position 1 is either generated by position 0, or position 0 propagates the initial carry in.

C2 = G1 OR (P1 AND G0) OR (P1 AND P0 AND C0). Carry into position 2 is generated at position 1, or generated at 0 and propagated through 1, or the initial carry propagated through both.

C3 = G2 OR (P2 AND G1) OR (P2 AND P1 AND G0) OR (P2 AND P1 AND P0 AND C0).

These expressions use only the original G and P values and the initial carry-in. No carry needs to wait for the previous carry. A lookahead unit computes all of them in parallel using a single level of AND and OR gates. The adder's total delay drops from N gate delays down to a small constant, regardless of how many bits you are adding.

The cost is circuit area. The carry expressions grow with each bit position. C31 in a 32-bit adder has 32 terms. Building one giant lookahead unit for all 32 bits would require AND gates with enormous fan-in, which brings its own capacitance delays. The practical solution is hierarchical lookahead: group 4 bits at a time into small lookahead units, then connect those units using a second-level lookahead circuit above them. A 64-bit adder might use three levels of hierarchy. The delay grows logarithmically with word size rather than linearly. A 64-bit carry lookahead adder is only slightly slower than a 4-bit one.

Subtraction is almost free

Once you have an adder, subtraction costs almost nothing extra. The key is two's complement from Topic 1.

To negate a number in two's complement you flip all its bits and add 1. So A minus B is the same as A plus (NOT B) plus 1. Your adder already adds. The carry-in of the first full adder is normally 0. Set it to 1 and you have added 1. Put a NOT gate on every bit of B before it enters the adder and you have flipped all of B's bits. The adder is now computing A plus (NOT B) plus 1, which is A minus B.

In practice this is combined into one circuit called an adder-subtractor. A single control line called SUB selects the mode. XOR gates sit on every B input: XOR with 0 passes B unchanged, XOR with 1 inverts it. The SUB line connects to all those XOR gates and also directly to the carry-in of the first stage. When SUB=0 you get addition. When SUB=1 you get subtraction. Same hardware. Same adder. Two extra wires.

The multiplexer: routing signals based on decisions

Almost every interesting circuit needs to choose between options. Should the processor use the result of the adder or the result of a logic operation? Should it read from register A or register B? The circuit that answers these questions is the multiplexer, almost always written MUX.

A 2-to-1 MUX has three inputs and one output. Two data inputs D0 and D1, and one selector S. When S=0 the output follows D0. When S=1 the output follows D1. The gate-level expression is: Output = (D0 AND NOT S) OR (D1 AND S). When S=0, the D0 term survives and D1 term is blocked. When S=1, the D1 term survives and D0 is blocked. The selector controls which data path is open at any moment.

MUXes scale up straightforwardly. A 4-to-1 MUX uses two selector bits to choose among four inputs. An 8-to-1 MUX uses three selector bits. Inside any processor, MUXes are everywhere: selecting which registers feed the ALU, routing results to the right destination, choosing between different operation modes. They are the traffic controllers of the digital world.

Everything connecting

Step back and look at what these three topics have assembled. In Topic 1 we established that everything in a computer is numbers. In Topic 2 we learned that transistors make gates and gates make decisions. Now in Topic 3 we have seen that gates combined in the right arrangements can add numbers, subtract them, and select between them.

The next step is to bundle all of this into one unit: the ALU. An ALU is just an adder-subtractor, a set of logic operation circuits (AND, OR, XOR applied to all bits in parallel), and a bank of MUXes controlled by an operation selector. The selector is a few bits that tell the ALU which of its internal circuits to connect to the output at any given moment. Add, subtract, AND, OR, compare: the same hardware, different selector value.

After the ALU we have memory. The circuits so far are purely combinational: they react to their inputs immediately and produce an output, but they remember nothing. For a computer to actually run a program it needs to store intermediate results. That requires a different kind of circuit, one built from feedback loops rather than forward-flowing logic. That is Topic 5, and it is where things get genuinely strange in the best possible way.

An adder is five gates. A subtractor is an adder plus two wires. A MUX is three gates. An ALU is these components bundled together. The most powerful calculating machine humanity has ever built is, at its foundation, a very large pile of very simple things arranged very carefully.
