How Computers Work at the Lowest Level

Representation: How Computers Speak

Before a computer can add, remember, or decide anything, it needs a language. This is where that language comes from and why it looks the way it does.

The question nobody asks

Here is something worth sitting with before we talk about bits or binary or any of that. Humans count in decimal, ten digits, zero through nine, because we have ten fingers. That's genuinely the whole reason. It's an accident of biology, not a mathematical law. So when engineers in the 1940s started building machines that could compute, the obvious thing would have been to make them count the same way we do. Why didn't they?

They tried. Early computing machines used analogue signals, varying voltages meant to represent different digits. Four volts for the digit 4, seven volts for 7, and so on. It sounds reasonable until you actually build it, because voltage in the real world is not a clean, stable thing. It drifts with temperature. It fluctuates when nearby components warm up. It picks up interference from power lines, from radio waves, from the simple fact of electrons moving through imperfect copper.

The problem isn't just that readings are slightly off. The problem is what happens next. A misread 3 becomes an input to the next calculation. That calculation produces a wrong result. That result feeds into the next step. Errors compound. Within a handful of operations, the answer is garbage, and you have no way to know where it went wrong.

Claude Shannon, working at Bell Labs in 1948 (and yes, this is partially who the AI you might use is named after), put this on firm mathematical ground. His argument was simple: if you only ever need to distinguish two states, you can put them so far apart electrically that noise can never bridge the gap. Call anything below one volt a zero. Call anything above four volts a one. Now noise has to shift your signal by three entire volts to cause an error. In normal electronics, that essentially never happens. The computation becomes reliable not because you built better components but because you redesigned the problem.

That single decision cascades into everything. Every computer ever built, from the machines that filled entire rooms in the 1950s to the chip in your phone, works in binary because of it.

A bit is just an answer to a yes/no question

Once you commit to two states, you need a name for one unit of that information. The word bit was coined by statistician John Tukey in 1947, a contraction of binary digit, and Shannon borrowed it as the atomic unit of his information theory. One bit is the answer to a yes-or-no question. Is the voltage high or low? Is the magnetic patch north or south? Is the surface of a CD pitted or flat? On or off. That's it.

By itself a bit can only represent two things. But the moment you chain bits together, the number of possible combinations explodes. Two bits give you four combinations. Three bits give you eight. Eight bits give you 256. The pattern is always 2 raised to the power of however many bits you have.

Each bit position is worth double the one to its right: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Click bits to flip them and watch the value build up. Try reaching 255 (all on) or 170 (alternating). Notice that the leftmost bit alone is worth more than all the others combined.

Eight bits became a byte, but not for elegant reasons

With 8 bits chained together you get 256 possible values, zero through 255. This grouping, called one byte, became the standard atom of computer memory. It's tempting to think there's some deep mathematical reason for choosing 8. There isn't, really. Early machines used 6-bit and 7-bit groupings. IBM standardised the 8-bit byte in the 1960s with the System/360, the industry followed, and a combination of 256 values being enough for a single text character, 8 dividing cleanly into halves and quarters, and simple historical momentum locked it in. The number isn't sacred.

Text is just numbers by agreement

Once you can represent numbers, everything else becomes a question of agreeing on what numbers mean. In 1963 a committee of engineers published ASCII, the American Standard Code for Information Interchange, which assigned a number to every letter, digit, and punctuation mark they thought mattered. Capital A became 65. Lowercase a became 97. The space character became 32. The digit character "7" (the symbol, not the number seven) became 55.

So when you type "Hi" and it gets stored, what actually goes into memory is the numbers 72 and 105. Two bytes. The computer has absolutely no idea what "H" or "i" means as letters. It stores 72 and 105, and the agreement about what those numbers represent lives entirely in the software reading them back. Strip that software away and the hardware sees nothing but two small integers.

ASCII covered 128 characters, enough for English but a disaster for everyone else. Arabic, Hindi, Chinese, Japanese, Greek, mathematical symbols, musical notation, none of it fit. The fix came decades later with Unicode, which now maps over 140,000 characters across 159 writing systems to unique numbers. The principle is exactly the same as ASCII. The table is just vastly larger. Even emoji are Unicode numbers and the 😂 face is character 128,514.

The negative number problem

Everything above handles positive whole numbers cleanly. The moment you need to represent a negative number, things get more interesting, and the history of how engineers solved it is genuinely instructive.

Attempt one: sign and magnitude

The first approach anyone reaches for is the obvious one: steal one bit as a sign flag. If the leftmost bit is 0, the number is positive. If it's 1, the number is negative. The remaining 7 bits hold the magnitude, meaning the size of the number ignoring sign.

This works for reading numbers. It fails the moment you try to compute with them. Try adding +5 and −5 using normal binary addition and you get −10, not zero. The addition hardware that works perfectly for positive numbers simply produces wrong answers when one of the inputs has that sign bit set. You would need completely separate subtraction circuitry that detects signs, decides which operand is larger, subtracts the smaller from the larger, and assigns the correct sign. And even then you'd have two representations of zero, positive zero and negative zero, which means every check for "is this result zero?" has to look for two different bit patterns.

Attempt two: one's complement

Someone had a cleaner idea. Instead of flagging the sign, define the negative of any number as "flip every bit." So +5 is 00000101 and −5 is 11111010. Addition almost works now, but add +5 and −5 and you get 11111111, which represents negative zero. Still two zeros. Still broken, just differently. One's complement was actually used in some real machines in the 1960s, but the double-zero problem never went away cleanly.

The idea that won: two's complement

The right way to think about this is not with signs at all. Think about a number line that wraps around, exactly like a clock face.

A clock has 12 positions. If you want to go back one hour from 1 o'clock, you land on 12. The clock doesn't have a negative position; it just wraps. The number 12 and "one hour before 1" are the same position on the clock face. That's not a bug in the clock. It's just modular arithmetic.

Binary numbers behave identically. With 8 bits you have 256 positions, 0 through 255. Add 1 to 255 and you get 256, which is nine bits. If you only keep eight bits, you wrap back to 0. The number line is circular. Now ask: what number, when added to 5, gives you 0 on this circular number line? You need X such that 5 + X = 256, which wraps to 0. So X = 251. In binary, 251 is 11111011. That should be our representation of −5.

Two's complement as a clock (4-bit)

A 4-bit two's complement system has 16 positions arranged in a circle. Positive numbers live on the right half (0 to 7). Negative numbers live on the left half (−8 to −1). They share the same bit patterns and only your agreement about interpretation changes which half you're reading. Notice that adding 1 always moves one step clockwise, and going past 7 wraps into negative territory.

Now verify with actual addition. Add 5 and −5, which is 00000101 + 11111011:

Type any two numbers between −128 and 127. The same adder, the same hardware logic, handles all combinations of positive and negative numbers without any special cases. The sign takes care of itself through the wrap-around.

The carry bit falls off the end and the result is zero. Correct. No sign detection. No special subtraction path. The same adder circuit handles positive plus positive, positive plus negative, and negative plus negative identically. That is why two's complement won and why every CPU made in the last fifty years uses it.

The mechanical recipe for finding the two's complement of any number: flip every bit, then add 1. For −5, start with 00000101, flip to 11111010, add 1, get 11111011. That's 251 as an unsigned number. That's −5 as a signed number. The bits are the same. The agreement about what they mean is the only difference.

Fractions and the problem integers cannot solve

Everything so far handles whole numbers. But the moment your program needs to represent 3.14, or the gravitational constant 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹, or just the result of dividing 1 by 3, integers fall apart completely. You need a different approach.

Fixed point: simple but constrained

The naïve fix is to agree on where the decimal point sits. Say you use 8 bits and decide the point always lives after the fourth bit. Then 01000100 means 0100.0100, which is 4.25. This is called fixed-point representation and it still exists. Embedded systems use it constantly because it's fast and predictable when you know your numbers will always stay within a narrow range.

The problem is that your range and your precision are in permanent conflict. Shift bits to the whole-number side and you get a wider range but coarser fractions. Shift them the other way and you get fine precision but tiny maximum values. You have to choose once, at design time, and if your signal ever goes outside that range, you get overflow. If you need both 0.0000001 and 1,000,000 in the same variable, fixed point cannot help you.

Floating point: scientific notation for silicon

The solution mirrors something from school science: scientific notation. Instead of writing 0.0000000000667, physicists write 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹. The key insight is separating what the significant digits are from where the decimal point sits. You can represent very large and very small numbers with the same handful of digits just by sliding the exponent.

Floating point does exactly this, in binary. A 32-bit float is split into three parts: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, and 23 mantissa bits. The value is reconstructed as sign × 1.mantissa × 2^(exponent − 127). The "1." before the mantissa is always assumed to be there. Since a normalised binary number always starts with 1, you get that bit for free without storing it, squeezing out an extra bit of precision from nothing. Engineers call it the hidden bit.

Try 0.1, then 0.2, then 0.3. Notice that 0.1 and 0.2 are stored as slightly imprecise approximations because their binary representations never terminate, so they get cut off. The stored value of 0.1 is not exactly 0.1.

Why 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3

This is the thing most programmers learn the hard way, usually at the worst possible moment. Open any programming language and compute 0.1 + 0.2. You will very likely get 0.30000000000000004, not 0.3. It looks like a bug. It is not a bug. It is a direct consequence of how floating point representation works.

The decimal fraction 0.1, when converted to binary, is 0.000110011001100110011... repeating forever, exactly the way 1/3 repeats forever in decimal. A 32-bit float has to cut this off after 23 mantissa bits. What gets stored is not 0.1 but the closest approximation that fits, which is something like 0.10000000149011612. The same thing happens to 0.2. Add two approximations and the tiny errors add up. The result is not 0.3 but a number very close to it, and "very close" is not the same as equal.

Try 0.1 + 0.2, then 0.5 + 0.25, then 1.0 + 2.0. Numbers that are exact powers of two (0.5, 0.25, 0.125) are represented perfectly and add without error. Numbers that require infinite binary digits (0.1, 0.2, 0.3) always carry a tiny approximation error.

This is not a flaw you can patch. It is a fundamental consequence of fitting infinite precision into finite bits. For most purposes the error is small enough to ignore. For financial software where you are counting money and rounding errors compound into real problems, the standard advice is to avoid floating point entirely and work in integers, storing amounts in pence or cents rather than pounds or dollars.

64-bit doubles use 11 exponent bits and 52 mantissa bits, giving roughly 15 to 16 significant decimal digits of precision instead of 7. The problem does not go away. It just gets much smaller.

The hardware knows nothing

Step back and look at what we have built. A physical fact, that transistors are reliably either on or off, gave us bits. Chaining bits gave us integers. A modular arithmetic trick gave us negative integers. Separating significant digits from scale gave us floating point. A lookup table gave us text.

And here is the thing worth really sitting with. None of these representations are built into the hardware. The CPU does not know what any bit pattern means. Whether those 32 bits represent the integer 1,078,523,331, the floating point number approximately equal to 3.14, or the text characters "B", "u", "n", "d", that is decided entirely by the program reading them, and only by the program.

When you declare a variable as int in your code, the compiler generates instructions that read those bits as a two's complement integer. Declare the same memory location as float and the compiler generates different instructions that read the same bits as a floating point number. The bits in RAM do not change. The instruction the CPU receives changes, and with it, the meaning of everything.

The computer does not speak. It counts. Every layer of meaning, whether that is a letter, a colour, or a sound wave, is a human agreement layered on top of counting. Strip those agreements away and what is left is numbers, all the way down.
