The First Mechanism: Letting the Past Shape the Present

At the end of the previous blog, we reached a place that was intentionally uncomfortable. We understood why time breaks static models, why treating sequences as lists fails, and why memory cannot simply be a place where the past is stored. What we did not yet have was a concrete way forward. We knew what a sequence demanded, but not how a system could actually meet that demand. This blog is about the first serious attempt to do exactly that.

The problem we are trying to solve is deceptively simple to state. We want a system that sees inputs one at a time, and yet does not start from scratch each time. We want the past to matter, but not as a frozen record. We want meaning to accumulate, to evolve, to gently shift as new information arrives, without collapsing into rigidity or evaporating into forgetfulness. In short, we want belief to persist and change at the same time.

How can a system let the past influence the present without losing control?

The first idea the field explored did not involve clever tricks or complex machinery. It came from a very direct observation. At every moment, a system already produces something internal — a partial interpretation, a hidden belief about what it has seen so far. So instead of storing the past somewhere else and retrieving it later, what if that internal belief itself could be reused? What if yesterday’s understanding could quietly flow into today’s computation?

This was the first true shift away from storage and toward process. Memory was no longer imagined as an archive sitting behind the system. It became something alive, something that moved forward with time. Each new input arrived not alone, but accompanied by the residue of everything that had come before. For the first time, the present was interpreted through the lens of the past.

An internal belief state flowing forward in time, continuously shaping how each new input is interpreted placeholder

This single idea was enough to unlock sequences. The same input could now mean different things depending on history. Order stopped being an artificial constraint and became a natural consequence. Meaning could finally unfold rather than reset. But this solution, elegant as it was, carried a hidden cost. The moment the past was allowed to flow forward, it also gained power. And power, when left unchecked, creates instability.

This blog is about understanding that first mechanism — and why it was not enough.

Before we talk about fixing it, refining it, or replacing it, we need to understand exactly what this mechanism gets right, and exactly where it begins to fail. Because every sequence model that followed is, in one way or another, a response to the problems introduced right here.

Belief as a Snapshot of the Past

To make this mechanism precise, we need an example where the word belief has an unambiguous mathematical meaning. The simplest such example is the mean.

Not the classroom mean, where all values are known in advance, but the running mean — the estimate of an average when data arrives one observation at a time.

Consider a temperature sensor again, but now be very clear about the task. We are not interested in individual readings. We are interested in the system’s current estimate of the average temperature of the machine.

That estimate is the belief.

Belief is not the latest reading — it is the current estimate of the mean.

Let us formalize this. At time step t, the system holds a belief mt. This value means:

“Based on everything I have seen so far, this is my current estimate of the mean temperature.”

This single number is the system’s entire memory. The past does not exist anywhere else. It survives only through this estimate.

Now a new sensor reading arrives, which we denote by xt. The system must update its estimate of the mean.

The most natural update rule is:

\[ m_{t+1} = m_t + \alpha (x_t - m_t) \]

This equation is not a trick. It is exactly what it means to update an estimate of a mean incrementally. The term \((x_t - m_t)\) measures how far the new observation is from the current estimate. The parameter \(\alpha\) controls how fast the estimate adapts.

The belief moves toward the new data, but never forgets the past.

Now let us walk through this numerically. Suppose the system has processed data for a while and currently believes the mean temperature is 50. That is, mt = 50.

A new reading arrives: 52. The system does not conclude the mean is now 52. It adjusts cautiously. With \(\alpha = 0.1\), the new belief becomes:

\[ m_{t+1} = 50 + 0.1(52 - 50) = 50.2 \]

Another reading arrives: 53. The estimate updates again:

\[ m_{t+2} = 50.2 + 0.1(53 - 50.2) \approx 50.48 \]

At no point does the system store the values 52 or 53. And yet, the belief is clearly responding to them. Older observations still influence the estimate, but their influence fades as more data arrives.

A running mean estimate adjusting gradually as new temperature readings arrive over time placeholder

This is the key insight. The running mean is a snapshot of the past. Not a snapshot of raw data, but a snapshot of what the past collectively implies.

If you were to unroll the update rule, you would find that mt is a weighted combination of all previous readings, with recent readings weighted more heavily and older readings weighted less.

Nothing was deleted. Nothing was retrieved. Influence simply decayed as belief was repeatedly updated.

Memory is not stored — it is folded into the current belief.

Now compare this with a static system. A static model sees only the current reading. To it, 52 and 53 arrive without context. It cannot distinguish a trend from noise.

But the belief-based system never sees a reading in isolation. Every new value is interpreted relative to the current estimate of the mean. The same reading can be surprising or unsurprising depending on what the system already believes.

The same sensor reading appearing normal or anomalous depending on the current running mean belief placeholder

This is the first true sequence mechanism. A single state variable that: persists over time, summarizes the past, and is revised incrementally as new data arrives.

But the limitation is now visible. All of history has been compressed into one number. That compression is severe. It destroys structure.

The mechanism knows how to remember — but not what to remember.

That limitation is exactly what drives the next part of this story.

From Mean to Belief: What We Have Really Built

Now that the example is concrete, we can step back and name what has actually happened. The running mean was never the point. It was only the simplest place where the underlying idea could not hide.

What we have really built is a system that carries forward a representation of the past. Not the past itself. Not a list of events. But a compressed summary of what the past implies.

That summary is what we have been calling belief.

Belief is a representation of history, not a record of history.

In the mean example, belief had a very specific meaning: the current estimate of an average. Because the task was simple, the representation was simple. One number was enough.

But the structural idea is much broader. At each moment, the system maintains some internal state. That state is meant to answer a single question:

“Given everything I have seen so far, what do I currently believe?”

Crucially, this belief is the only thing that survives across time. The raw data does not. The individual events do not. They are folded into the state and then disappear.

This is the decisive shift away from storage. We are no longer keeping the past around in case we might need it. We are continuously rewriting the past into a form that can be used immediately.

Past inputs being progressively compressed into a compact belief state as time moves forward placeholder

This direction is not a heuristic. It is a necessity. Any system that operates in real time, under finite memory and finite computation, must do something like this.

If it tries to store everything, it drowns. If it throws everything away, it becomes amnesic. The only viable option is compression.

Sequence modeling begins when the past is forced to live inside the present.

Seen this way, the running mean is just one point on a much larger spectrum. Instead of a single scalar belief, we could imagine a vector of beliefs. Instead of estimating one quantity, we could estimate many interacting properties of the world.

But the logic remains the same. At every step, the system carries forward a state. That state is revised when new evidence arrives. And that state is the only bridge between past and present.

This is why the direction we are taking is the right one. We are no longer asking how to store sequences. We are asking how to represent them.

At the same time, the limitation is now unavoidable. Compression is lossy.

The running mean destroyed information about variability. It destroyed information about order. It destroyed information about rare but important events.

That loss was acceptable only because the task was simple. As soon as tasks become richer, the question is no longer whether belief should exist, but what structure belief should have.

The problem has shifted from remembering the past to choosing how the past should be represented.

This is where the story must go next. If belief is going to carry the past forward, then its structure matters. Its dimensionality matters. Its dynamics matter.

The next attempts in the history of sequence models were all answers to the same question:

What kind of internal state is rich enough to represent the past, yet stable enough to evolve over time?

Now that we understand what belief really is, we are finally ready to see why building it correctly is so hard.

What Is Lost When the Past Is Compressed

Once we accept belief as a compressed snapshot of the past, we are forced to confront a harder and slightly uncomfortable question. What exactly disappears during that compression? The running mean example made belief feel stable, reasonable, even elegant. It suggested that the past could be folded into a single number and still remain useful. But that calm hides a deep and unavoidable cost. Compression always destroys information. It has to. The only real question is which information gets destroyed.

A single state can summarize the past — but it cannot preserve its shape.

To see this clearly, consider two different histories. In the first, a sensor produces the values 10, 10, 10, 10. In the second, it produces 0, 20, 0, 20. If belief is the running mean, both histories collapse to exactly the same belief. The same number. The same snapshot. And yet, these two histories are fundamentally different. One is stable and calm. The other is wildly oscillatory. One suggests a steady system. The other suggests instability. That distinction has vanished completely. There is no way to recover it. Once the histories have been compressed into a single belief, that information is gone forever.

Two very different sequences collapsing into the same belief value, with temporal structure erased placeholder

This is the first kind of loss: variation. The belief knows the average, but it does not know how that average came to be. It cannot tell whether the past was smooth or chaotic, whether values hovered gently or swung violently. All traces of fluctuation have been flattened away. Now consider a different pair of histories. In one case, the sequence is 1, 2, 3, 4. In the other, it is 4, 3, 2, 1. Again, the belief converges to the same value. But these histories are not interchangeable. Growth and decline are not the same phenomenon. Direction matters. Trend is meaning. And yet, once the past is compressed into a single scalar belief, order disappears. The belief does not remember what came first. It does not know whether things are improving or deteriorating. That information has been erased by design.

Order is not decoration — it is information.

Now consider something more subtle. Imagine two histories where the mean is identical, the variation is similar, and even the overall trend looks comparable. But in one of those histories, a rare but important event occurred — a spike, a failure, a warning signal. If belief is a simple compressed state, that event is absorbed and diluted. Its identity is lost. The belief cannot know that something exceptional happened. It only knows that the average nudged slightly. The past has been smoothed so aggressively that anomalies dissolve into noise.

A rare but significant event being absorbed into a belief update, losing its identity in the compression placeholder

This is not a flaw in implementation. It is a consequence of representation. When many events are forced to share a single container, they must compete. Most of them lose. And there is a deeper loss still. Causality. When belief is a single state, the system cannot know which parts of the past caused which outcomes. Everything is mixed together. Everything is blended. The past becomes influence without explanation, effect without story. History turns into a smear rather than a sequence of causes and consequences.

Compression turns history into influence, not explanation.

At this point, it should be clear that belief is doing exactly what it was designed to do. It enables continuity. It allows real-time operation. It makes sequence processing possible under finite memory and computation. But it also discards structure, and the discarded structure is often precisely what complex tasks depend on. Sequence problems are rarely about averages. They are about transitions, timing, persistence, and selective importance. A single belief state cannot express all of that, no matter how carefully it is updated or how well it is tuned.

Once the past has been compressed, some distinctions are gone forever. And this is the tension that drives the rest of the story. Belief must exist. Compression is unavoidable. And yet, compression destroys meaning. Every sequence model that follows — from richer state representations, to gated updates, to attention mechanisms — is an attempt to answer the same question: how can a system preserve the parts of the past that matter, without storing the past itself?

Why One Belief Is Not Enough

Once we understand what compression destroys, the next conclusion follows almost unwillingly. The problem is not that belief exists. The problem is that belief is too small. A single state, no matter how carefully updated, is being asked to do too much work. It is expected to summarize variation, preserve order, retain rare events, encode trends, and support future interpretation — all at once. That expectation is unreasonable. The failure is not in the update rule. It is in the dimensionality of the belief itself.

A single number can summarize a past, but it cannot describe one.

The running mean failed not because it was wrong, but because it was narrow. It could answer one question extremely well: “What is the average of what I have seen so far?” But the moment the task asked a different question — “Is the system stable or volatile?”, “Is something changing?”, “Did something unusual just happen?” — the belief had nothing to say. That information was never stored, because there was nowhere to store it. Compression had already erased it.

This reveals a deeper truth. Belief is not a scalar concept. In real sequence problems, belief is structured. At the very least, a system may need to believe multiple things at the same time. It may need to believe what the typical value is, how much it fluctuates, whether it is trending upward or downward, whether recent events should be trusted more than older ones, and whether something exceptional has occurred that should not be averaged away. Trying to encode all of this into a single number is like trying to describe a scene using only brightness.

Multiple aspects of the past (average, variation, trend, anomalies) all competing to be represented inside a single belief state placeholder

This is the moment where belief quietly turns into representation. Instead of asking for one number that summarizes everything, we begin to ask for a collection of numbers, each capturing a different aspect of the past. Not because we enjoy complexity, but because the task demands it. The past is not one-dimensional, and no amount of clever updating can make it so.

But this move introduces a new tension. As soon as belief becomes a vector rather than a scalar, new questions appear. How do these components interact? Which parts of belief should change quickly, and which should remain stable? How do we prevent some dimensions from dominating while others fade into irrelevance? The moment belief gains structure, the problem shifts from remembering to coordinating.

Richer belief preserves more history — but it also becomes harder to control.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Even in simple settings, different aspects of the past operate on different timescales. The mean may change slowly. Variability may change quickly. Rare events may matter intensely for a short period and then fade. A single update rule applied uniformly across all dimensions cannot respect these differences. What helps one part of belief adapt may destabilize another.

And so, increasing the size of belief does not automatically solve the problem of sequence. It merely exposes a deeper one. Once belief is multidimensional, we must decide how information flows within it, how long it persists, and how strongly it influences future interpretation. Without answers to these questions, richer belief collapses under its own complexity.

A higher-dimensional belief state evolving over time, with different components changing at different rates placeholder

This is the point where the story of sequence models truly begins. The problem is no longer whether belief should exist, or even what belief should represent. The problem is how belief should evolve. How much of it should change when new input arrives, which parts should be protected, and which parts should be allowed to forget.

Once belief becomes structured, stability becomes the central challenge.

Everything that follows in the history of sequence modeling is a response to this realization. Feedback alone is not enough. Rich state alone is not enough. The system needs a way to regulate influence — to decide, moment by moment, what should persist, what should adapt, and what should be ignored.

That need for regulation is what drives the next major idea in this story. Because the moment belief becomes more than a number, uncontrolled evolution stops being merely inefficient and starts becoming catastrophic.

This is where we must go next.

Making Belief Richer: A Numerical Construction

Up to this point, belief has been discussed in words. To really understand what a richer belief means, it helps to build one explicitly and watch it evolve. So let us return once more to the simplest possible setting: a stream of numbers arriving one at a time. This time, however, we will be precise about what belief contains and how it changes.

Suppose a sensor produces the sequence

10, 12, 11, 13, 12, …

If belief were only the running mean, then at time \(t\) belief would be a single number \(\mu_t\). After the first few samples, \(\mu_t\) might settle near 11.5. This belief answers exactly one question: what is the typical value I have seen so far? But it answers nothing else. So we enlarge belief deliberately.

Let belief now be a vector with two components:

\[ \mathbf{b}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_t \\ \sigma_t \end{bmatrix} \]

Here \(\mu_t\) is the running mean, and \(\sigma_t\) is a running measure of spread. Both are updated incrementally as new data arrives. After observing 10, 12, 11, 13, 12, the belief might look something like

\[ \mathbf{b}_t \approx \begin{bmatrix} 11.6 \\ 1.1 \end{bmatrix} \]

This belief already feels more human. It does not just say “the value is about 11.6.” It also says “and it usually varies by about one unit.” Two different histories that previously collapsed into the same belief are now separated. Stability and volatility are no longer confused.

Belief is no longer a number — it is a description.

Encouraged by this, let us add one more component. Many real systems care not just about level and variability, but about direction. Is the signal drifting upward or downward? So we extend belief again:

\[ \mathbf{b}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_t \\ \sigma_t \\ \tau_t \end{bmatrix} \]

Here \(\tau_t\) is a simple estimate of recent trend. If recent values tend to increase, \(\tau_t\) is positive. If they decrease, \(\tau_t\) is negative. Now belief answers three questions simultaneously: what is typical, how much does it fluctuate, and where is it heading?

For a gently rising signal, belief might evolve as

\[ \mathbf{b}_t \approx \begin{bmatrix} 11.6 \\ 1.1 \\ +0.3 \end{bmatrix} \]

At this point, belief feels genuinely expressive. It has shape. It preserves distinctions that a scalar belief destroyed. Two histories with the same mean but different dynamics now live in different regions of belief space. This is exactly what we wanted.

Now introduce a new observation: a sudden outlier, say the value 25.

Numerically, this single value barely deserves to rewrite the entire past. Intuitively, the mean should move a little, variability should jump noticeably, and the trend should be treated cautiously. But the belief vector has no such intuition. All three components must be updated using the same incoming value.

After processing 25, belief might become

\[ \mathbf{b}_{t+1} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 13.8 \\ 5.4 \\ +2.1 \end{bmatrix} \]

Notice what has happened. The mean has been pulled upward more than feels reasonable. The variability has exploded, which is expected. But the trend has also surged, falsely suggesting a strong upward drift. A single event has rewritten multiple aspects of belief, even though it should have influenced them differently.

The belief vector knows more — but it cannot decide what to trust.

This is the failure mode, and it appears only once belief becomes rich enough to matter. Each component of belief evolves on a different timescale. The mean should resist sudden change. Variability should react immediately. Trend should update conditionally, not blindly. Yet the belief update treats all components uniformly. The same evidence pushes everything.

We can try to fix this by tuning update rates. Slow down the mean. Speed up variability. Dampen trend. But this merely postpones the problem. No fixed set of update rules can handle every situation. Sometimes an outlier should be ignored. Sometimes it should trigger a reinterpretation of the entire past. The belief vector has no way to make that decision.

Crucially, nothing is wrong with the contents of belief. The vector is sensible. The quantities are meaningful. The failure comes from evolution, not representation. Belief has become expressive enough to expose its own weakness.

Once belief is multidimensional, updating it blindly becomes destructive.

This is the key lesson. Moving from scalar belief to vector belief is necessary, but not sufficient. Richer belief preserves more of the past, but it also introduces competing timescales, conflicting influences, and internal instability. The problem is no longer what belief contains, but how belief is allowed to change.

And that realization pushes the story forward. If belief is a vector, then not all of it should update all the time. Some components must be protected. Some must adapt quickly. Some must be overwritten only when the evidence is strong enough.

When Belief Becomes Unstable

Once belief becomes a vector, a new problem quietly takes center stage. The issue is no longer representation. We know how to construct meaningful belief vectors. We know how to encode averages, variability, trends, and other aspects of the past. The problem is no longer what belief is, but how belief behaves when it is repeatedly updated. This is where naive recurrence begins to fail, not occasionally, but fundamentally.

Recall the belief vector we constructed:

\[ \mathbf{b}_t = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_t \\ \sigma_t \\ \tau_t \end{bmatrix} \]

At every time step, belief evolves by combining the previous belief with the new observation \(x_t\). Abstractly, this looks harmless:

\[ \mathbf{b}_{t+1} = f(\mathbf{b}_t, x_t) \]

But this equation hides a dangerous assumption. It assumes that influence accumulates in a controlled way. It assumes that repeatedly feeding belief back into itself will not distort it. In practice, neither assumption holds.

Feedback is not neutral — it amplifies or erases.

To see this numerically, imagine a belief update rule that slightly reinforces existing belief. Each new observation nudges belief in the same direction it is already leaning. This feels reasonable. Past belief should influence present interpretation. But now consider what happens over many steps. Small biases accumulate. Components of belief begin to dominate. The mean drifts. The trend grows exaggerated. The system becomes overconfident.

This is one failure mode: domination. Certain components of belief grow larger and larger, not because the data demands it, but because feedback keeps reinforcing them. Once this happens, new evidence struggles to correct the belief. The past overwhelms the present.

Belief components growing uncontrollably due to repeated feedback, overpowering new input placeholder

Now consider the opposite extreme. Suppose we weaken feedback to prevent runaway growth. Belief now updates conservatively. New observations influence belief strongly, while old belief fades quickly. This avoids domination, but creates a different failure. Past information disappears before it can be used. Long-term structure is lost. The system becomes reactive rather than contextual.

This is the second failure mode: forgetting. The belief becomes myopic. It sees only the recent past. Patterns that require accumulation over time never form. The system behaves as if it has no memory at all.

Too much feedback causes domination. Too little causes amnesia.

These are not tuning problems. They are structural problems. No single choice of update strength can satisfy both stability and adaptability across all situations. Some signals demand long-term memory. Others demand rapid revision. A fixed update rule cannot distinguish between them.

This instability becomes even more severe as belief grows richer. Each component of belief feeds back into the next update. Errors propagate. Small misinterpretations compound. Over time, belief no longer reflects the data stream; it reflects its own history of updates.

Numerically, this appears as either exploding values or vanishing influence. Either belief grows without bound, or it collapses toward a trivial state. In both cases, meaning is lost. The system is no longer tracking the sequence; it is trapped by its own dynamics.

Two failure regimes of belief evolution: runaway growth versus rapid decay placeholder

At this point, the core difficulty of sequence modeling is fully exposed. Belief must persist over time, but persistence creates instability. Belief must adapt to new information, but adaptation erases context. Both are required. Neither can dominate.

Sequence modeling fails when influence is uncontrolled.

This is the moment where naive recurrence reaches its limit. Feeding belief back into itself is not enough. Rich belief without regulation does not converge toward understanding. It oscillates between rigidity and chaos.

What is missing is not memory, and not representation, but control. A way for the system to decide, at each step, how much of belief should be preserved, how much should be updated, and how much should be discarded. Without that decision, belief cannot evolve safely.

This realization marks a turning point in the history of sequence models. From here on, progress no longer comes from making belief larger or update rules cleverer. It comes from learning how to regulate influence itself.

That is the idea that comes next.
